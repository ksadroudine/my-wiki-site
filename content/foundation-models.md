## Summary

Stanford's 2021 report that coined the term "foundation model" for AI systems (BERT, GPT-3, CLIP) trained on broad data at scale and adaptable to a wide range of downstream tasks. Covers the emergent capabilities and homogenization risk this scale produces, and the report's domain-by-domain analysis of opportunity (healthcare, law, education, robotics) and risk.

## Concept

Stanford's Center for Research on Foundation Models introduced the term "foundation model" in this 2021 report to describe a paradigm shift already underway in AI: models such as BERT, DALL-E, and GPT-3, trained on broad data at scale (typically via self-supervision) and adaptable — through techniques like fine-tuning — to a wide range of downstream tasks, rather than built and trained for one narrow purpose. The term is deliberately chosen to capture a duality: these models are critically central to the modern AI applications built on top of them, yet remain incomplete on their own, since a foundation model requires further adaptation before it becomes any specific, useful application. <span class="src">On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models</span>

The report's central technical observation is that foundation models, while built from standard deep learning and transfer-learning techniques that predate them, exhibit genuinely emergent capabilities once trained at sufficient scale — capabilities not present in smaller versions of the same architecture and not straightforwardly predictable from the training process itself. GPT-3, with 175 billion parameters, illustrates how far this had already stretched expectations: it can be adapted via natural-language prompts alone to perform passably on tasks it was never explicitly trained to do. This same scale-driven effectiveness across many different tasks creates a powerful incentive toward homogenization — building many different downstream applications on top of the same underlying foundation model rather than training bespoke models for each — which the report frames as a double-edged development: homogenization provides real leverage (one well-built foundation model can power many applications efficiently) but also means that any defect, bias, or failure mode inherited by the foundation model propagates automatically into every application built on top of it. Compounding this risk, the report is explicit that despite the accelerating pace of real-world deployment, there is currently no clear understanding of how these models actually work internally, when or why they fail, or the true boundaries of what they're capable of — a genuine knowledge gap, not merely a communication problem, precisely because the most significant capabilities emerge unpredictably from scale rather than from any single traceable design choice researchers can point to. <span class="src">On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models</span>

The report organizes the capabilities that power foundation-model applications into several areas: processing different modalities (language and vision being the most mature), affecting the physical world through robotics, performing reasoning, and interacting with humans — treating each as requiring its own distinct technical innovation, since raw model capability doesn't automatically translate into usefulness for any specific application domain. Language and vision have led the way, in the report's account, precisely because they benefit from abundant raw training data and cheap, virtual application environments in which to test and deploy models; robotics faces a structurally harder path because it remains anchored to the physical world, lacking both the abundance of easily gathered data and the low-cost virtual testbeds available to language and vision — the report points to generic, environment-agnostic video footage of humans performing tasks as one promising, non-domain-specific data source that could help close this gap for robotics foundation models specifically. Applied across specific domains, the report finds foundation models particularly well-suited to healthcare and biomedical research, where expert knowledge is scarce and costly, since abundant multimodal data already exists (images, text, molecular structures) and the sample efficiency foundation models bring to adaptation directly reduces the amount of expensive expert time required; to legal applications, where ample data already exists in the form of legal documents and generative capability suits many of the field's core writing tasks, though the report cautions that significant further improvement is needed before models can reliably reason across multiple sources to produce genuinely truthful, coherent long-form legal documents rather than merely plausible-sounding ones; and to education, where the report sees clear potential for adaptive, personalized learning applications — given foundation models' open-ended generative capabilities (such as automatically generating practice problems) and interactive capabilities (such as giving feedback to teachers) — contingent on models improving at reasoning about student cognition and aligning generated content with actual learning goals rather than superficially plausible content. Across all of these domains, the report's overarching argument is that foundation model research, at the time of writing still largely confined to computer science and concentrated in the tech industry, has clear potential to extend AI's reach into many sectors well beyond tech — meaning the effects (both beneficial and harmful) of homogenization, emergent capability, and incomplete understanding are set to become more pervasive across people's lives, not less, as adoption spreads. <span class="src">On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models</span>
