## Summary

Four terms captured how people inside the AI industry talked about the technology in 2025, namely reasoning models, evals, synthetic data and vibes. Reasoning models work through multi-step problems by backtracking and revising, at a much higher computing cost than earlier models. Evals are batteries of automated tests that decide whether a frontier model is safe and capable enough to release. Synthetic data is training material produced by one AI model for another, which became necessary as the supply of human-written text ran out and carries the risk of passing on errors. Vibes refers to judging a chatbot by its qualitative feel instead of benchmark scores, and projects such as Chatbot Arena and VibeCheck try to measure it.

## Highlights

- "Reasoning" refers to a model's ability to work through multistep problems, backtracking and revising as needed, rather than producing an answer in one pass — OpenAI's o1 and o3 models and DeepSeek's R1 (the model that "shook up Silicon Valley" in January 2025) exemplify this category, taking a minute or longer to answer a question but showing their intermediate steps and handling more complex problems as a result.
- Reasoning models cost substantially more to operate than earlier AI models because of the additional computing power required per query, and whether that added capability translates into genuine economic value remains, in the words of AI researcher Melanie Mitchell, "still an open question."
- "Evals" (evaluations) are batteries of automated tests — such as probing whether a model can be prompted into hacking a computer network or providing bioweapon-synthesis instructions — that determine whether a frontier AI model is safe to release; the practice remains entirely voluntary, run by AI companies themselves alongside third-party labs and government bodies like the UK's AI Safety Institute, with no binding requirement that a company disclose or act on unfavorable results.
- "Synthetic data" — training data generated by one AI model (the "teacher") for another (the "student") — has become necessary because most advanced models have already consumed essentially all freely available human-generated text on the internet; DeepSeek used synthetic data from one of its own models to train another, and OpenAI separately investigated whether DeepSeek had also queried OpenAI's models to generate training data in violation of OpenAI's terms of service.
- Synthetic data carries a specific risk: because large language models can hallucinate, an inaccurate "teacher" model produces training data that makes the "student" model dumber rather than smarter, requiring careful human monitoring of the outputs used for training.
- "Vibes" (or a "vibe check") refers to judging an AI chatbot's output by qualitative feel — whether responses are concise or overwrought, pandering or confrontational — rather than by quantitative benchmark scores; OpenAI's president publicly endorsed vibe checks as "great evals" in their own right, not merely an informal supplement to formal testing.
- UC Berkeley's Chatbot Arena crowdsources vibe checks at scale: users see anonymous, randomly paired responses from different AI models to the same prompt and vote on which they prefer, and the resulting rankings (from more than two million votes since the project's 2023 launch) are closely watched across the AI industry as an informal but influential measure of model quality.
- A research project called VibeCheck attempts to formalize this qualitative judgment into measurable dimensions — ranking chatbots along continuums like serious-versus-humorous and formal-versus-informal — on the argument that these qualitative properties have a measurable, real impact on how people judge AI models even though they resist reduction to a single correctness metric.

## Concept

<span class="src">AI Buzzwords You Need to Know Now</span> (WSJ, February 2025) defines four terms that captured specific, concrete shifts in how frontier AI models were being built, tested, and judged as of early 2025, moving beyond the more foundational AI/ML/deep-learning vocabulary that had already stabilized by that point. "Reasoning" describes models capable of working through multistep problems with backtracking and revision — the article's example is coordinating an all-hands meeting by cross-referencing calendars and room availability, a task humans perform with what feels like effortless common sense but that requires genuine multistep planning. OpenAI's o1 and o3 models and DeepSeek's R1 exemplify this category: they take noticeably longer to respond than earlier models, in exchange for handling more complex problems and exposing their intermediate reasoning steps, at meaningfully higher computational cost per query. Whether this translates into commensurate economic value remains genuinely contested — the article quotes AI researcher Melanie Mitchell's explicit skepticism that reasoning capability will necessarily prove economically valuable at the cost required to run it.

"Evals" (evaluations) are the AI industry's term for the automated safety testing that determines whether a frontier model is fit to release, probing for dangerous capabilities such as facilitating computer intrusion or providing instructions for synthesizing weapons. The article is careful to note the practice's current limitation: evals remain entirely voluntary, conducted by the AI companies themselves (increasingly alongside third-party labs and government bodies like the UK's AI Safety Institute, quoted describing evals as "early tripwires ahead of more advanced capabilities"), with no binding requirement that a company disclose unfavorable results or forgo releasing a model that fails them — a structural incentive problem the article flags explicitly. "Synthetic data" addresses a scaling constraint: since most advanced models have already consumed essentially all freely available human-generated text online, researchers increasingly train new models on data generated by an existing "teacher" model for a "student" model to learn from, a technique DeepSeek used in developing its own models and that OpenAI separately investigated DeepSeek for potentially having used against OpenAI's own models in violation of its terms of service. The technique carries a specific risk given LLMs' propensity to hallucinate: an inaccurate teacher model produces training data that makes its student model measurably worse, not better, requiring careful human oversight of synthetic training data.

Finally, "vibes" captures a notable shift in how AI insiders themselves judge chatbot quality: increasingly by qualitative feel (is a response concise or overwrought, pandering or confrontational) rather than exclusively by quantitative academic benchmarks. The article treats this as a legitimate methodological development rather than a lapse in rigor, quoting OpenAI president Greg Brockman's public endorsement that "vibe checks are great evals" in their own right. UC Berkeley's Chatbot Arena operationalizes this at scale — presenting users with anonymous, randomly paired model responses to vote between, accumulating more than two million votes since its 2023 launch into industry-watched rankings — while a research project called VibeCheck attempts to formalize the underlying qualitative judgment into measurable dimensions (serious versus humorous, formal versus informal), arguing that these "soft and fuzzy" properties have a demonstrable, measurable impact on how people actually judge AI models even though they resist reduction to a single correctness score.
