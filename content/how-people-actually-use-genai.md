## Summary

Research that mined online discussions over three years shows that the most common uses of generative AI shifted from technical assistance in 2024 to emotional and self-actualization needs in 2025, with therapy and companionship becoming the top use and organizing one's life and finding purpose entering the top ranks. Usage data from OpenAI and Anthropic broadly corroborate this, and all three sources show that usage is highly concentrated in a few categories, although they disagree on how much AI is used for coding. A separate meta-analysis of 106 experiments by MIT researchers found that human-AI combinations on average beat humans working alone but did not beat the better of human-only or AI-only performance. Combinations succeed mainly when each party is given the subtasks it does better, and content creation is one area where they produced a real gain.

## Concept

Marc Zao-Sanders' original 2024 HBR study set out to answer a question he found conspicuously under-addressed a year into the ChatGPT era: despite hundreds of millions of weekly users and multi-trillion-dollar economic projections, were people actually finding gen AI useful in concrete, specific ways, or was the discourse mostly generic feature-list phrases ("text summarization," "code review") that did little to persuade the skeptical majority — a group that, even among the world's billion knowledge workers, represented 90% of people who still weren't using ChatGPT regularly at the time. To find out, Zao-Sanders' team at Filtered Technologies mined tens of thousands of posts from Reddit and Quora specifically for concrete, first-person accounts of gen AI delivering real value, filtering out the substantial noise (inanity, repetition, abuse) endemic to anonymous online forums to surface authentic use cases, eventually cataloging more than 100 distinct categories ranked by frequency and expert assessment. These 100 categories organized into six broader themes: Content Creation & Editing (23%), Technical Assistance & Troubleshooting (21%), Personal & Professional Support (17%), Learning & Education (16%), Creativity & Recreation (13%), and Research, Analysis & Decision Making (10%) — a spread the study reads as evidence of gen AI's genuinely broad utility across both work and leisure, creative and technical domains. Notably, the single most common use case wasn't a content-generation task at all but idea generation — brainstorming — a finding the study treats as significant precisely because it positions gen AI as augmenting an existing human process (a person still has to select, refine, and act on the ideas) rather than fully automating a task end to end, a pattern the study found held true for the vast majority of the 100 identified use cases, with a human somewhere in the loop to check, approve, or apply the AI's output in nearly every case. The study also identified a striking access-democratization pattern: 13 of the 100 use cases concerned law, coding, or medicine — domains where expert human services are traditionally expensive, hard to access on demand, and often unavailable to many people at all — with users increasingly treating gen AI as filling that gap directly, drafting legal documents, reviewing code, or seeking preliminary medical guidance without engaging a paid professional, while the study is careful to note the models don't always grasp the nuanced, context-specific, or ethical considerations a genuine expert would, meaning oversimplified or outright incorrect outputs remained a real risk in these higher-stakes categories. <span class="src">How People Are Really Using GenAI</span>

Exactly one year later, Zao-Sanders and HBR's editors repeated the identical social-listening methodology at greater scale, scouring more data and adding article-sourced examples alongside the original Reddit/Quora forums, and found the resulting 2025 top-100 list had shifted dramatically: 38 of the 100 entries were entirely new compared to 2024, and — most strikingly — the single most common use case flipped from a technical, work-oriented category to an explicitly emotional one. Therapy/Companionship became the new #1 use case, joined in the top five by two other newly prominent, self-actualization-oriented categories: "Organizing my life" (helping people become more aware of and act on their own intentions, habits, and goals) and "Finding purpose" (determining personal values, working through roadblocks, and taking concrete self-development steps). At the theme level, Personal & Professional Support grew from 17% of use cases in 2024 to 31% in 2025 — becoming by far the largest single theme — pulling share mainly from Technical Assistance & Troubleshooting, which fell from 21% to just 15% over the same period. Reddit users describing AI-delivered therapy consistently cited three converging advantages: round-the-clock availability, low or no cost, and freedom from the fear of human judgment — with one South African user specifically framing this as filling a genuine structural access gap, noting their country has roughly one psychologist per 100,000 people and one psychiatrist per 300,000, making an always-available LLM a meaningful, if imperfect, substitute where professional mental healthcare is simply scarce; the study notes recent research finding AI-delivered therapeutic responses can already be indistinguishable from human-written ones in blind comparisons, even as broader debate about computerized therapy's full potential and risks continues (the phenomenon has also been separately observed emerging in China). This shift toward emotionally-weighted use cases wasn't confined to individuals experimenting privately — EY was independently training employees across its roughly 400,000-person workforce to work alongside sector-specific agentic systems, including 150 AI agents deployed specifically for tax-related tasks, illustrating that the same underlying capability shift was simultaneously reshaping formal enterprise deployment. The study closes on a genuinely contested note about gen AI's effect on human cognition itself: some Reddit users candidly described having "grown too dependent" on the tools, worried about atrophying their own capacity for complex thought, while others framed active, deliberate use of AI as essential to avoiding career stagnation — a tension the research surfaces without resolving, since both experiences appeared to be authentic in the underlying forum data. <span class="src">How People Are Really Using GenAI in 2025</span>

A third installment in the same research program, published roughly seven months later in late 2025, shifts from single-study reporting to explicit methodological synthesis, motivated by a practical concern for business leaders: companies are investing billions in AI tools while struggling to demonstrate meaningful returns, and understanding how people actually use AI — as distinct from how vendors claim they use it — is a genuine input into avoiding costly missteps and identifying real opportunities. The piece triangulates three major 2025 studies of human AI-usage behavior, each built on a fundamentally different method with its own tradeoffs: usage logs (what users actually do, as captured directly by the LLM provider itself — OpenAI's and Anthropic's own reports rely on this method) offer real behavioral data at large volume but are limited to a single platform, exclude logged-out or opted-out users, and lack rich context due to privacy constraints (OpenAI states explicitly that "no member of the research team ever saw the content of user messages"); surveys (what users say, when asked) allow researchers to probe any specific detail including demographics, but carry real self-selection bias and typically yield lower data volume; and social listening (what users say, unasked — Zao-Sanders' own method) captures unusually candid truth, since forum anonymity creates the psychological safety for people to discuss therapeutic, intimate, or controversial use cases they wouldn't disclose to a survey or reveal in privacy-sanitized corporate logs, at the cost of lower volume, source bias, and no demographic data at all. The piece is explicit that none of these three methods alone captures the full picture, and that all research — including studies published by AI companies themselves — should be read with awareness of the publisher's own vested interest, since usage reports in particular tend to emphasize growth and impressive examples while downplaying failure modes or limitations; the strongest signal, in the author's framing, comes specifically from convergence across genuinely independent methods. Where the three 2025 studies agreed, the agreement was substantial and consistent: ChatGPT had reached roughly 800 million weekly active users sending about 20 billion messages weekly; Anthropic found 40% of employees now report using AI at work, double the share from two years earlier; and all three studies independently found usage highly concentrated in a power-law distribution — OpenAI found 78% of all messages fall into just its top three categories (practical guidance, writing, and information-seeking), Anthropic found 20% of task categories account for 87% of usage, and Zao-Sanders' own top-20 use-case buckets (out of 100+ identified) accounted for more than half of total observed use — a convergent finding the piece reads as a clear signal that organizations should concentrate AI investment on a small number of genuinely high-impact use cases rather than attempting comprehensive coverage from the outset. Writing similarly dominated across all three studies (OpenAI found it accounts for about 40% of work-related messages; at least 20 of Zao-Sanders' top-100 use cases are squarely writing-related), and education/learning use grew consistently across the same period (Anthropic's education-related usage grew from 9.3% to 12.4%; roughly 10% of all ChatGPT messages are tutoring or teaching requests; 20% of Zao-Sanders' catalogued use cases fall in the learning/education bucket) — as did automation-oriented, "doing rather than asking" usage (Anthropic found directive, automation-style conversations rose from 27% to 39% of usage within eight months, reaching 77% of enterprise API transcripts specifically; OpenAI found 56% of work tasks are "doing" rather than "expressing or asking"). Where the three studies genuinely diverged, the piece treats the divergence itself as informative rather than as noise to be smoothed over: Anthropic reported 36% of its sample using gen AI for coding, versus just 8% in Zao-Sanders' own research and 4.2% in OpenAI's data — a gap the piece attributes partly to Anthropic's broader category definition (which folds in math) and partly to Anthropic's distinct early reputation specifically as a coding-focused model provider, while explicitly flagging that "there's some further explanatory work to do" rather than treating the discrepancy as fully resolved. A second, smaller apparent disagreement — OpenAI finding only 1.9% of messages fall into "relationships and personal reflection" versus Zao-Sanders' own finding that therapy/companionship was the #1 single use case in his 2025 report — turns out to be less dramatic than it first appears once the underlying percentages are compared directly: therapy/companionship, despite ranking #1 among 100-plus categories in Zao-Sanders' data, still only accounted for roughly 4% of his total observed use, meaning the "disagreement" largely reflects differing methodology and categorization rather than a genuine substantive conflict about how much therapeutic AI use is actually occurring. The piece closes with seven concrete practices for business leaders reading AI research critically: deliberately following AI optimists, skeptics, and neutrals to avoid a filter-bubble view of the technology; mapping any given study's findings against one's own direct organizational experience rather than accepting them at face value; using AI itself to summarize and synthesize AI research (a genuinely meta but practical technique); periodically reading one full scientific paper closely rather than only registering headline-level takeaways, since headlines are optimized to surprise rather than to convey essential truth; tracking an organization's own internal usage metrics rather than relying purely on external studies, since external research can suggest what and how to measure but a specific organization's actual context is what ultimately matters for decision-making; treating cross-study disagreement (like the coding-usage discrepancy documented here) as a signal worth investigating rather than dismissing, since it may reveal either an unresolved measurement problem or a genuine untapped business opportunity; and, finally, simply experimenting with social listening as a research method directly, since — unlike usage-log access — it remains available to essentially anyone willing to do the reading. <span class="src">Making Sense of Research on How People Use AI</span>

Running alongside this three-part usage-pattern research program, a distinct and more rigorously experimental line of research from MIT's Center for Collective Intelligence addresses a related but methodologically separate question: not what people are using gen AI for, but whether combining human and AI effort actually improves task performance compared to either working alone — and its central finding runs directly counter to a common industry assumption. Reviewing 370 unique effect sizes drawn from 106 separate experiments published between January 2020 and July 2023, comparing human-only, AI-only, and combined human-AI performance directly, the MIT researchers found that human-AI combinations on average did outperform the baseline of humans working entirely alone — but did not, on average, outperform AI working entirely alone, and scored below whichever single approach (human-only or AI-only) happened to be the strongest performer in a given study; the study's lead author called this "our most surprising finding," directly contradicting an assumption that many people would hold going in. The mechanism the researchers propose is a calibration problem: in tasks where AI substantially outperforms humans (their example is detecting fake hotel reviews — AI alone achieved 73% accuracy, versus 69% for the human-AI combination and just 55% for humans alone), the humans involved tend to also be poor judges of exactly when to trust the AI's output versus overriding it with their own weaker judgment, which drags the combined performance below what the AI alone would have achieved unassisted. The inverse pattern held in tasks where humans already outperformed AI on their own — classifying bird images by species being the researchers' example, where humans alone scored 81% accuracy, AI alone scored 73%, but the human-AI combination reached 90%, exceeding both individual baselines — because in these cases, the humans involved were also comparatively skilled at correctly judging when their own judgment should override the AI's suggestion, rather than deferring to it indiscriminately. This distinction leads the researchers to separate two genuinely different forms of human-AI collaboration: "human-AI augmentation," where the combined system merely beats humans working alone (a comparatively low and, per this research, commonly achieved bar), and the much rarer "human-AI synergy," where the combination beats both humans alone and AI alone simultaneously — the outcome most organizations implicitly assume they're getting when they deploy AI collaboratively, but one this research suggests is achieved far less consistently than commonly believed. Achieving genuine synergy, per the researchers, depends on correctly diagnosing in advance which of the three configurations (human alone, AI alone, or combined) will actually perform best for a specific task — a diagnosis many organizations get wrong because they tend to systematically overestimate the effectiveness of whatever collaborative system they've already built, a bias the researchers suggest can be corrected through randomized, A/B-style experiments comparing outcomes across all three configurations directly — and then, critically, redesigning the entire underlying work process around that diagnosis rather than simply reassigning individual subtasks between human and AI within an unchanged process; the researchers' guiding heuristic is that humans tend to excel specifically at subtasks requiring contextual understanding and emotional intelligence, while AI systems excel at subtasks that are repetitive, high-volume, or heavily data-driven, and that after an initial redesign, organizations should adopt a continuous-improvement loop — start with a basic workflow, monitor its actual performance, and refine it iteratively based on outcomes and user feedback, rather than treating the initial design as final. Generative AI specifically stood out within the broader dataset as a genuinely promising exception to the discouraging headline finding: across the reviewed studies, human-AI combinations performed worse than best-alone baselines specifically on decision-making tasks (which made up the bulk of the research reviewed and tended to show negative combined effects), but performed measurably better on content-creation tasks — a category still comparatively underexplored, representing only about 10% of the papers reviewed, but showing a significantly more positive average effect size for human-AI synergy than decision-making tasks did. The researchers attribute this specifically to generative AI's fundamentally more iterative and interactive collaboration model compared to earlier, single-shot AI systems designed to complete one discrete task and stop: humans can now engage gen AI in an ongoing cycle of drafting, editing, and reworking text, images, music, or video, with the AI adapting to human feedback in real time — a dynamic, back-and-forth refinement loop that echoes, from a controlled-experiment angle, exactly the kind of "human somewhere in the loop" pattern the Zao-Sanders usage research separately observed emerging organically across the vast majority of real-world use cases people described in their own words. The researchers are careful to frame their headline finding as a call for more sophistication rather than a reason to retreat from human-AI collaboration altogether: the lesson isn't that combining humans and AI doesn't work, but that organizations need to become considerably more deliberate and evidence-based about specifically when and how to combine them, rather than assuming collaboration is automatically beneficial by default. <span class="src">When humans and AI work best together — and when each is better alone</span>

## Open Questions

- None of the four sources reconciles why usage-pattern research (Zao-Sanders' three-part series) finds gen AI succeeding across an enormously broad range of use cases including many decision-adjacent ones, while the MIT performance research specifically finds human-AI combinations underperforming on decision-making tasks — it's unclear whether real-world users are self-selecting into task types where combination genuinely helps, or whether usage volume and actual performance improvement are simply different, only loosely correlated measures.
- The shift toward therapy/companionship as gen AI's top documented use case between 2024 and 2025 raises questions about mental-health safety and regulation that none of the three usage-research pieces address in depth — the vault would benefit from a dedicated source on AI-therapy risk/regulation to pair with this concept's account of AI-therapy's documented growth.
