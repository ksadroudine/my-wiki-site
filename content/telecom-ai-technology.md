## Summary

AI-native telecom networks need a different technology foundation from the batch analytics operators have used for years, built on clean multi-vendor data, cloud-native programmable networks, GPU compute, high-speed networking and flash storage, and in-house AI expertise. On that foundation, teams of specialized agents can ingest network, customer and billing data and act on it in real time, but their autonomy creates privacy, security, compliance and critical-infrastructure risks that need human oversight, explainability and fail-safe design. Where each workload runs is a workload-by-workload decision, with training and sensitive data increasingly kept under operator control, inference running at the edge, and customer-facing agents running in public cloud with on-premises fallback.

## Concept

Telecom operators have used AI for years in predictive maintenance and customer analytics, but the ambition behind AI-native networks is qualitatively different. It requires processing vast amounts of real-time network data fast and precisely enough to enable dynamic optimization, automation and eventually autonomous operation, rather than periodic batch analytics. The central claim of the roadmap is that this cannot be achieved by layering new tools onto an unchanged architecture, because AI has to be embedded in the network's operational foundation, supported by new data, infrastructure and expertise. <span class="src">Strategic Approach for Building AI-Native Telecom Networks</span>

Two prerequisites sit ahead of any specific capability. Data handling means collecting, normalizing and cleaning data from multi-vendor environments, which is difficult in telecom because equipment and software from many suppliers use inconsistent formats and conventions, and unreliable inputs produce unreliable outputs regardless of model sophistication. Cloud-native architecture, meaning open interfaces, APIs and virtualization and softwarization technologies including Network Functions Virtualization, Software-Defined Networking and Open RAN, is the second, because automation needs programmable network functions to act on. Without that layer an AI system might diagnose a problem correctly yet have no standard way to fix it. <span class="src">Strategic Approach for Building AI-Native Telecom Networks</span>

Three infrastructure domains then need rethinking. Compute should add GPUs for AI and machine-learning workloads, with training, the compute-intensive and latency-tolerant work, placed in private clouds and inference, which must respond to live conditions, placed at the network edge. Networking should add SmartNICs that offload data processing, high-bandwidth Ethernet at 100GbE or 400GbE, and low-latency interconnects such as InfiniBand or RDMA over Converged Ethernet, because AI traffic can overwhelm conventional networks. Storage should move to high-performance flash with object-storage capabilities that operate coherently across hybrid and multicloud environments. <span class="src">Strategic Approach for Building AI-Native Telecom Networks</span>

The roadmap also names organizational prerequisites. Operators need internal data literacy and AI expertise to manage proprietary datasets, because infrastructure alone does not produce working AI-native operations, and they are advised to develop smaller proprietary models for their own network conditions rather than depend entirely on third-party general-purpose vendors, which preserves control over where data is stored, how it is processed and how models are trained. Any vendor AI solution should be tested and tuned on the operator's own production data before it is trusted, a discipline that matters because vendor performance claims often fail independent scrutiny, as discussed in [Algorithmic Auditing and AI Failures](#/concept/algorithmic-auditing-and-ai-failures). The roadmap closes with an expectation that these changes take significant time and sustained effort before delivering measurable savings, so it frames them as a multi-layer strategic transformation rather than a technology upgrade. <span class="src">Strategic Approach for Building AI-Native Telecom Networks</span>

On top of this foundation, operators are adopting agentic AI, coordinated teams of specialized agents rather than one monolithic model, to handle the volume and velocity of data that networks generate, including telemetry, billing records, customer interaction logs and service feedback. In one described architecture a data aggregation agent continuously ingests network metrics, customer data, billing records and experience signals, and a master orchestrator agent correlates insights across agents and resolves conflicts between them. A network optimization agent tunes performance, capacity and availability, a customer experience agent uses behavioral and service signals to personalize interactions and reduce churn, a fraud detection agent watches billing and usage for anomalies that indicate revenue leakage, and analytics and reporting agents produce business-facing insight, with a service optimization layer aligning network behavior, customer outcomes and business goals. The intended result is a shift from reactive, manual workflows to autonomous real-time operations, which is what the source means by telecom becoming AI-native rather than simply using AI tools. <span class="src">Agentic AI in Telecom — From Data Overload to Intelligent Action</span>

Agents are also attractive because of a talent problem. An estimated 60% of the fiber technician workforce is on track to retire, and much of the tacit knowledge that keeps networks running, such as undocumented processes and niche tool expertise, lives only in experienced employees' heads. Unlike traditional automation scripts, which need clean, pre-structured data, agents are built to reason over structured systems data and unstructured field reports, transcripts and documentation together, so they can capture and scale institutional knowledge. In one field example a multimodal agent helping a technician diagnose a storm-caused outage interprets visual data on site while querying backend systems, proposes a resolution consistent with best practice and coordinates with other operators when infrastructure is shared, reducing manual work while keeping the technician in the loop. The same flexibility has a limit, because agents are only as effective as the environment they enter, and without clean data, documented processes and governance they accelerate errors instead of fixing operations, a pattern echoed by a Gartner finding that 63% of organizations lack confidence in their AI-readiness data practices and its prediction that 60% of AI projects will be abandoned by 2026 for lack of AI-ready data. <span class="src">How AI agents are powering the next wave of telecom operations</span>

Autonomy is also the source of the architecture's main risks, because agents embedded in telecom infrastructure become custodians of sensitive subscriber and enterprise data. A compromised agent could expose personal information, usage patterns or business-critical communications. Because agents act without a human reviewing every decision, an attacker could leave a compromised agent performing its normal function while it acts maliciously, or repurpose it entirely. Agents operating across jurisdictions face differing rules such as GDPR, CCPA and telecom-specific regulation, which raises the risk of inadvertent violations, and since networks underpin emergency services, financial transactions and enterprise operations, failure or manipulation could cascade well beyond the operator. Hallucination, the generation of plausible but false output, is separately flagged as unresolved where agents rely on model output for decisions. <span class="src">The Dark Side of Agentic AI in Telecom Networks</span>

The proposed mitigations combine technical and governance controls. They include layered security through encryption, authentication and intrusion detection, regular patching, compliance checks embedded in AI operations with audit logging, human-in-the-loop mechanisms that let operators intervene on anomalies through clear manual-override paths, explainability so that decisions carry an understandable rationale, and fail-safe design with the ability to revert to baseline operation during an attack or anomaly plus redundancy and disaster recovery. The source leaves open how to balance autonomy against risk, which governance model should oversee agent behavior, whether human-in-the-loop should be the default over full autonomy, and what role international standards should play. <span class="src">The Dark Side of Agentic AI in Telecom Networks</span>

Where this technology runs is the third question. AI and GenAI adoption is accelerating faster than a single cloud placement policy can handle, because workloads differ in their latency, sovereignty and control requirements. As AI moves from a peripheral analytics layer to the core of network intelligence, operators are investing in localized, secure infrastructure to satisfy regimes such as GDPR and India's TRAI, since real-time functions like RAN optimization and fraud detection need low-latency inference close to the network edge, while model training, which is less latency-sensitive but more sensitive in data-control terms, benefits from staying under the operator's internal control. The central question for operators is whether their hybrid architectures are designed for long-term AI scalability and regulatory agility, and whether GenAI-driven autonomy can be gained without compromising network trust, performance or control. A hybrid design built only for cost or flexibility is likely to need rework as AI workloads grow. <span class="src">Why Telcos Need a Balanced Hybrid Cloud Strategy</span>

The named challenges are high capital and operating expense for scalable dual-mode infrastructure, shortages of MLOps and GenAI-stack skills, the complexity of managing a model's lifecycle when part of it runs on a distributed, resource-constrained edge, and the need to interoperate with cloud-native tooling not built for telecom constraints. Hyperscalers are responding, with AWS, Azure and Google Cloud building telco-grade services that include metro-based low-latency zones and SLA-backed performance guarantees, so some of the traditional reasons for keeping workloads on-premises, namely latency and guaranteed performance, can now be met inside public cloud offerings designed for telecom, which moves the line between on-premises and cloud. <span class="src">Why Telcos Need a Balanced Hybrid Cloud Strategy</span>

In practice the balance varies by workload category. Model training, which historically ran almost entirely in public cloud where elastic compute was easiest to obtain, is being selectively pulled back on-premises for sensitive models, driven by sovereignty and control rather than cost. Data lakes and analytics are moving toward hybrid designs that keep the most sensitive data local while still running cloud analytics on top, using federated learning to train or refine models on distributed local data without the raw data leaving its sovereign environment. Core network functions such as virtualized RAN, the User Plane Function and multi-access edge computing stay on-premises because of hard real-time requirements, but they are no longer passive, since AI inference now runs on top of them at the edge to optimize traffic routing, latency and efficiency. Customer-experience platforms, OSS/BSS, orchestration layers and development and test environments move the other way, adopting GenAI agents in public cloud for agility and autonomous workflows while keeping fallback provisions to revert specific workloads to on-premises control when a compliance or latency threshold requires it. <span class="src">How AI Is Decentralizing Telco Workloads</span>

Taken together, the sources describe telco cloud as shifting from a single hosting choice between public and private or cloud and on-premises to what one source calls an intelligent, adaptive fabric, in which each placement of training or inference, sensitive or non-sensitive data, and real-time network function or customer-facing application is a deliberate engineering decision. Cloud strategy therefore has to be AI-aware in how it places training and inference, compliance-aligned with data-sovereignty rules in each jurisdiction, and edge-ready for real-time inference, rather than optimizing for any one of the three and treating the others as afterthoughts. <span class="src">How AI Is Decentralizing Telco Workloads</span> <span class="src">Why Telcos Need a Balanced Hybrid Cloud Strategy</span>
