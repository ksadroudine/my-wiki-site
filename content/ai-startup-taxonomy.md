## Summary

A six-type framework — originators, explorers, infrastructure builders, enhancers, optimizers, and experimenters — classifies AI startups by how they create, deliver, and extract value from AI, rather than by industry or hype. Each type carries distinct capital requirements, competitive moats, and risks, giving founders, investors, and corporate buyers a sharper diagnostic than existing "makers/shapers/takers"-style frameworks.

## Highlights

- Existing AI-categorization frameworks are insightful but mostly built for large incumbents or high-level adoption strategy rather than startups specifically: McKinsey's "takers/shapers/makers" (using models as-is, tailoring off-the-shelf AI, building foundation models); BCG's four-stage maturity continuum (not doing much, experimenting, scaling, full "value engine" — only 4% of surveyed companies reach the last stage); RBC/S&P Global's "enablers vs. adopters"; and the investor "picks and shovels vs. gold miners" framing.
- AI Originators build foundation models themselves (OpenAI, Anthropic, Perplexity, Cohere, Mistral) — capital-intensive, talent-intensive, technically deepest, most likely to explicitly pursue AGI, facing extreme entry barriers but also model commoditization risk, since underlying model quality across originators is already converging even as OpenAI retains a usage lead.
- AI Explorers (Cognition Labs, SandboxAQ, Safe Superintelligence, Thinking Machines Lab) aren't commercializing today's AI at all — they're pursuing scientific or architectural breakthroughs (agentic AI, quantum AI, AGI) five to ten years out, funded by mission-driven VCs or large incumbents, often deliberately secretive, with success measured by research output and benchmarks rather than revenue.
- AI Infrastructure Builders (Scale AI for data labeling, Pinecone for vector databases, Weights & Biases for ML experiment tracking, plus chip startups like Tenstorrent, Mythic, and Groq alongside incumbents Nvidia/AMD/Broadcom/Qualcomm) are the "pick and shovel" providers whose success depends on ecosystem adoption and workflow integration depth rather than raw model quality.
- AI Enhancers apply general-purpose models to specific vertical or functional problems (Descript for video/podcast editing, Jasper for marketing content) — sometimes dismissed as "wrappers," but this is where the majority of AI startup activity concentrates (46% of Y Combinator's spring 2025 batch were "AI agent" startups) and differentiation comes from proprietary data, domain focus, and workflow design rather than technical superiority.
- AI Optimizers don't build or sell AI at all — they use it internally to run their existing business better (logistics startups optimizing delivery routes, DTC retailers personalizing marketing, fintech startups automating fraud detection), succeeding through operational excellence rather than AI breakthroughs; McKinsey's 2025 survey found 71% of organizations now regularly use generative AI, up from 65% in early 2024.
- AI Experimenters — by far the largest cohort — are using AI (ChatGPT for drafts, Midjourney for design, pilot customer-support tools) without any budget, roadmap, or integration plan, and many will remain in this category indefinitely absent leadership alignment and investment; BCG reports 74% of companies are still in this "not doing much" or pure-experimentation zone.
- The six types aren't mutually exclusive or permanent — a startup can move from experimenter to optimizer to enhancer over time — but transitions carry real risk and rarely succeed without matching investment; the leap from enhancer to originator specifically demands the kind of capital and technical depth few companies that attempt it actually have.
- For corporate customers buying AI capability, the type-matching logic is concrete: partner with infrastructure builders only if in-house AI capability already exists and needs acceleration (e.g. building an internal "AI factory"); engage enhancers for fast, function-specific wins (sales, content, HR, logistics); benchmark against optimizers in your own sector to gauge realistic ROI; and only try to emulate originators or explorers if genuinely prepared to fund AI R&D at their level, since imitating them without that commitment produces neither breakthrough capability nor commercial traction.
- For investors, the typology sharpens diligence questions existing "AI-driven innovation" claims tend to obscure: does the startup actually own the model, data, or distribution underlying its claimed defensibility; is there anything technically proprietary beneath the user interface; and is the realistic exit path a product, a platform, or a feature — since an experimenter using off-the-shelf tools internally, an enhancer with genuinely sticky vertical usage, and an optimizer generating internal operating leverage represent three very different kinds of opportunity even when all three describe themselves the same way.

## Concept

<span class="src">Six Types of AI Startups, Explained</span> (MIT SMR, February 2026) opens by arguing that existing frameworks for categorizing AI companies — McKinsey's "takers, shapers, makers" distinction between using models as-is, tailoring off-the-shelf AI, and building foundation models; BCG's four-stage AI-maturity continuum, under which only 4% of surveyed companies reach full "value engine" status; RBC Wealth Management and S&P Global's "enablers vs. adopters" split; and the investor shorthand distinguishing AI "gold miners" from "picks and shovels" suppliers — were built primarily to describe large incumbents or high-level adoption strategy, and don't capture the strategic diversity of startups and growth-stage companies specifically. In response, the authors propose a six-part typology organized not around industry, technology, or hype but around how a company creates, delivers, and extracts value from AI. AI Originators build foundation models themselves — OpenAI, Anthropic, Perplexity, Cohere, and Mistral are startup examples, alongside incumbents like Google, Meta, and Microsoft — operating at the technical frontier with the highest capital intensity, the deepest talent requirements, and (for some) explicit AGI ambitions; the category faces extreme barriers to entry but also converging, increasingly commoditized model quality, even as OpenAI retains a clear usage lead. AI Explorers (Cognition Labs, Markovate, and NinjaTech AI in agentic AI; Sygaldry, SandboxAQ, and D-Wave Quantum in quantum AI; Safe Superintelligence, Thinking Machines Lab, and Ndea in AGI) aren't trying to commercialize today's AI at all — they're pursuing scientific or architectural breakthroughs on a five-to-ten-year horizon, funded by mission-driven VCs or large incumbents, often deliberately secretive, measuring success by research output and benchmark achievement rather than revenue. <span class="src">Six Types of AI Startups, Explained</span>

AI Infrastructure Builders are the "pick and shovel" providers supplying the data, APIs, and developer frameworks that let others build and deploy AI at scale — Scale AI (data labeling), Pinecone (vector databases), and Weights & Biases (ML experiment tracking) are cited startup examples, alongside more mature platforms like Databricks and startup chip makers Tenstorrent, Mythic, and Groq (distinct from incumbent chip suppliers Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, and Qualcomm); their success depends less on any single model's quality and more on ecosystem adoption and workflow-integration depth. AI Enhancers sit at the application layer, taking general-purpose models and applying them to specific vertical or functional problems — Descript (AI-assisted video and podcast editing) and Jasper (AI-generated marketing content) are examples of companies that built no model of their own but wrapped existing models into valuable, customer-facing products; this is where the majority of AI startup activity concentrates (46% of Y Combinator's spring 2025 batch were explicitly "AI agent" startups), and differentiation here comes from proprietary data, domain focus, and workflow design rather than technical superiority, since "a thin wrapper on ChatGPT is not a product." AI Optimizers don't build or sell AI at all — they use it behind the scenes to run their existing business better, illustrated by logistics startups (7bridges, Settyl, Cogsy) optimizing delivery routes, direct-to-consumer retailers (ThirdLove, Function of Beauty) personalizing marketing, and fintech startups (Sardine, Taktile, SESAMm) automating fraud detection with LLMs; their success depends on operational excellence rather than AI breakthroughs, consistent with McKinsey's 2025 finding that 71% of organizations now regularly use generative AI, up from 65% in early 2024. AI Experimenters, by far the largest cohort, are using AI tools (ChatGPT for internal drafts, Midjourney for design exploration, pilot customer-support bots) without any budget, roadmap, or integration plan, and many will remain in this category indefinitely absent leadership alignment and real investment — BCG data cited in the article puts 74% of companies in this "not doing much" or pure-experimentation zone. The article stresses these six types aren't mutually exclusive or permanent (a startup can move from experimenter to optimizer to enhancer over time), but warns that transitions carry real risk and capital requirements — the leap from enhancer to originator specifically demands a level of capital and technical depth few companies attempting it actually possess. <span class="src">Six Types of AI Startups, Explained</span>

The article closes with distinct strategic guidance for three stakeholder groups. Founders are urged to answer "what kind of AI company are you, and how quickly can you get there" rather than the now-too-simplistic "are you an AI company," with type-specific priorities: explorers compete on talent, compute, and proprietary model quality; infrastructure builders win through developer-friction reduction and ecosystem depth; enhancers must differentiate through workflow design and proprietary data rather than interface polish; optimizers should deploy AI only where it delivers real margin improvement, not wherever it's trendy; and experimenters must avoid what Gartner calls the "pilot-to-production chasm." Investors are urged to use the typology as a diligence lens for questions existing AI-hype claims tend to obscure: does the startup actually own the model, data, or distribution underlying its claimed advantage; is there anything technically proprietary beneath the interface; and is the realistic exit path a product, a platform, or a feature — since an experimenter using off-the-shelf tools internally, an enhancer with genuinely sticky vertical traction, and an optimizer generating internal operating leverage represent three fundamentally different investment opportunities even when all three describe themselves using identical AI buzzwords. Corporate customers seeking to buy AI capability from startups are advised to match partner type to their own organization's maturity: partner with infrastructure builders only when in-house AI capability already exists and needs acceleration (for instance, building an internal "AI factory"); engage enhancers for fast, function-specific wins in sales, content, HR, or logistics; benchmark against sector-specific optimizers to gauge realistic ROI expectations; and only look to originators or explorers for inspiration, not imitation, unless genuinely prepared to fund AI R&D at their level. <span class="src">Six Types of AI Startups, Explained</span>

## Open Questions

- The article doesn't address how a startup's type should shape its own hiring strategy, beyond noting explorers need PhD-level research talent — whether enhancers or optimizers need meaningfully different organizational structures isn't explored.
- The six-type framework is presented as of February 2026 and doesn't address how quickly a given startup's type classification might need to be revisited as the underlying AI landscape (model commoditization, falling infrastructure costs) continues shifting — the article notes transitions between types happen but not how often the framework itself would need updating.
