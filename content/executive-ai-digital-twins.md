## Summary

A small but growing number of executives are creating AI replicas trained on their own writing, speeches and communication style to handle routine tasks, public appearances and employee questions. LinkedIn co-founder Reid Hoffman's twin, trained on 22 years of his material, has delivered more than 75 presentations, and companies are also using twins for internal coaching and mentoring. Early adopters report substantial time savings, but the practice raises open questions about accuracy, since twins can invent answers or misjudge tone, about whether employees will accept them and about who owns a twin's accumulated knowledge when an employee leaves. The clearest governance principle so far is that digital twins should support human decisions without replacing human accountability.

## Concept

<span class="src">Too Much Work to Do? Have Your Digital Twin Handle It</span> (WSJ, May 2026) documents an emerging executive practice: creating an AI-driven replica of oneself, trained on one's own writing, speeches, and communication patterns, to absorb routine work that would otherwise consume an executive's time directly. Reid Hoffman's "Reid AI" is the most developed example — trained on 22 years of his books, speeches, podcasts, and articles, it has delivered more than 75 addresses and presentations since 2024 (with human preparation involved), speaks 74 languages, and by Hoffman's own estimate saves him roughly half his time in the weeks it is deployed. Other examples extend the pattern to internal, one-on-one coaching contexts rather than only public-facing appearances: Greif's chief human resources officer built "BalaBot," which has interacted with nearly 3,300 employees seeking guidance since its December launch, while Quantium Health CEO Brian Hartzer's "Virtual Brian" has been used by direct reports to prepare for performance reviews, in one case helping an executive manager recognize she had underrated her own contributions in a self-assessment.

Adoption is not without friction. Advocates frame the trend in ambitious terms — a December report from the Institute for Corporate Productivity's chief strategy officer concludes digital work twins could be "the most significant productivity multiplier since the personal computer," and Hoffman predicts within a decade most companies with more than 50 employees will assign trained virtual counterparts to senior roles, with rank-and-file employee twins following within a similar timeframe. But the article documents concrete, present-day failure modes: Reid AI has hallucinated answers to simple opinion questions and occasionally misjudges tone (failing to look serious after a joke), while one executive's public-facing digital twin visibly malfunctioned mid-event, stuttering and repeating the same line in front of roughly 200 conference attendees, prompting the executive to shut it down live and acknowledge "AI isn't ready for the main stage." Workforce acceptance is also not automatic: employees at one firm initially resisted having their own emails and work documents used to train a colleague's digital twin, a concern addressed through explicit opt-out provisions for sensitive personal or work content. A senior intellectual-property counsel quoted in the piece frames the emerging governance principle as one where digital twins with any supervisory role must "support human decision-making, not replace legal review or human accountability" — reflecting the real risk that an AI double could misstate employment policy or other legally consequential information without a human catching the error.

The article closes on the practice's most unresolved dimension: ownership of the accumulated knowledge inside a digital twin once the underlying employee leaves the company. One organization studied, Upwork, had already confronted this directly and established a policy — departing employees may take their digital twin's likeness and personal expertise with them, while the company retains any proprietary business knowledge the twin accumulated while the employee was there. More broadly, the article raises the possibility that departing employees could eventually be compensated specifically for leaving a digital twin and its associated knowledge behind — treating what the twin has learned as a distinct, potentially severable company asset from the human who trained it.
