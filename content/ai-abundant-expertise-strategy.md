## Summary

How AI is collapsing the cost of accessing expertise, and what that means for corporate strategy given Coase's theory that a firm's boundaries are set by the relative cost of doing things internally versus buying them externally. Covers the "triple product" AI delivers, three strategic questions about which expertise AI will make obsolete, and three real case studies of companies capturing their own experts' judgment into AI systems.

## Concept

The article's starting frame is that a business is fundamentally a differentiated bundle of expertise — a combination of deep theoretical knowledge and practical know-how — organized to accomplish specific tasks for customers; companies typically hold broad expertise across many functional areas but build genuine competitive advantage from superior proficiency in just a handful of activities, as with Toyota's lean-manufacturing expertise, Walmart's distribution expertise, Procter & Gamble's consumer-marketing expertise, or Nvidia's GPU-design expertise. Because competition is unrelenting, companies must continually evolve how they deploy expertise to stay relevant, and history shows what happens when they fail to keep pace: Nokia commanded 40 percent of the global mobile-phone market in 2007 on the strength of hardware and manufacturing expertise, but the smartphone era's shift to software-ecosystem expertise caught Nokia (along with Motorola, Sony Ericsson, and BlackBerry) unable to adapt in time, and virtually all its market share migrated to Apple and Samsung's iOS and Android ecosystems instead. Two structural forces are accelerating how fast this kind of displacement can now happen: the total body of relevant expertise in the world keeps expanding at a pace no individual or single company can track (published academic papers referencing AI's application in biology alone grew from under 200 in 2001 to more than 45,000 just twenty years later), while simultaneously the cost of accessing existing expertise keeps falling. The article grounds the strategic implication of this second force in Ronald Coase's 1937 theory of the firm, which argues that a company's optimal size and scope is set by the relative balance of internal versus external costs — as external costs (accessing expertise through the market) fall, Coase's logic predicts companies should narrow their internal scope and rely more on external sourcing, a pattern already visible in how modern direct-to-consumer retailers assemble an entire operating stack (Shopify for e-commerce, Google for advertising, Stripe for payments, Amazon for logistics, Salesforce and Workday for back-office, Microsoft for cloud and AI) rather than building each capability internally, letting even small and midsize businesses compete credibly against much larger, more vertically integrated incumbents. <span class="src">Strategy in an Era of Abundant Expertise</span>

AI represents the newest and fastest-moving chapter of this cost-of-expertise decline. Providers are rapidly embedding AI "copilots," "bots," and "assistants" — GitHub Copilot for coding, ServiceNow's Now Assist, Salesforce's Agentforce — directly into existing workflows, and while the quality of embedded expertise in these tools is already relatively high, it continues improving quickly even as its cost keeps collapsing (the price of accessing OpenAI's GPT-4 through a developer API fell more than 99 percent over an 18-month span). The strategic significance is that AI assistants let companies access expertise for individual tasks or steps within a process, rather than needing to outsource or fully replace an entire end-to-end process at once, as offshoring and traditional outsourcing historically required — meaning process-level improvements are now achievable with far more granularity and far lower switching cost than before, and workers at every level may increasingly shift toward supervisory roles, approving AI-driven actions and managing exceptions rather than executing every step personally. Companies that successfully exploit this dynamic realize what the article calls a "triple product." The first component, cost and time savings, shows up concretely across domains: developers using AI coding tools (GitHub Copilot, Amazon CodeWhisperer, Replit, Cursor) complete tasks 20 to 55 percent faster across multiple studies; a large real-world study found generative AI assistants helped call-center representatives resolve 14 percent more issues per hour; an experiment found security professionals using an AI assistant completed tasks 7 percent more accurately and 23 percent faster; and Moderna's 6,000 employees collectively built more than 900 specialized internal AI assistants that compressed tasks (from optimizing clinical-trial drug doses to drafting regulatory responses) from weeks down to minutes. The second component, greater workforce productivity, follows from treating expertise as normally distributed across any population of employees — AI assistants put at least a baseline level of expertise into every user's hands, and early deployment data shows this pattern consistently: a BCG/Harvard randomized controlled trial found AI-augmented consultants completed 12 percent more tasks 25 percent faster than a control group, with output-quality scores rising 43 percent for lower-skilled consultants versus 17 percent for the highest-skilled ones — AI narrows the performance gap between average and top performers more than it widens the ceiling, with implications for onboarding time, workforce flexibility, and organizational structure (wider managerial spans become viable if median performance requires less oversight). The third component, more investment in activities that actually matter, follows once the first two free up resources: smart companies identify the handful of processes where they can credibly claim world-class expertise, double down there, and lean on AI-enabled third-party platforms for everything else — illustrated by FocusFuel, a caffeinated-gummy startup founded in 2023 that used gen AI across market analysis, supplier identification, packaging design, and marketing strategy to launch its entire operation within months and reach seven-figure revenue in its first eight months, while its founders concentrated their own attention on product strategy and customer relationships specifically. <span class="src">Strategy in an Era of Abundant Expertise</span>

Capturing this triple-product return in practice requires a real foundation and a deliberate rollout approach, not a single big-bang initiative. The article assumes companies are already reasonably far along in digital transformation — digitized data, cloud computing adoption, established security and governance protocols — since waiting to complete transformation entirely before attempting any AI-driven gains is unrealistic given how long that process typically takes; the better path is starting with a small number of processes, sometimes just one, where AI has already demonstrated value elsewhere and where digitized data and software-centric workflows make near-term improvement genuinely achievable (coding, customer service, marketing, and general productivity tasks are cited as the most tractable starting points). AI safety and governance need to be treated as foundational from the outset rather than bolted on afterward, given real risks around bias, misinformation, deepfakes, and cyberattack, and these principles are far easier to define concretely against one specific business process than in the abstract. Because organizational change is often harder than the underlying technical implementation, the article recommends cultivating a deliberate group of early-adopter employees who become internal AI champions and peer mentors, accelerating adoption organically rather than relying solely on top-down mandates — reinforced by genuine investment in company-wide AI training, illustrated by Moderna's mandatory 20-hour internal "AI academy," since research shows AI initiatives commonly fail at the front line without it. Finally, AI needs a real budget allocated even for early-stage activities, on the expectation that productivity gains will more than offset AI costs over time, with the end state being that AI becomes a standard, ongoing line item across every division's budget — lines of business, functions, and the corporate center alike — rather than a bounded pilot-program expense. Coursera illustrates this full arc: its CEO recognized gen AI's potential early, invested in specialized training for its software engineers, deployed an AI coding assistant, and within roughly a year had incorporated AI capabilities into multiple products (translation, personalized learning, automated course creation), positioning the company to keep innovating as new AI capability becomes available rather than treating its initial rollout as a one-time project. <span class="src">Strategy in an Era of Abundant Expertise</span>

The article's strategic conclusion is that every company needs to reevaluate its own strategy against three specific questions as AI makes more expertise abundantly and cheaply available to competitors and customers alike. First, which aspects of the problem a company currently solves for customers will customers simply use AI to solve for themselves — travel agents, for instance, have already lost the basic destination-research and reservation-booking functions to AI apps capable of building tailored itineraries directly from a traveler's preferences, and as AI's ability to take action improves further (making the actual reservations, not just recommending them), travel agents will need to reinvent themselves around genuinely differentiated value, such as curating unique events and experiences AI can't originate on its own. Second, which types of expertise a company currently possesses will need to evolve most to stay ahead of AI's growing capability rather than be commoditized by it — in medicine, where AI can already outperform physicians at some forms of image-based diagnosis, doctors' offices will need to lean further into the nontechnical capabilities AI doesn't replicate: empathy, caregiving, and collaborative treatment-planning across a care team. Third, which durable assets a company can build or strengthen specifically because AI is unlikely to directly erode them — brands, deep customer relationships, ownership of genuinely scarce physical assets, and network effects are named as categories largely outside AI's current reach; a consumer-product designer can already use AI to generate new prototype designs quickly, but the quality of those designs still depends entirely on the depth and nuance of the specifications feeding them, meaning the ability to gather deep, trusted customer research may become more differentiating over time than the raw design-generation capability itself, which AI increasingly commoditizes. The overall argument is not that differentiated expertise stops mattering as AI advances, but that which specific expertise and assets actually confer advantage will keep shifting, and the companies that thrive will be the ones that continuously re-examine and adapt that bundle rather than assuming past sources of advantage remain durable by default. <span class="src">Strategy in an Era of Abundant Expertise</span>

<span class="src">How 3 Companies Are Using AI to Mimic Expert Judgement</span> (HBR, November 2024) supplies a complementary, internally-focused counterpart to the other source's externally-focused Coasian argument: rather than a company buying cheap expertise from the outside market (AI copilots, embedded assistants), these three anonymized case studies show companies capturing and scaling the judgment of their own best internal experts. At a German venture capital firm the article calls Bavaria Ventures, junior analysts overwhelmed by a rising volume of startup applications lacked the experience to spot promising deals even when following the firm's own decision template, so the company built "AI-4-VC," a hybrid machine-learning and NLP system trained to mimic how an experienced managing partner ranks applications; a senior analyst periodically reviews and can overrule its rankings, with each override retraining the model's criteria weights in near-real-time, and the system proved accurate even on relatively small datasets. At an Indian satellite-analytics firm the article calls Sat Vista Analytics, overloaded remote-sensing specialists were replaced not by rule-based automation but by "analysis avatars" built by observing what the company's best specialists actually did with data — deliberately not by asking them to explain their process, since practitioners are often poor at articulating their own tacit expertise — letting junior specialists and new hires absorb best practices without monopolizing senior staff's time. And at an Australian company the article calls Be Smarter, rather than building generic online training courses, R&D leadership deployed a roster of AI agents, each representing a distinct area of technical expertise, that employees could select from when facing a problem and rate for usefulness, creating a peer-driven recommender system for which agent best suits which type of problem. Across all three cases, the article frames this as a shift from static, rule-based 1980s-style expert systems (which struggled to keep pace with fast-changing managerial contexts) to continuously updated, feedback-driven systems that stay current as conditions and preferences evolve. <span class="src">How 3 Companies Are Using AI to Mimic Expert Judgement</span>

Read together, the two sources describe two complementary paths to the same underlying shift: expertise becoming abundant and cheap to access, whether that expertise is purchased from an external AI-powered market (the Coasian argument) or captured from a company's own best people and scaled across the rest of the organization (the expert-system case studies). Both converge on a similar warning about durability: expertise-based advantage doesn't disappear under either path, but it becomes something a company must continuously re-earn — by evolving which expertise it holds, in the first source's case, or by continuously retraining captured expert systems against new data and overrides, in the second's — rather than something that, once established, persists on its own.
