## Summary

The "Rule of 40" — that a software company's growth rate plus profit margin should exceed 40% — is a widely used gauge of SaaS company health, and Bain and OPEXEngine's analysis of nearly 80 SaaS companies found that the ones who beat it consistently outperform on five specific operational benchmarks — employee retention, sales-and-marketing efficiency, customer lifetime value, opex discipline, and existing-customer growth — capabilities the market rewards with roughly three times the enterprise-value-to-revenue multiple of companies that fall short. A companion piece surveys the broader alphabet soup of alternative SaaS metrics (ARR, RPO, NRR, contribution, adjusted EBITDA, TAM) that investors must decode alongside the Rule of 40 to evaluate cloud company performance.

## Highlights

- Companies that beat the Rule of 40 (combined growth rate plus profit margin exceeding 40%) are, on average, three times more valuable in terms of total enterprise value to revenue than companies that fall short of it.
- Employee retention was the single most important of the five leading indicators Bain and OPEXEngine identified for beating the Rule of 40 — leading software companies understand that employee satisfaction and customer satisfaction are correlated, and invest in both accordingly.
- Sales-and-marketing efficiency, measured via the "magic number" (the ratio of revenue growth to sales-and-marketing spend), reflects how productively a company's salesforce converts spend into incremental growth-oriented revenue rather than just maintenance revenue.
- High-performing SaaS companies show better customer lifetime value-to-customer acquisition cost ratios, driven by targeting the right customers and delivering products with high value over extended periods — digital-native SaaS companies have a structural edge here over incumbents.
- Opex discipline distinguishes Rule-of-40 winners: their product development processes are efficient, they ruthlessly cull underperforming items from the development pipeline to concentrate on the most promising opportunities, and they keep sales, administrative, and customer-success costs in check.
- Rule-of-40 winners are more efficient at generating new revenue from existing customers (upgrades, cross-selling) while also doing a better job limiting customer churn — growing efficiently from within their existing base rather than relying solely on new-logo acquisition.
- Beyond the Rule of 40, cloud companies report a proliferating set of alternative metrics investors must learn to decode: annual recurring revenue (ARR, monthly recurring revenue times 12), remaining performance obligations (RPO, contracted-but-not-yet-recognized revenue and backlog), net retention rate (NRR, same-store-sales-style tracking of revenue per existing customer over time), contribution (transaction profitability before fixed costs), adjusted EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization, stock-based compensation, and other one-time items), and total addressable market (TAM).

## Concept

The "Rule of 40" — the principle that a software company's combined growth rate and profit margin should together exceed 40% — has become a widely used shorthand gauge of SaaS company performance, and Bain's research finds the market rewards it decisively: companies that beat the 40% threshold are, on average, three times more valuable in terms of total enterprise value to revenue than companies that fall short. To understand what specifically distinguishes the winners, Bain and OPEXEngine analyzed 2019 financial data for nearly 80 SaaS companies and found that those beating the Rule of 40 scored consistently higher across five specific operational benchmarks — capabilities the market appears to price in directly. The single most important of the five was employee retention: leaders in software, and in every field, understand that customer satisfaction correlates closely with employee satisfaction, and devote resources to protecting both together, with Bain noting that measuring and managing employee satisfaction itself needed to account for pandemic-era shifts in staffing, workspace, travel, and safety expectations at the time of the research. Second was efficient sales-and-marketing spend, tracked via the "magic number" — the ratio of revenue growth to sales-and-marketing spend — which functions as a direct measure of salesforce productivity and whether the sales organization is genuinely geared toward growing the business rather than simply maintaining it. Third was customer lifetime value: Rule-of-40 leaders show better customer-lifetime-value-to-customer-acquisition-cost ratios, reflecting effective targeting of the right customers and delivery of products that sustain high value over extended periods; Bain notes companies across industries were improving this metric via digital investment that strips cost out of the acquisition process, with digital-native SaaS companies holding a structural edge over less digitally native incumbents in pushing this advantage further. Fourth was disciplined operating-expense management: Rule-of-40 winners already carried better opex-to-revenue ratios entering the 2020 recession, reflecting efficient product development processes that avoid sinking investment into products that go nowhere — many of these companies are, in Bain's description, "ruthless" about culling their development pipeline, pulling investment from less-promising ventures to concentrate resources on the most promising ones, while simultaneously running highly productive sales organizations, containing administrative costs, and continuing to invest specifically in customer success. Fifth was efficient growth from the existing customer base: Rule-of-40 leaders generate new revenue from existing customers — through upgrades and cross-selling — more efficiently than their peers, while also doing a better job limiting customer churn, meaning they grow both from expanding what existing customers spend and from keeping more of those customers in the first place, rather than relying primarily on new-customer acquisition to hit growth targets. Bain's broader economic context for this analysis was the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic: even as most enterprise IT budgets were being cut amid the resulting economic disruption, many enterprise software buyers were simultaneously planning to increase spending on SaaS products, particularly in security and collaboration categories, as more employees shifted to remote work under lockdowns — a trend Bain saw as reinforcing the broader move away from on-premise software. Combined with the fact that companies already excelling on these five operational benchmarks entered the downturn from a position of structural strength, Bain's assessment was that even amid pandemic-driven economic hardship, many SaaS companies performing well on these dimensions should be able to sustain their growth trajectories and continue beating the Rule of 40.

A companion piece, <span class="src">The Cloud Is Already Confusing—the Metrics Make It Worse</span> (Barron's, 2021), widens the lens from the Rule of 40 specifically to the broader vocabulary of alternative metrics cloud companies use to describe their own performance — vocabulary investors must learn to read critically rather than take at face value. The article opens with venture investor Bill Gurley's prescient 1998 warning that alternative performance measures let companies generate impressive-looking revenue by effectively "selling dollar bills for 85 cents," a critique aimed even at the now-standard price-to-sales ratio; that warning proved accurate ahead of the dot-com crash, yet complex metrics never disappeared and instead multiplied as cloud companies grew more sophisticated. The article defines the terms investors most need to decode: annual recurring revenue (ARR, useful for companies blending subscription and term-based contracts, since it smooths out the up-front revenue recognition typical of term deals); remaining performance obligations (RPO, contracted work not yet recognized or completed, a forward-looking signal distinct from ARR); net retention rate (NRR, a same-store-sales-style measure of revenue growth per existing customer, with high NRR — one company profiled hit 168% — signaling that existing customers want more of what a company sells); contribution (transaction-level profitability before fixed costs, sometimes paired with a "take rate" for marketplace businesses); adjusted EBITDA (standard EBITDA with stock-based compensation and one-time items stripped out); and total addressable market (TAM), the most marketing-oriented of the group and the easiest to inflate. Read together with the Rule of 40 research, this piece supplies the metric vocabulary investors need before they can even apply Bain's five operational benchmarks — ARR and NRR in particular are the raw inputs behind growth-rate calculations that feed directly into the Rule of 40 itself. <span class="src">The Cloud Is Already Confusing—the Metrics Make It Worse</span>

## Open Questions

- This 2020 research predates the AI-driven SaaS disruption covered elsewhere in the vault by several years — it's unclear from this source alone whether the five operational benchmarks identified here (especially the "magic number" and CLV/CAC ratios, both built around per-seat subscription economics) remain equally predictive of company value once consumption-based, AI-driven pricing models become widespread, or whether a revised set of benchmarks will be needed for the AI+SaaS era.
- The underlying 2019 dataset (nearly 80 SaaS companies) is not named or broken out by company in this source, so the specific companies driving the five-benchmark findings can't be independently verified from this article alone.
- The metrics-glossary piece is a 2021 snapshot and doesn't say which of ARR, RPO, NRR, contribution, or adjusted EBITDA is most prone to being gamed or presented misleadingly — it warns generally that "zany measures" persist despite past warnings, without ranking today's metrics by risk the way it does for the historical price-to-sales example.
