## Summary

AI-generated code accumulates technical debt faster than human-written code, especially in legacy ("brownfield") systems and in the hands of inexperienced developers, but the goal for leaders isn't zero debt — it's managing debt strategically, using frameworks like PAID to prioritize remediation by business value and treating tech debt reduction as an investment in AI-readiness rather than a pure cost center. A companion BCG framework flips the lens from AI-generated debt to AI-remediated debt, mapping six myths that derail AI-driven modernization of pre-existing legacy systems and the orchestrated, evidence-grounded toolchain that actually works.

## Concept

MIT Sloan Management Review's research on AI-augmented software development — based on interviews spanning junior developers to CIOs across insurance, web hosting, social media, defense, consulting, and fintech, alongside trade-press review and economic modeling — finds that generative AI, while a genuine productivity booster in controlled settings (GitHub reports Copilot users up to 55% more productive; McKinsey finds developers completing tasks up to twice as fast with AI assistance), carries a major caveat: those studies were conducted on isolated tasks in controlled environments, not on software built atop complex, real-world existing systems. When AI-generated code is scaled rapidly or applied to brownfield (legacy) environments, technical debt compounds far faster and becomes much harder to manage. Technical debt itself functions like financial debt: the "principal" is the work needed to modernize and refactor code, and the "interest" is the ongoing complexity tax that slows maintenance, complicates scaling, and raises failure risk — while some debt is unavoidable, deploying AI-generated code without care is, in the researchers' framing, akin to borrowing at a much higher interest rate than usual, since "the problem with AI is that it can't see the big picture." The evidence for this acceleration is now measurable at scale: GitClear's analysis of millions of lines of code between 2020 and 2024 found an eightfold increase in duplicated code blocks and a twofold increase in code churn — both markers of declining code quality — over a period coinciding with AI coding assistants' rise, and Google's 2024 DORA (DevOps Research and Assessment) report found that a 25% increase in AI usage improves code review and documentation quality but simultaneously produces a 7.2% decrease in delivery stability. Two factors most strongly predict how much risk a given AI-coding deployment carries: the development environment (greenfield projects with no legacy code carry meaningfully lower risk than brownfield projects layered onto existing systems, since early-stage greenfield code will likely require major revision regardless of how it was produced) and developer skill level (highly skilled developers can recognize architectural flaws and intervene before debt spreads, while low-skilled developers are more likely to let AI-generated debt snowball — one Fortune 50 developer described the dynamic bluntly: with AI, "a junior engineer can write as fast as a senior engineer, but they don't have the cognitive sense of what they're doing... or if it's a good idea to do what they're doing"). When both risk factors are present simultaneously — an inexperienced developer working in a brownfield environment — the researchers suggest it may be best to avoid deploying AI-generated code entirely. The consequences of letting this kind of debt compound unmanaged are not abstract: Southwest Airlines' 2022 crew-scheduling meltdown, rooted in long-accumulated technical debt, stranded more than 16,900 flights and cost over $750 million; technical debt also drove the 2024 CrowdStrike outage that disrupted health care delivery worldwide; and a combination of antiquated air traffic control technology and staffing shortages caused massive 2025 delays and cancellations at Newark Liberty International Airport. To manage rather than eliminate this AI-accelerated debt, the researchers recommend three concrete moves: developing clear, specific guidelines for when and how AI-assisted coding tools should be used (going beyond the high-level responsible-AI principles — fairness, privacy, inclusiveness — that companies like Microsoft, Google, Meta, and Salesforce have already established, toward day-to-day guidance that will likely need to expand into using AI to actively improve existing legacy code bases, an approach Morgan Stanley has already begun experimenting with in-house since off-the-shelf models aren't yet reliable enough for legacy code translation); treating technical debt management as a standing engineering priority built into everyday workflows rather than an emergency response to something breaking, since the researchers' economic modeling shows a brief short-term performance gain from ignoring debt is eventually eclipsed by its long-term cost; and investing specifically in training junior developers to use AI tools without letting debt snowball, which requires evolving traditional code review so senior developers coach junior colleagues on responsible AI use rather than only checking code quality after the fact — functioning as a guardrail against the erosion of next-generation developers' foundational skills. <span class="src">The Hidden Costs of Coding With Generative AI</span>

An earlier, broader MIT SMR piece on the same underlying problem — co-authored with Accenture and grounded in a study of 1,500 global companies across 10 countries and 19 industries, plus in-depth C-level interviews — establishes the strategic framework this more AI-coding-specific research builds on. Technical debt, in this account, isn't just an IT problem: with over $2.4 trillion in annual US costs, it's a business liability requiring CEO-level attention, and as AI is poised to penetrate every business function, "all technical debt is becoming AI technical debt" — outdated infrastructure and applications directly block organizations from deploying the AI solutions that could otherwise reshape how they compete. Companies best positioned for AI-driven change share a "reinvention-ready digital core" — cloud infrastructure, data, and AI components that can be easily updated — and typically dedicate around 15% of their IT budget to tech debt remediation. Snack company Mondelez International illustrates this pattern concretely: facing a fragmented IT landscape of more than 1,000 applications, many redundant and costly to maintain, Mondelez worked with Accenture to assess each application on software quality, performance, and operating cost, consolidating its portfolio to reduce both total cost of ownership and tech debt — freeing it to later launch a generative-AI-powered marketing platform with Accenture and Publicis Groupe. The research's core strategic reframe is that the goal of tech debt management is not eliminating debt entirely, but managing it deliberately, using categories borrowed from lending — principal (cost of updating outmoded technology), interest (the ongoing cost of working around it), liabilities (issues that arise from leaving it unaddressed), and opportunity cost (value the company cannot realize because of it) — with remediation prioritized at the level of principal first, since managing it well minimizes the accrual of interest, liabilities, and lost opportunity downstream. Not all technical debt is bad: some is a deliberate, worthwhile investment, illustrated by a company deploying a custom generative AI chatbot for internal knowledge management that will inevitably encounter bugs and a learning curve due to knowledge gaps — if leaders let fear of that debt kill the project outright, the company loses the team, talent, and head start it would otherwise have once more mature AI solutions emerge, ceding the opportunity to a competitor willing to tolerate the same short-term cost. To prioritize among many candidate remediation projects, the researchers developed the PAID framework, which scores debt along two axes — the level of technical debt present and its effect on business value — yielding four action categories: Prioritize (high debt, high business-value impact, needing immediate remediation — e.g., a healthcare provider's patient data scattered across silos, directly affecting care quality); Address (high debt, low business-value impact currently, but scheduled for remediation before that impact grows — e.g., a bank's outdated but functional CRM that limits personalization); Investigate (low debt, high business-value impact, where neglect is nonetheless risky — e.g., an outmoded inventory system quietly complicating long-term financial planning); and Document (low debt, low business-value impact, warranting monitoring but low remediation priority — e.g., obsolete software used by a team about to be merged into another business unit). The PAID framework doubles as a tool for deciding what technical debt can be strategically written off entirely when a company is ready to leapfrog to AI-enabled services, illustrated by CTT, Portugal's 500-year-old national postal service, which tackled a costly patchwork of on-premises systems by migrating select data-center workloads to the cloud, cutting IT operating costs 15% and — as a direct payoff — reducing the time to start work on a new AI solution from weeks to hours. The cost of moving too slowly is equally concrete: Sungard Availability Services, a provider of on-premises IT disaster recovery services, filed for bankruptcy in 2022 in part because it had outlined but not executed fast enough on addressing its technical debt as customer demand shifted toward cloud-based backup and remote-work capabilities, a shift COVID-19 sharply accelerated — leaving Sungard unable to compete with more cloud-focused rivals once demand intensified. The researchers' closing reframe is to treat tech debt remediation not as a cost center but as an investment in innovation capacity: since AI is now synonymous with innovation for most organizations, framing debt remediation in terms of the AI-enabled opportunities it unlocks — rather than purely the maintenance burden it removes — shifts the internal conversation from "sunk cost" to "growth enabler." <span class="src">How to Manage Tech Debt in the AI Era</span>

<span class="src">Six Myths CIOs Must Avoid in AI-Based IT Modernization</span> (BCG, August 2026) flips this concept's lens from AI-generated debt to AI-remediated debt: rather than AI coding tools accumulating new technical debt (this concept's other two sources), BCG addresses how AI can be misapplied when it's used to pay down existing legacy debt through IT modernization, achieving 25-35% cost reductions and 30-40% faster delivery only when six specific misconceptions are avoided. Myth 1 (modernization delivers ROI on its own) warns that many legacy systems are already amortized and functional, so modernization needs an explicit business case paired with business redesign, not a purely technical justification — echoing this concept's PAID framework's insistence on scoring debt by business-value impact, not technical severity alone. Myth 2 (a single LLM or agent can modernize an entire system) is grounded in a case study directly analogous to this concept's brownfield-risk findings: a large organization applying one AI model across a portfolio of thousands of applications saw only 30% code-accuracy over its first 12 months, rising to 85% only once the team stopped relying on the model alone and instead built an automated, evidence-based map of dependencies, transactions, and data flows to ground it — BCG's prescribed fix is an orchestrated toolchain (deterministic dependency mapping, evidence-backed generative AI for documentation, architecture-support tools, engineering agents, and automated testing, each applied to the specific stage it suits) rather than one tool for every task. Myth 3 (AI can explain everything from the code base alone) extends this concept's "AI can't see the big picture" finding into a concrete mitigation: a telecom operator team applying AI across 60 applications and roughly 10 million lines of code accelerated documentation by up to 80% and improved accuracy by around 60% specifically by pairing AI with a deterministic, runtime-telemetry-grounded system map rather than trusting AI's own plausible-sounding narrative of undocumented legacy behavior. Myths 4 through 6 extend the same evidence-before-automation discipline further downstream: AI cannot design target architecture from specifications alone, since specifications capture original intent while actual system behavior drifts from it over years of patches, so architecture still requires human judgment on tradeoffs even when AI generates and stress-tests the options; AI-assisted coding alone does not accelerate modernization when critical design decisions (domain boundaries, behavioral requirements, data-migration approach) remain unresolved, illustrated by a major transportation operator whose AI-rewritten legacy code produced unreliable, silently-breaking results until the team invested one week building a trusted dependency map before resuming; and AI does not reduce the need for testing or de-risk cutover, since AI can draft test cases and accelerate triage but only within an environment that already has clearly defined outcomes and robust regression baselines — without which "AI-driven testing can produce a lot of activity leading to limited assurance." BCG's recommended three-stage sequence (Discover: build a fact-based, deterministic-plus-AI-accelerated understanding of the current system; Reimagine: use AI to generate and compare target-state options and sequence a roadmap with stable intermediate states; Transform: execute with AI agents inside guardrails, validating continuously) operationalizes this concept's own "principal first" PAID-framework logic specifically for AI-driven modernization projects, reinforcing that the discipline of grounding AI in verified evidence before trusting its output applies as much to AI remediating old debt as it does to AI generating new debt. <span class="src">Six Myths CIOs Must Avoid in AI-Based IT Modernization</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source quantifies how much of the measured 2020-2024 rise in code duplication and churn (GitClear's data) is specifically attributable to AI coding tools versus other contemporaneous factors (team growth, codebase age, etc.) — the correlation is presented but not causally isolated.
- It's unclear from either source how the PAID framework's "level of technical debt" axis should be measured in practice for AI-generated code specifically, versus for traditionally accumulated legacy debt — the framework predates the AI-coding-specific research and isn't explicitly adapted to it.
- BCG's six myths are illustrated with unnamed case studies (a large organization, a telecom operator, a major transportation operator) rather than named companies, so it's not possible to independently verify or cross-reference these cases against this concept's other named examples (Mondelez, CTT, Sungard).
