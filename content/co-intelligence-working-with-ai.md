## Summary

Ethan Mollick's four principles for working productively with generative AI — always invite it in, stay the human in the loop, treat it like a person while specifying what kind, and assume it's the worst AI you'll ever use. Also covers his framework for deciding which tasks to keep for yourself, delegate, or fully automate.

## Concept

Co-Intelligence frames current-generation generative AI as a General Purpose Technology (GPT) — comparable in scope to steam power or the internet — but argues its impact on work and education may ultimately exceed those of earlier general-purpose technologies, because unlike technologies that primarily automated mechanical or repetitive physical labor, AI functions as a "co-intelligence" that directly augments or substitutes for human thinking itself; the book cites early studies finding productivity improvements in the 20-80 percent range across a wide variety of job types as evidence of the scale of this effect, while noting that these gains still land within the plausible range of productivity effects documented for earlier general-purpose technologies like steam power. From this premise, the book proposes four practical rules for working with AI productively rather than either avoiding it or over-trusting it. The first, "always invite AI to the table," argues that because AI is a genuinely novel general-purpose technology with no established instruction manual, the only reliable way to learn its actual value and limits for a specific task is direct, repeated experimentation — including experimentation that turns out disappointing, unnerving, or simply unhelpful, since even a failed attempt teaches something about where the technology's boundary actually lies for that kind of work. The second, "be the human in the loop," insists that incorporating human judgment into how an AI system is actually used remains essential even as the technology improves, motivated in part by a specific behavioral finding: an AI is implicitly optimizing to produce an answer the user will find satisfying, and this "make the user happy" objective can outweigh accuracy, meaning a sufficiently insistent user can talk a confidently-worded but fabricated answer out of the system and be persuaded by its confidence regardless of correctness. <span class="src">Co-Intelligence</span>

The third rule, "treat AI like a person, but tell it what kind of person it is," is grounded in the empirical observation that persona and role instructions measurably change output quality — research cited in the book found LLMs produce better answers when a user frames a request as personally consequential (e.g. "this is important to my career"), and giving the model explicit guidance about what kind of persona to adopt reliably improves the interest and usefulness of its output — while the book is simultaneously explicit that treating AI as a person carries real risk, since doing so can create unrealistic public and even expert expectations, obscure the system's actual nature as software, and shape how users relate to it emotionally in ways that may not serve their well-being; the recommendation to adopt this framing is thus presented as a pragmatic, results-oriented heuristic rather than an ontological claim about AI having genuine personhood. The fourth rule, "assume this is the worst AI you will ever use," is a forward-looking discipline meant to prevent users from treating today's specific capability limitations as permanent facts about the technology, given the pace at which model capability has continued to improve. Hallucination — the tendency of LLMs to generate confident, plausible-sounding, but factually incorrect output — is treated in the book as a structural consequence of how these models actually work rather than a solvable implementation bug: because a language model has no genuine ground-truth knowledge store and instead generates each output by predicting a statistically plausible continuation token by token, tasks that require exact recall are especially prone to hallucination, models are generally not optimized to express "I don't know" as a response, and — critically — asking a model to explain its own reasoning after generating an answer produces another plausible-sounding piece of generated text rather than a genuine account of the actual internal process that produced the original answer, since the model has no real introspective access to that process. <span class="src">Co-Intelligence</span>

The book restates and applies the "Jagged Frontier" concept — also documented in this vault through the BCG/Harvard field experiment Mollick co-authored — as the underlying reason familiarity through repeated hands-on use matters so much: because AI capability is unevenly distributed across tasks in ways that don't track human intuitions about which tasks should be easy or hard for a machine, the only reliable way an individual can learn where a specific task in their own work sits relative to that boundary is direct, sustained experimentation rather than reasoning about it in the abstract. Building on this, the book proposes a three-category framework for deciding how to allocate any given task along a spectrum of trust in the AI. Just Me Tasks are kept entirely human, either because AI genuinely isn't useful there yet, or — increasingly important as AI capability grows — because a person deliberately chooses to keep a task human for personal or ethical reasons, such as raising one's children, making genuinely important decisions, or expressing one's own values, independent of whether AI could technically assist. Delegated Tasks are assigned to AI but still checked by a human, typically tasks that are tedious, low-importance, or time-consuming for a person but efficient for AI to handle — the book stresses that "delegated" does not mean "low-stakes," since a delegated task can still be complex and carry real consequences if the AI's output goes unchecked and turns out wrong. Automated Tasks are handed to AI entirely, without ongoing human review — described as still a very small category in current practice, but one the book expects to expand as AI performance improves within specific, well-bounded domains. Centaur and Cyborg working patterns, covered in more empirical depth in this vault's companion concept on the BCG/Harvard jagged-frontier study, are presented here as the two practical approaches for capturing AI's benefit across this task spectrum: Centaur work keeps a clean division of labor, switching wholly between human-owned and AI-owned tasks, while Cyborg work blends human and AI effort within a single task at a much finer grain (for instance, letting AI complete a sentence a human started, and iterating back and forth); the book's recommended progression is to start from the first rule (inviting AI into everything), let Centaur-style task-switching emerge naturally as a person's sense of their own jagged frontier sharpens through that experimentation, and let Cyborg-style fine-grained blending develop organically from there rather than being adopted as a deliberate first step. <span class="src">Co-Intelligence</span>

A distinct organizational risk the book highlights is what it calls shadow AI use going unreported: an individual worker who discovers how to automate a substantial share of their own job faces a genuine, rational disincentive to disclose that discovery, since the natural managerial response to a visible productivity breakthrough is often simply "fire people, save money" rather than reinvesting the freed capacity — meaning organizations that actually want to capture and scale the bottom-up AI discoveries their own employees are already making need to deliberately and visibly reduce the fear associated with disclosing AI use (through incentives and psychological safety, not just permission), rather than assuming employees will volunteer productivity-enhancing AI workflows once given technical access to the tools. <span class="src">Co-Intelligence</span>
