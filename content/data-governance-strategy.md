## Summary

Treating data as a managed organizational asset rather than a departmental byproduct, including the case for a Chief Data Officer and enterprise-level data accountability. Covers the shift from ETL to ELT as big data's volume, variety, and velocity outgrew traditional pipelines, and the unresolved question of how to value data on a company's books. The book’s later chapters add an inventory-management analogy for valuing and stewarding data and guidance on leading and incrementally implementing a data strategy.

- Data should be managed as an organizational asset the same way physical assets like a truck fleet are: an organization should justify data-related investment against business need and priority, rather than accumulating or maintaining data by default. <span class="src">Modern Data Strategy</span>

- The rise of a dedicated Chief Data Officer role reflects organizations recognizing that managing an asset as important and fast-growing as data requires someone senior enough to have real authority across departments, not just within IT. <span class="src">Modern Data Strategy</span>

## Concept

Modern Data Strategy argues that data should be managed with the same organizational discipline applied to any other significant corporate asset — a fleet of trucks, a portfolio of real estate — rather than being allowed to accumulate as an incidental byproduct of whatever business processes happen to generate it. Just as an organization would debate the costs and benefits of acquiring new physical assets against a specific business need, the source argues data-related investment should be similarly justified against business priority, with dedicated organizational roles (most visibly the Chief Data Officer) and formal data-management domains — data quality, data governance, data architecture, data warehousing, master data management, interoperability, privacy, and security — treated as genuine management disciplines with their own standards and certifications, rather than as an undifferentiated subset of general IT responsibility. This reframing directly addresses a common organizational failure mode the source identifies: individual business units historically behaved as though they personally owned the data flowing through their own systems, which was individually rational (keeping tight control over data that provided them a competitive or operational advantage) but collectively harmful to enterprise-wide data management, since it fragmented and duplicated data across the organization; the recommended fix is separating data accountability (who answers for a given dataset's quality, security, and appropriate use) from data ownership (which the source argues should sit at the enterprise level rather than with whichever department happens to generate or first use a dataset). <span class="src">Modern Data Strategy</span>

The scale challenge behind this discipline is captured in the "3 V's" definition of big data — volume, variety (a complex mix of structured and unstructured formats), and velocity (the speed at which data changes) — severe enough across all three dimensions that conventional data-management approaches simply cannot cope. This scale shift is what drove the well-documented migration in data-pipeline design from ETL (extract, transform, load — where data is transformed to match its eventual target structure before being loaded anywhere) to ELT (extract, load, transform — where raw data is captured and loaded first, in its original form, and transformation is deferred until a specific analytical need actually calls for it). The underlying motivation for this shift is that organizations increasingly wanted to capture and retain as much raw data as possible up front, preserving future analytical optionality, rather than committing to a fixed set of transformations at ingestion time that might discard data that later turns out to matter. The source's central caution about this shift, however, is that raw-data hoarding is not itself a strategy: one of the most commonly cited reasons big-data analytics initiatives fail is not insufficient new data, but an organization's inability to properly manage and make sense of the data it already has, and research cited in the source on top-performing ("elite") data organizations found the two most consistently observed practices among them were, first, getting data governance genuinely right, and second, treating information as a valuable asset in practice, not merely in mission-statement language — both practices that a growing volume of poorly-governed raw data actively undermines rather than helps. <span class="src">Modern Data Strategy</span>

A specific and still largely unresolved question the source raises is how — and whether — data should be formally valued and carried on a company's balance sheet as an asset. Under current accounting practice, most organizations do not estimate the value of their data using the same cost-basis, market-value, or revenue-potential approaches applied to other intangible assets, and the source cites research finding this is partly explained by a financial incentive: treating data-related costs as an ordinary expense rather than a capitalized asset lets companies avoid showing data on their balance sheet, along with the associated tax implications of doing so. Despite this financial incentive against formal valuation, the source argues genuine practical reasons remain to value data internally even without formal accounting recognition — greater organizational accountability for data quality and use, a clearer basis for measuring IT effectiveness, and a stronger justification for information-systems spending — and proposes that organizations begin by maintaining an internal "data balance sheet" to track the value of their data assets over time, as a starting discipline that does not require resolving the broader formal-accounting question first. <span class="src">Modern Data Strategy</span>

The book's chapter-level treatment extends the asset analogy beyond valuation. Chapter 4 maps inventory management onto data management across four dimensions: cost (ordering and holding costs against the cost of obtaining, loading, licensing and administering data, with losses from poor quality as the counterpart to wastage), inventory types, stewardship (business-manager responsibility against domain-based stewardship, with coordination among data architects, IT and subject-matter experts) and obsolescence (reclaiming space against moving data among online storage, near-line storage and archives, which makes searches, integration and reporting more efficient). The analogy does not assign data an explicit value but approximates it through the resources required to manage it. <span class="src">4. Physical Asset Management vs. Data Management</span>

Chapters 5 and 6 turn to execution. Leading a data strategy requires a maturity of data management that organizations must grow over time, collaboration between business and IT, and a team that includes data people as well as process and technology people. Implementing it means choosing where to begin on the basis of critical business needs and the data management infrastructure already in place, and formalizing informal initiatives such as data stewardship, rather than attempting a comprehensive programme. <span class="src">5. Leading a Data Strategy</span> <span class="src">6. Implementing a Data Strategy</span>
