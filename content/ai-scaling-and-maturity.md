## Summary

Scaling AI starts with choosing projects well and ends with the organizational maturity that determines financial results. Frameworks for selecting use cases weigh risk against demand, favor a quick, industry-specific first pilot that creates measurable value and then concentrate on a deep and narrow domain rather than many shallow pilots, while MIT CISR's four-stage maturity model links higher maturity to above-average financial performance. CGI and KPMG explain how to cross the pilot-to-production gap through an operating model, modernized IT, data interoperability, agentic automation, culture and resilience, and BCG's survey data show spending narrowing to AI and mature firms earning about twice the return of less mature ones.

## Concept

Three frameworks, spanning different points in an organization's AI journey, address how to decide which initiatives deserve investment instead of chasing every plausible use case. The earliest is a 2x2 matrix for generative AI that plots risk, how likely and damaging fabricated or inaccurate output would be, against demand, whether a real, sustained need exists beyond the hype. The insight is to consider the two jointly, since both vary across industries and functions, and the high-demand, low-risk quadrant, with marketing copy, internal learning content, text review and editing and brainstorming, is where investment should concentrate first, because errors have limited consequences, a human typically reviews the output or the content is not fact-heavy, and demand already exists. Marketing illustrates it, since it needs constant idea generation, audience-tailored messaging and text-rich content, is rarely fact-dense in ways that create risk and has abundant example content to prime a model, which is why several marketing-copy startups became unicorns early. The caution is that even a nominally low-risk use such as corporate learning remains vulnerable to bias and factual error as humans are, so output should be treated as a first draft to refine through better prompts and genuine human review. <span class="src">A Framework for Picking the Right Generative AI Project</span>

Once a starting point exists, Andrew Ng addresses what makes a strong first pilot, whose purpose is partly to create value and more importantly to convince skeptical stakeholders to invest in AI capability. A good pilot delivers a quick win, ideally in six to twelve months, and running two or three parallel pilots raises the odds of at least one clear success. It is sized correctly, neither so trivial that success convinces no one nor so large that it stalls, and it is specific to the organization's industry rather than a generic capability, since a horizontal use case, such as a medical-device company building an AI resume screener, is likely to be outbuilt by a larger platform serving many industries and will not persuade stakeholders that AI is worth it for their own business. It can be accelerated with credible outside partners while an internal team is built, since waiting to build full capability risks falling behind the technology, and it must create value through reducing costs, increasing revenue through recommendation and prediction systems or enabling a new line of business. <span class="src">How to Choose Your First AI Project</span>

Ng's experience leading Google Brain illustrates the logic. His team did not tackle web search or advertising, Google's most central but politically fraught domains, and instead began with speech recognition, whose clear win earned credibility to bring in Google Maps as a second project, each success compounding until Google Brain became central to Google's AI transformation. He advises treating AI as automation of discrete tasks rather than entire jobs, since a radiologist's job includes reading X-rays, operating equipment, consulting colleagues and surgical planning, and automating just the reading is far more tractable than automating the entire role, appointing a cross-functional leader who bridges AI and domain knowledge, keeping the team small at typically 5 to 15 people, doing business-value and technical diligence before committing resources, and publicizing milestones internally through talks, awards and even external press, sharing credit generously with the business team so that other units follow. <span class="src">How to Choose Your First AI Project</span>

A later-stage framework addresses how broadly to deploy gen AI once pilots are done. The consumer goods company Reckitt faced a long list of individually ROI-positive use cases across presentations, customer support and procurement and recognized that pursuing them all would give only marginal, disconnected gains, so it concentrated on marketing, where gen AI could apply across interlinked tasks, insight generation, content creation and product development, that reinforce each other, with real-time consumer insight improving product innovation and in turn loyalty and advocacy. This deep and narrow approach produced 60% faster product-concept generation and brand and marketing communication processes 30% or more efficient within two years. The alternative, shallow and broad deployment of many disconnected use cases across departments, yields some real savings but is structurally limited, since when only 5-10% of tasks in a function use gen AI executives have little reason to rethink how it works, initiative fatigue sets in, easily copied use cases give little advantage and employee skepticism grows. <span class="src">Stop Running So Many AI Pilots</span>

A 2024 BCG survey of 1,000 CXOs found only 4% of companies pursue deep and narrow, yet they earn roughly double the ROI of shallow-and-broad deployers over time, a gap the authors expect to widen because deep and narrow compounds hard-to-copy advantage while shallow and broad captures easily replicated efficiency. It is harder because it demands wholesale change management, which at Reckitt was about 70% of the challenge against 20% for data readiness and 10% for the AI itself. The framework gives four steps for choosing where to go deep. Place candidate applications on a spectrum from office productivity, such as summarizing meetings, which creates no advantage on its own, through domain reinvention, the end-to-end reimagining of a function that is the opportunity for almost any firm, to new business models, which are exciting but hard for companies whose core product, like toothpaste, does not lend itself to AI. Target domains where deep AI protects or extends an existing strength or builds a new one that is hard to copy, as L'Oréal's Beauty Genius chatbot draws on a decade of augmented-reality investment and proprietary skin-biology research that a copycat cannot replicate, and IKEA used gen AI to amplify its design-service strength, letting designers finish in about 10 minutes projects that took paid consultants days, drawing on its large community of interior designers and volume of quality data. <span class="src">Stop Running So Many AI Pilots</span>

Third, prioritize efficiency before growth within the domain, since cost reduction is faster and builds the executive confidence to greenlight harder growth bets, the sequence Reckitt followed by showing quick marketing-efficiency wins, such as compressing media campaign analysis from days to hours, before authorizing longer-horizon growth work. Fourth, map the domain's real work instead of picking use cases by impression, as Reckitt catalogued all 300 distinct tasks of its 2,000-plus global marketers through surveys and interviews and plotted each by automation potential and opportunity size, finding roughly 100 suited to gen AI and learning that marketers' entire role would need reconsidering as routine work was absorbed. Throughout, the organizing question is whether a top competitor could replicate the advantage with gen AI, since a rival need only offer a good-enough, cheaper, simpler alternative to do damage. <span class="src">Stop Running So Many AI Pilots</span>

After piloting, organizations must scale, and MIT CISR's enterprise AI maturity model, built from a 2022 survey of 721 companies and 2024 executive interviews, ties four stages to financial performance, since organizations in the first two underperform their industry's financial average and those in the last two outperform it, so maturity is a driver of competitive outcomes and not a curiosity of technology adoption. Stage 1, Experiment and Prepare, held 28% of enterprises and centers on workforce AI literacy, policy, becoming more evidence-based and comfortable with automated decisions, including early discussion of where humans must stay in the loop and what counts as acceptable and ethical AI, along with board and executive literacy. Stage 2, Build Pilots and Capabilities, was the largest group at 34% and shifts from experimentation to systematic innovation, defining success metrics, piloting use cases deliberately, tracking pilot value and telling the lessons story inside and outside. Its hardest part, according to MIT CISR's Peter Weill, is cultural, moving from command and control to coach and communicate, since AI's value depends on front-line employees making decisions and customers serving themselves, which a rigid hierarchy cannot support, and consolidating data silos and preparing data safely, often with investment in APIs, is also a defining challenge. <span class="src">What’s your company’s AI maturity level</span>

Stage 3, Industrialize AI Throughout the Enterprise, held 31% and means scalable enterprise architecture, transparent outcomes through business dashboards, a pervasive test-and-learn culture and broad process automation, and Weill is direct that applying AI to an incredibly complicated process makes success far harder, so simplification must precede automation. Organizations at this stage use foundation models and smaller task-specific language models on proprietary data on secure platforms to create new value, reaching what Weill calls the holy trinity of architecture, reuse and agents, the hard parts of the stage. Stage 4, AI Future-Ready, reached by just 7%, embeds AI in all decisions and uses proprietary AI internally while selling AI-based business services, AI capability as a product or both, built on people, platforms and four types of AI working together, analytical, generative, agentic and robotic. The recommendation is for senior technical and data leaders to assess honestly which stage describes most current AI activity, rather than edge cases, to discuss which capabilities need work and to proceed boldly from wherever they start. <span class="src">What’s your company’s AI maturity level</span>

CGI addresses the mechanics of progressing. AI now tops innovation-investment priorities across a three-year horizon in its client research, so organizations face pressure to focus on long-term scalability or risk diminished customer expectations, lost advantage and difficulty competing for scarce talent, and they tend to fail in one of two opposite ways, moving too cautiously with small safe pilots that never add up to value or too recklessly across too many initiatives without weighing risk. Scaling needs more than layering technology over unchanged infrastructure, inadequate data or opaque training-data provenance, and rests on five imperatives. A future-fit operating model is product-centric with scalable, agile, cross-functional teams, standardized processes with clear metrics and KPIs and a deliberate balance of standardization against agility, giving leaders visibility of total cost of ownership. IT modernization updates legacy systems and accelerates cloud adoption to match the pace AI needs, since AI on outdated infrastructure underperforms however sophisticated it is, and data strategy and interoperability focuses on cloud, SaaS and on-premises platforms, using AI to improve data quality and break silos. Agentic AI and automation reduces manual burden so teams can focus on strategic work, and culture and talent strategy invests in upskilling and an integrate-AI-everywhere mindset framed as augmentation, since failing to make that framing convincing risks attrition and talent competition. With these in place commercial organizations deploy AI quickly across the enterprise and respond to change faster, and public-sector entities gain faster responses to citizen requests and stronger cross-agency collaboration, while the last element is partnership with technology providers who share the strategic vision and stay contractually engaged through long-term delivery and support rather than only initial deployment. <span class="src">Scaling AI to Deliver Tangible Business Outcomes</span>

KPMG explains why the pilot-to-production gap opens in practice. Its central reframe is that the barrier is rarely failed experimentation but enterprise readiness, since a pilot runs on carefully curated data with minimal oversight, while production AI must work with a federated, silo-laden data landscape, dozens of legacy and modern applications each with their own API and security model, and governance requirements such as GDPR and CCPA compliance, model risk management, bias detection and explainability that the pilot architecture was never built to meet, or in KPMG's words, the pilot did not fail, the enterprise environment was not designed to support it. A diagnostic asks five questions, whether the organization can explain where AI costs and value sit, whether successful pilots have a path to enterprise deployment, whether tools change workflows and decisions rather than merely being deployed, whether the governance model could be explained to a regulator tomorrow and whether the architecture could adapt to shifting infrastructure conditions without major redesign. <span class="src">How CIOs Turn AI Pressure Into Enterprise AI That Scales</span>

KPMG's five CIO priorities are pursued together because each reinforces the others. Align technology strategy with enterprise priorities first, defining an operating model with a central AI center of excellence for governance so business-unit requests do not fragment the environment tool by tool. Build a credible investment narrative that ties AI to decision speed, customer responsiveness and resilience instead of a narrow cost-reduction frame, using tech-asset management and FinOps discipline to make spend forecastable and self-funding. Strengthen architecture and data foundations by replacing project-based data preparation with a data fabric or mesh with clear ownership, since fragmented infrastructure rather than model quality is the most common barrier to scale. Turn adoption into an operating model by delegating AI-driven process redesign to business-unit leaders, with IT as enabler rather than owner, and building executive AI fluency, and strengthen operational resilience by mapping system dependencies and operationalizing governance of vendors, data residency and permissions before disruption forces it. In KPMG's closing framing, organizations that get this balance right do not necessarily run more experiments than peers but become the organizations where AI works. <span class="src">How CIOs Turn AI Pressure Into Enterprise AI That Scales</span>

BCG's IT Spending Pulse supplies market-level evidence for the maturity-performance link. IT leaders expect 2026 spending to grow 5.8%, the strongest outlook the survey has recorded, but the growth is narrow, since 66% of buyers plan to increase AI and machine-learning spending, up 21 points from mid-2025, while cutting nearly every other category, with CRM and ERP negative again and IT services sentiment down 19 points, reflecting reprioritized budgets and not reshoring of outsourced work. AI is the only product category in which buyers plan to expand rather than consolidate their vendor roster, with a net 34% planning to add providers, and buyers are narrowing generative AI use cases to a smaller set of high-impact areas, analytics, customer service, security and internal communications, even as adoption of those priorities rises, which BCG frames as a move from experimentation to disciplined execution. <span class="src">IT Spending Pulse AI Takes Priority as Confidence Returns</span>

That discipline shows in returns. Weighted-average measured ROI on generative AI and agent deployments has risen to 13.8%, but high-maturity firms report roughly 19% against 8-9% for little-to-no- and low-maturity adopters, a gap BCG attributes less to spending more than to superior visibility into AI and token costs, which counterintuitively leads mature firms to more permissive, less throttled token policies because their top 10% of users generate outsized productivity without a proportionate share of spend, while less mature firms without that visibility default to hard usage limits and cautious, tactical cost management. <span class="src">IT Spending Pulse AI Takes Priority as Confidence Returns</span>
