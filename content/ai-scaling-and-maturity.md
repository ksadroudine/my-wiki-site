## Summary

MIT CISR's four-stage enterprise AI maturity model — organizations in the top two stages financially outperform their industry peers, those in the bottom two underperform — paired with CGI's five foundational imperatives for scaling AI past the pilot stage into tangible, sustained business outcomes. A companion KPMG framework diagnoses why scaling exposes decades-old structural fractures in enterprise architecture, and sets out five CIO priorities for closing the pilot-to-production gap. A BCG IT-spending survey supplies fresh market-level confirmation of the same maturity-performance link — high-maturity adopters now report roughly double the ROI of laggards, and AI is the only technology category where buyers are expanding rather than consolidating vendors.

## Concept

Two complementary frameworks address what happens after an organization has successfully piloted AI and needs to scale it — one describing the stages an organization typically progresses through, the other prescribing the foundational work required to actually get there. MIT CISR's enterprise AI maturity model, based on a 2022 survey of 721 companies and follow-up 2024 executive interviews, identifies four stages and, notably, ties them directly to financial performance: organizations in the first two stages underperform their industry's financial average, while those in the last two outperform it — establishing that AI maturity isn't merely a technology-adoption curiosity but a genuine driver of competitive financial outcomes. Stage 1, Experiment and Prepare, accounted for 28 percent of surveyed enterprises and centers on workforce AI literacy, formulating AI policy, becoming more evidence-based in decision-making, and building organizational comfort with automated decision-making generally; at this stage, organizations begin the foundational conversations about where humans need to remain in the loop for oversight and what constitutes acceptable and ethical AI use, alongside board- and executive-level literacy initiatives and broader enterprise skill-building. Stage 2, Build Pilots and Capabilities, was the largest single group at 34 percent, and represents the shift from pure experimentation to systematic innovation: defining metrics for success, piloting use cases deliberately (rather than accidentally), tracking the actual value pilots create, and telling that story of lessons learned both internally and externally to build momentum. The hardest part of Stage 2, per MIT CISR's Peter Weill, is cultural rather than technical — moving an organization from a command-and-control culture to a coach-and-communicate one, since AI's real value depends on enabling front-line employees to make decisions and customers to self-serve, neither of which a rigidly hierarchical, command-driven culture can actually support. Consolidating fragmented data silos and safely, securely preparing data for AI use is also a defining Stage 2 challenge, often requiring meaningful investment in the application programming interfaces that link disparate data sources and technologies together. Stage 3, Industrialize AI Throughout the Enterprise, held 31 percent of surveyed organizations and represents a significant step up in both potential business impact and the platform capabilities required to achieve enterprise-wide scale: building genuinely scalable enterprise architecture, making data and AI outcomes transparent through business dashboards, developing a pervasive test-and-learn culture, and expanding business-process automation broadly rather than function by function. Weill's framing is direct: trying to apply AI to an "incredibly complicated" unsimplified process makes success far harder, so simplification has to precede automation, not follow it. Organizations at this stage make significant use of foundation models and smaller, task-specific language models, applying them to proprietary data on secure platforms to create and capture genuinely new value — reaching what Weill calls the "holy trinity" of Stage 3: architecture, reuse, and agents, described as the genuinely hard parts of this stage to get right. Stage 4, AI Future-Ready, was reached by just 7 percent of surveyed enterprises and represents AI embedded in all decision-making, with organizations using proprietary AI internally while also selling new AI-based business services — the AI capability itself as a product, sold to other enterprises, or both — built around a combination of people, platforms, and four distinct types of AI working together: analytical, generative, agentic, and robotic. MIT CISR's practical recommendation for any organization using this model is straightforward: bring senior technical and data leaders together to honestly assess which stage best describes the majority of the organization's current AI activity (rather than fixating on edge cases), discuss which capabilities and skills genuinely need more work to progress, and proceed with deliberate boldness regardless of starting point. <span class="src">What’s your company’s AI maturity level</span>

CGI's complementary framework addresses the specific mechanics of what it takes to actually progress through stages like these — moving beyond initial experimentation and pilots into AI that reliably delivers tangible, sustained business outcomes at scale. The starting observation is that organizations, having made significant AI investments already (AI now tops innovation-investment priorities across a three-year horizon in CGI's own client research), face real pressure to focus on long-term scalability specifically or risk concrete downside: diminished customer expectations, lost competitive advantage, and difficulty competing for scarce AI talent. In practice, organizations attempting to scale tend to fail in one of two opposite ways: moving too cautiously, sticking to small, safe pilots that never accumulate into real value, or moving too recklessly, pursuing too many initiatives simultaneously or going all-in without adequately weighing the risks involved. CGI's framework holds that successfully scaling AI requires much more than layering new technology on top of unchanged infrastructure, inadequate data, or opaque training-data provenance — organizations must also directly address culture and change management, built on five foundational imperatives. A future-fit operating model means adopting a product-centric development approach with scalable, agile, cross-functional teams, standardizing processes while establishing clear metrics and KPIs to actually measure outcome quality and value, and deliberately balancing standardization against the agility needed to keep innovating responsibly, securely, and reliably — with the operating model itself needing to enable efficient resource allocation and give leadership real visibility into total cost of ownership. IT modernization means updating legacy systems and accelerating cloud adoption specifically to match the pace of innovation AI-at-scale actually requires, since AI initiatives layered onto genuinely outdated infrastructure tend to underperform regardless of how sophisticated the AI itself is. Data strategy and interoperability means focusing specifically on interoperability across cloud, SaaS, and on-premises data platforms, using AI itself to improve data quality and break down the silos that would otherwise undermine model accuracy and performance — a data foundation that has to be actively engineered, not simply assumed to already exist. Agentic AI and automation means deliberately reducing manual task burden so that human teams can redirect their attention toward genuinely strategic work rather than routine execution. And culture and talent strategy means investing directly in employee upskilling and fostering what CGI calls an "integrate AI everywhere" mentality — explicitly framing AI as augmentation that enhances how people work and enables more creative tasks, rather than as a threat to job security, since organizations that fail to make this framing convincing risk exactly the attrition and talent-competition problems that undermine scaling efforts from the inside. With this foundation genuinely in place, CGI argues commercial organizations gain the ability to deploy AI quickly across the enterprise and respond faster to market change, while public-sector entities specifically gain faster response times to citizen service requests and stronger cross-agency collaboration. A final, often-underweighted element of CGI's framework is partnership: organizations need technology partners who genuinely share their strategic vision and stay contractually engaged through long-term service delivery and support, not merely through initial deployment — since an experienced partner with real industry expertise is what actually helps align people, process, and technology toward the return on investment scaling AI is supposed to deliver, turning AI from a promising but unrealized technology into a genuine, trusted engine of business growth. <span class="src">Scaling AI to Deliver Tangible Business Outcomes</span>

<span class="src">How CIOs Turn AI Pressure Into Enterprise AI That Scales</span> (KPMG, 2026) diagnoses why the pilot-to-production gap this concept's other two sources describe abstractly opens up in practice, and supplies a five-priority CIO roadmap for closing it. KPMG's central reframe is that the barrier to AI progress is rarely failed experimentation — it is enterprise readiness: a pilot runs on carefully curated data with minimal oversight, but production AI must interact with a federated, silo-laden data landscape, dozens of legacy and modern applications each with their own API and security model, and a governance web (GDPR/CCPA compliance, model risk management, bias detection, explainability) that the original pilot architecture was never designed to satisfy — in KPMG's words, "the pilot didn't fail. The enterprise environment simply wasn't designed to support it." KPMG's diagnostic for whether an organization's structural conditions are actually in place asks five practical questions: can the organization explain where AI costs and value sit across the business; do successful pilots have a defined path to enterprise deployment; are AI tools changing workflows and decisions or merely being deployed; could the AI governance model be explained clearly to a regulator tomorrow; and could the architecture adapt to shifting infrastructure conditions without major redesign. KPMG's five CIO priorities, meant to be pursued simultaneously rather than sequentially since each reinforces the others, are: align technology strategy with enterprise priorities first, defining a foundational operating model (including a central AI Center of Excellence for governance) so individual business-unit AI requests don't fragment the technology environment tool by tool; build a credible investment narrative that connects AI to decision speed, customer responsiveness, and resilience rather than a narrow cost-reduction ROI frame, using tech-asset management and FinOps discipline to make AI spend forecastable and "self-funding"; strengthen architecture and data foundations by replacing project-based data prep with a scalable data fabric or mesh with clear ownership, since infrastructure fragmentation (not model quality) is the most common barrier to enterprise scale; turn adoption into an operating model rather than a rollout exercise, delegating responsibility for AI-driven process redesign to business-unit leaders (with IT acting as enabler, not owner) and building genuine executive AI fluency so the C-suite can sponsor the effort credibly; and strengthen operational resilience by improving visibility into system dependencies and operationalizing governance (vendor relationships, data residency, permissions) before economic, regulatory, or geopolitical disruption forces the issue. KPMG's closing framing directly echoes this concept's own MIT CISR finding that maturity — not experimentation volume — is what separates outperformers: "organizations that achieve this balance don't necessarily run more AI experiments than their peers. They simply become the organizations where AI works." <span class="src">How CIOs Turn AI Pressure Into Enterprise AI That Scales</span>

<span class="src">IT Spending Pulse AI Takes Priority as Confidence Returns</span> (BCG, September 2026) supplies fresh market-level survey evidence directly corroborating this concept's central claim that AI maturity correlates with financial performance, drawn from BCG's ongoing IT Spending Pulse tracker. The clearest signal is budget concentration: IT leaders now expect 2026 spending to grow 5.8% year over year — the strongest outlook the survey has recorded, and above pre-tariff-shock 2025 expectations — but that growth is narrowly directed: 66% of buyers plan to increase AI/ML spending (up 21 points from mid-2025) even as they cut nearly every other category, including CRM and ERP (both turned negative again) and IT services (down 19 points in sentiment, reflecting reprioritized budget growth rather than actual reshoring of outsourced work). AI is also the only product category where buyers plan to expand rather than consolidate their vendor roster (a net 34% planning to add providers, versus net consolidation everywhere else), and buyers are simultaneously narrowing which GenAI use cases they pursue — concentrating on a smaller set of high-impact areas (analytics, customer service, security, internal communications) even as adoption of those priority use cases climbs, a discipline BCG frames as the market moving "from experimentation to disciplined execution." That discipline shows up directly in the maturity-performance link this concept's MIT CISR source establishes: weighted-average measured ROI on GenAI/agent deployments has risen to 13.8% overall, but high-AI-maturity firms report roughly 19% ROI versus just 8-9% for little-to-no- and low-maturity adopters — a gap BCG attributes not simply to spending more, but to superior visibility into AI and token costs, which counterintuitively leads mature firms toward more permissive, less-throttled token usage policies (since their top 10% of AI users generate outsized productivity without a proportionate share of spend), while less-mature firms lacking that visibility default to harder usage limits and more cautious, tactical cost management. <span class="src">IT Spending Pulse AI Takes Priority as Confidence Returns</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source specifies how long organizations typically spend in each of the four MIT CISR maturity stages, or whether stages can be skipped by companies starting from a strong existing data/cloud foundation.
- KPMG's five priorities and the BCG spending survey are not reconciled with each other: it's unclear whether BCG's high-maturity firms (19% ROI) are the same organizations that have implemented KPMG's specific five-priority roadmap, or whether "maturity" is being measured on different underlying bases across the two sources.
