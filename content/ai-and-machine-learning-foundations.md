## Summary

Artificial intelligence, machine learning and deep learning are nested fields in which machines learn patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules, and deep learning became practical through layered neural networks, abundant digital data and GPU computing. These capabilities moved from research to production in systems such as Google Translate and to business use as a general-purpose technology that overcomes the limits of tacit knowledge but brings risks of bias, low interpretability and hard-to-diagnose errors. Foundation models trained at scale on broad data and built on the Transformer architecture, with its attention mechanisms, now underlie most generative AI, offering leverage through homogenization and emergent capability while concentrating shared defects and leaving open how they work and when they fail.

## Concept

Artificial intelligence, machine learning and deep learning are often used interchangeably but nest as progressively narrower specializations. AI is the decades-old goal of building machines that perform tasks normally requiring human cognition, such as perceiving, reasoning, learning and problem-solving. Machine learning is the technique that now underlies most AI, first defined by Arthur Samuel in the 1950s as the field of study that gives computers the ability to learn without explicitly being programmed, in which instead of a programmer writing a rule for every case, the approach sometimes called software 1.0, the system is given data and learns its own rules from the patterns in it. This matters for tasks such as recognizing a face, which humans do effortlessly but cannot easily reduce to a hand-written algorithm, because the machine in effect programs itself from examples. Deep learning is a further specialization that uses neural networks with many layers to process complex, unstructured data such as text, images and audio with less human intervention and often greater accuracy than traditional machine learning, at the cost of much more data and computing power. <span class="src">What Is AI (Artificial Intelligence)</span> <span class="src">Machine learning, explained</span> <span class="src">What Is Deep Learning</span>

Machine learning systems can be classed by purpose, as descriptive, explaining what happened, predictive, forecasting what will happen, or prescriptive, recommending what to do, and by how they are trained. Supervised learning trains on data that humans have labeled, such as thousands of images tagged as dog or not dog, so that the model replicates the labeling on new examples, and it is the most common approach in production. Unsupervised learning gives the model unlabeled data and lets it surface patterns that were not specified, for example unexpected customer segments, and reinforcement learning trains by trial and error against a reward signal, which is the approach behind game-playing and autonomous driving systems. A short glossary supports the vocabulary, with an algorithm as the rules that tell a machine what to do with input data, a hallucination as confidently presented fabricated or inaccurate output, natural language processing as machines analyzing and generating human language, a neural network as interconnected processing nodes loosely modeled on brain neurons and a token as the sub-word unit of text that a language model processes and generates. <span class="src">Machine learning, explained</span> <span class="src">Artificial Intelligence, Explained</span>

Within deep learning, three architectures suit different problem shapes. Feed-forward networks move information one way from input to output and suit judging a single instance on its own features, such as flagging one transaction as possibly fraudulent. Convolutional neural networks mirror the organization of the brain's visual cortex and suit perceptual tasks such as identifying disease in a medical scan or detecting a company's logo in a social media post. Recurrent neural networks include backward-looping connections that let them weigh a sequence of inputs against one another, which suits detecting fraud by comparing a transaction with an individual's entire spending history or predicting how text or speech will continue. <span class="src">What Is Machine Learning</span>

Three developments converged in roughly the last decade to make deep learning viable. Geoffrey Hinton and colleagues at the University of Toronto developed a layered self-training method in which an early layer learns simple features such as edges from massive exposure to raw data and then feeds its output to the next layer, which learns more complex features such as a nose or an ear, and so on, so that deep refers to the number of stacked layers and not to depth of understanding. Digitization supplied the volume of data required, since, unlike a child who learns a concept from a few examples, a deep learning system is a brute-force pattern detector that needs millions of examples, such as 19 million cat pictures before statistically significant cat features emerge. Researchers also repurposed GPUs, built for the parallel rendering demands of video games, to run the millions of simultaneous connections in a neural network, compressing weeks of training into a day. <span class="src">Deep Learning Will Radically Change the Ways We Interact With Technology</span>

Google's 2016 overhaul of Google Translate shows the three developments converging in production. The Google Brain team replaced the decade-old phrase-based statistical system, which translated piece by piece with limited awareness of context, with Google Neural Machine Translation, a deep network trained end to end on Google's accumulated corpus of translated text and run on custom Tensor Processing Units, and the jump in quality was large enough that some bilingual reviewers described the output as approaching human-level competence almost overnight, evidence that deep learning was shifting from research curiosity to infrastructure able to reinvent consumer products at global scale. In the same period deep learning moved beyond elite teams, as accessible frameworks and pretrained models let a wider range of engineers experiment with techniques that once needed specialist expertise, a democratization compared with earlier shifts in software development, and Google's Jeff Dean told a conference that if you are not using deep learning already, you should be. <span class="src">The Great A.I. Awakening</span> <span class="src">Machine Learning Is No Longer Just for Experts</span>

Brynjolfsson and McAfee's 2017 HBR article gives an early, influential account of the business significance. It places machine learning among the general-purpose technologies, the steam engine, electricity and the internal combustion engine, each of which catalyzed waves of complementary innovation across unrelated industries, so that the combustion engine eventually produced not just cars but big-box retailers, cross-docking warehouses and suburbs. The central claim is that machine learning succeeds because it overcomes Polanyi's Paradox, the 1964 observation by the philosopher Michael Polanyi that humans know more than they can tell, which had blocked automating any task resting on tacit knowledge such as recognizing a face or riding a bike, since supervised learning derives a mapping from labeled examples, such as animal pictures to species or sound waveforms to words, instead of requiring an explicit rule. Early business impact was concrete, as JPMorgan Chase's system for reviewing commercial loan contracts reduced work that took loan officers 360,000 hours to a few seconds, Infinite Analytics' ad-placement system tripled advertising return for a global consumer packaged goods company and drove USD 125 million in annual revenue for a Brazilian online retailer's search and discovery, and Udacity's chat-room sales support system, trained on ordinary chat logs treated as labeled data, raised salesperson effectiveness by 54% after 1,000 training cycles. <span class="src">The Business of Artificial Intelligence</span>

The article also names three risks that machine learning adds relative to explicit-rule software. Hidden bias can be inherited from historical data, as a hiring model can perpetuate past recruiters' demographic biases without any biased rule being written. Interpretability is low because neural networks deal in statistical rather than literal truths, which makes it hard to prove that a system will behave correctly outside its training data, and errors are hard to diagnose because the structure producing a wrong answer can be too complex to unpack. On where machine learning changes work, it operates at three nested levels, tasks and occupations, as machine vision frees radiologists to focus on critical cases and patient communication, business processes, as Amazon redesigned fulfillment-center workflow and layout around robots and optimization algorithms, and business models, as music moved from selling individual songs to a personalized subscription station, and the most effective division of labor usually automates one or two steps of a longer process rather than an entire job, since designing new combinations of technology, human skill and capital to meet customer needs remains a task machines do poorly. <span class="src">The Business of Artificial Intelligence</span>

Foundation models sit at the top of this stack. Stanford's Center for Research on Foundation Models coined the term in a 2021 report to describe a paradigm shift already underway, in which models such as BERT, DALL-E and GPT-3, trained on broad data at scale, typically through self-supervision, are adapted through techniques such as fine-tuning to many downstream tasks instead of being built for one narrow purpose. The term captures a duality, since these models are critically central to the applications built on them yet incomplete by themselves, requiring adaptation before they become any particular useful application. Technically they are deep learning models built on the transformer architecture and trained on vast unstructured, unlabeled data, usable out of the box or adaptable through fine-tuning, a much shorter and cheaper training pass on a smaller labeled data set, and large language models are the subclass trained and optimized to process and generate natural-language text, whose mechanics and limits are treated in [LLM Mechanics, Limitations and Scaling](#/concept/llm-mechanics-limitations-and-scaling). <span class="src">On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models</span> <span class="src">What Is Deep Learning</span>

The report's central technical observation is that foundation models, though built from deep learning and transfer-learning techniques that predate them, show emergent capabilities once trained at sufficient scale, capabilities absent from smaller versions of the same architecture and not readily predictable from the training process. GPT-3, with 175 billion parameters, can be adapted by natural-language prompts alone to perform passably on tasks it was never trained to do. Because the same model works across many tasks, there is a strong incentive toward homogenization, building many applications on one foundation model rather than training bespoke models, which gives leverage but means that any defect, bias or failure mode of the foundation model propagates to every application built on it. The report stresses that despite rapid deployment there is no clear understanding of how these models work internally, when or why they fail or what they can ultimately do, a genuine knowledge gap because their most significant capabilities emerge unpredictably from scale rather than from a traceable design choice. <span class="src">On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models</span>

The report groups the capabilities behind foundation-model applications into modalities, with language and vision the most mature, effects on the physical world through robotics, reasoning and interaction with humans, each needing its own technical innovation because raw capability does not automatically make a model useful in a specific domain. Language and vision lead because they have abundant raw data and cheap virtual environments in which to test and deploy, whereas robotics remains anchored to the physical world without either, and the report suggests generic, environment-agnostic video of humans performing tasks as a promising data source for robotics foundation models. Across domains, foundation models suit healthcare and biomedicine, where expert knowledge is scarce and costly, multimodal data of images, text and molecules is abundant and sample-efficient adaptation reduces expert time, law, where ample documents exist and generation suits many core tasks, although models must improve substantially before they can reason across sources to produce truthful, coherent long documents and not merely plausible ones, and education, where open-ended generation, such as creating practice problems, and interactive feedback to teachers promise adaptive, personalized learning if models improve at reasoning about student cognition and aligning with learning goals. Because research was then still concentrated in computer science and the tech industry, the report expects that the effects of homogenization, emergence and incomplete understanding will become more pervasive as adoption spreads. <span class="src">On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models</span>

The Transformer, introduced in Google's 2017 paper Attention Is All You Need, replaced the sequential processing of earlier language architectures with a design built entirely on an attention mechanism that processes the entire input at once instead of one token at a time. Parallelism makes training and inference faster and, more importantly for output quality, lets the model learn long-range dependencies between distant words, because attention connects any position to any other directly and information does not decay as it is passed step by step. The architecture has two paired components, an encoder that converts input text into a sequence of hidden states capturing its meaning and a decoder that generates output one word at a time and can attend to the encoder's hidden states at any position instead of a fixed nearby context, which lets it handle long-range grammatical and semantic dependencies, such as translating a pronoun whose referent appeared many words earlier. <span class="src">Understanding Google’s “Attention Is All You Need” Paper and Its Groundbreaking Impact</span>

Two mechanisms make attention computable. Scaled dot-product attention takes a query matrix representing what the model is looking for, a key matrix representing what is available to match and a value matrix holding the content to retrieve, computes attention weights from the dot product, a similarity measure, between queries and keys, scaled down to keep the weights from growing too large as vector dimensionality rises, and outputs a weighted sum of the values, which in effect selects and blends the most relevant information. Multi-head attention runs several such computations in parallel, each with its own learned query, key and value matrices called a head, so the model can attend to different parts and aspects of the input and capture a richer set of relationships, with the outputs concatenated into one combined representation. Self-attention is the configuration within the encoder and decoder in which queries, keys and values all come from the same sequence, so that a sequence is related to itself, which is how the model learns in the sentence I love my dog that love relates to both I and dog regardless of distance, information the decoder then uses in generating output such as a translation. <span class="src">Understanding Google’s “Attention Is All You Need” Paper and Its Groundbreaking Impact</span>

The paper is foundational for the current generative AI landscape, since the Transformer underlies both major branches of later large language models, encoder-oriented models such as BERT and decoder-oriented generative models such as GPT-3 and its successors, and it already powered production systems including Google Translate, which covers more than 100 languages, Facebook's assistant M and Microsoft's Bing search engine. <span class="src">Understanding Google’s “Attention Is All You Need” Paper and Its Groundbreaking Impact</span>

- None of these sources addresses training-data copyright and provenance debates in detail.
- The business-impact figures in the 2017 HBR article, for JPMorgan, Infinite Analytics and Udacity, are nearly a decade old and not updated by any other source, so it is not established whether those systems remained in production or were superseded by deep-learning-native approaches. <span class="src">The Business of Artificial Intelligence</span>
- The foundation-model report dates from 2021 and predates mainstream deployment of ChatGPT and the generative AI wave that followed, so the wiki does not show whether or how its concerns about homogenization and emergent capability were validated or revised. <span class="src">On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models</span>
