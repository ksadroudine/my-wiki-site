## Summary

Cloud computing is the shared, standardized and automated delivery of computing resources as a service, and it rests on a small set of foundations that business leaders need to understand. These include three nested service models (infrastructure, platform and software as a service), the public, private, hybrid, multicloud, community and government deployment models, the microservice and container architecture that defines a cloud-native application, and the standards that make interoperability and portability possible. Together they explain why hybrid environments have become the practical default and why how an application is built matters more than where it runs.

## Concept

Cloud computing is best defined functionally, as the shared, standardized and automated delivery of computing resources (applications, compute, storage, networking, development platforms and even business processes) as a service. Standardization gives consumers consistent interfaces, and automation provisions and releases resources according to business rules, resource availability and security demands; without both, a cloud would be merely remotely hosted infrastructure. Multi-tenancy is the architectural pattern that makes the economics work, because many customers share the same underlying infrastructure and software instance while their data and configuration remain isolated, so the provider avoids replicating dedicated hardware or software for each of them. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.1 Understanding the Cloud</span>

Delivery is organized in three layers, each built on the one below. Infrastructure as a service supplies a virtual server equivalent to a physical one, including an operating system, in public (pay-as-you-go, self-service) and private (policy-gated) forms. Platform as a service adds abstracted middleware, development tooling and deployment services so that the operating system is hidden from developers. Software as a service is a complete multi-tenant business application, such as Salesforce, Google's G Suite or Adobe Creative Cloud, where the customer cares only about functionality, performance, availability and security. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.1 Understanding the Cloud</span>

Software as a service changes both the architecture and the economics of software. Because every customer of a multi-tenant application shares one code base, customers avoid the capital expense of scaling their own infrastructure, maintenance and updates are bundled into the subscription, and a business can configure the application's behavior without touching the underlying code, which keeps upgrades simple, although heavy process customization makes them harder. A single technical fault can affect every customer at once, so vendors have an unusually strong commercial reason to invest in uptime and continuity. Salesforce.com illustrates how such a business grows. Marc Benioff's "no software" pitch won small businesses first, because the absence of capital expense and the month-to-month commitment made switching low-risk, and large enterprises followed more slowly, often buying through sales and marketing budgets rather than formal IT procurement. Salesforce then opened its platform, branded Force.com and later Customer 360 Platform, to independent software vendors, which let partners such as Veeva Systems build a pharmaceutical CRM with built-in regulatory reporting on its infrastructure and customer base. In effect, a SaaS vendor that opens its platform is offering a PaaS, which gives partners faster time to market and access to qualified customers (the source's analogy is a food court) in exchange for dependency risk if the vendor changes its APIs, so vetting API stability is essential due diligence. Delivering the expectation of unlimited resources also requires genuinely cloud-native architecture rather than a lifted legacy application, and the requirement that SaaS stay available while features are deployed is what drives continuous deployment and DevOps. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.9 Using Software as a Service</span>

Platform as a service is defined by what it removes from the developer's concern. The platform supplies networking, file systems and input and output as abstracted services, so applications need no operating-system expertise and port more easily between platforms than infrastructure-as-a-service applications port between operating systems. Organizations must also decide whether to manage their own PaaS environment, which gives more control and more operational burden, or to use a vendor-managed one. PaaS is tightly coupled with two practice shifts: DevOps, which merges development and operations so that customer-facing features ship as soon as they are ready instead of in numbered releases, and agile development, in which small cross-functional teams work in sprints of two to four weeks, ideally staffed with full-stack developers, so that some teams deliver a feature in two or three days. Three variants serve different needs. Public PaaS (AWS Elastic Beanstalk, Azure App Service, Red Hat OpenShift, Google App Engine) is the fastest route to deployment and the largest scale, private PaaS runs inside a company's data center for regulated industries whose auditors need in-person access or whose data must stay in a particular country, and open PaaS is built on open-source software to avoid single-vendor lock-in and support multicloud strategies. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.10 Standing on Platform as a Service</span>

The deployment models differ mainly in ownership, access and control rather than technology. A public cloud is owned and operated by a third party for use by others, and some customers pay a premium for dedicated, non-multi-tenant instances when governance rules forbid sharing infrastructure. A private cloud is owned and operated by or exclusively for one organization, sits behind a firewall and is built around governance, security and compliance, and public vendors increasingly sell appliance versions of their services (AWS Outposts is the example) that can be installed in a customer's own data center, which blurs the line between private cloud and public technology run locally. Public clouds subdivide into commercial clouds backed by service level agreements (generally written to protect the vendor, with carve-outs for the outages that cause many real incidents), open social community clouds such as Facebook and LinkedIn (low security, no SLA and no guarantee the community will persist), open technical community clouds such as GitHub, and government clouds, which are segregated variants meeting stricter isolation and vetting requirements because a government data breach can become an international incident. Private clouds subdivide into privately owned and managed clouds (a financial services firm acting as its own cloud provider to partner banks), vendor appliances behind the customer's firewall, and commercial contractual private clouds run by a third party under a formal SLA. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.7 Introducing All Types of Clouds</span>

The terms hybrid and multicloud are often used loosely, but the source draws a precise line. A hybrid cloud requires that private and public resources work together toward a shared goal, for example a public development platform sending data to a private application or a SaaS analytics service ingesting data from several clouds, while a disconnected public-cloud prototype, a SaaS application with no data flow back to the data center, or divisions that each standardized on their own single public cloud are explicitly not hybrid. Multicloud is a strategic pattern rather than a cloud type, namely the coordinated use of two or more public clouds, motivated by developer choice, vendor-risk diversification, cost and performance arbitrage and resilience against any one vendor's pricing or reliability. Its endpoint, automatic movement of running workloads between providers by policy, was still unrealized because multicloud management tooling and application portability were immature, and in practice multicloud usually arises accidentally when teams or acquisitions choose different vendors. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Cloud adoption does not remove the data center. Most medium and large companies still run one for systems of record such as accounting and inventory, and virtualization, which decouples software from hardware, has been the main lever for streamlining it. Many organizations deliberately evolve the virtualized data center into a private cloud as a staging area for cleaning up virtual-machine sprawl and making applications cloud-ready, and after migrating to the public cloud the modernized private cloud becomes the foundation of a hybrid environment. Data sovereignty, legacy applications that are not cloud-ready and workloads with no business case for migration keep other systems in place. Overprovisioning is a further cost driver in traditional data centers, which are commonly sized well beyond actual peak demand as a hedge against outages, inflating energy costs relative to the compute used, an inefficiency that elastic, on-demand allocation is designed to remove. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Market evidence explains why hybrid has become the practical default. Bain's 2019 analysis found public cloud had pulled decisively ahead, but the dominance reflected trade-offs more than preference, since many enterprises would have preferred a private solution and chose public cloud because it was cheaper, richer in features and easier to integrate than private alternatives, which are often too costly or hard to manage internally. Private cloud vendors therefore now compete by emphasizing integration with public services, even for customers in highly regulated industries. Security plays a dual role, remaining a top-cited worry while also motivating adoption among executives who believe providers protect against breaches and privacy failures better than their own operations could. Disappointments persist unevenly: cost is the largest source of dissatisfaction, because expected savings often fail to materialize, and dissatisfaction with compliance and regulatory issues rose roughly 50 percent between 2016 and 2018 even as satisfaction with security and reliability improved, which shows the parts of the value proposition maturing at different rates. <span class="src">Public vs. Private Cloud The Market Says Hybrid</span>

McKinsey's 2025 account of the SaaS market adds its current economics. The global market reached roughly $3 trillion by 2022 after nearly a decade in which software market capitalization grew at twice the pace of the broader market, but growth slowed as enterprises cut IT spending in response to higher interest rates, pushing boards to ask what the best use of capital is and leading many companies to adopt the Rule of 40, under which growth rate plus free cash flow rate should reach at least 40 percent. Generative AI has disrupted software faster than SaaS did, with large enterprises spending roughly $15 billion on it in 2023 and reaching about 2% of the enterprise software market in a year, a share SaaS took four years to reach. McKinsey expects generative AI to make customers switch vendors more often as they chase new capabilities, so SaaS companies must keep pace with development and defend client relationships rather than assume renewal, and it advises leaders to reimagine their category rather than add AI features, to reallocate R&D in line with a 10 to 20 percent AI revenue contribution within five years, and to revisit pricing, packaging and proprietary-data strategy. Product-led growth, which puts the product rather than a salesperson at the center of acquisition and expansion, is rarely successful alone, and the more durable pattern is product-led sales, which layers it onto sales-led growth so that incumbents can reach smaller customers cheaply while larger prospects still experience the product before signing a large contract. Only 6% of global software revenue is earned by non-technology companies despite widespread ambition, and McKinsey identifies six success factors for building a new SaaS business inside such a company: iterate quickly with a minimum viable product, exploit existing proprietary data and digital assets (Anheuser-Busch InBev built a B2B e-commerce platform for retailers from its own data), plan for scale from day one, recruit scarce SaaS talent from wider pools such as open-source communities and hackathons, tie pricing and selling to product-usage data, and protect funding through the period before results appear. <span class="src">What is SaaS</span>

Cloud-native architecture builds applications as collections of small, independently deployable microservices that communicate only through standardized APIs. Each microservice serves one narrow function and is developed and deployed by a small team, which simplifies development, makes changes smaller and less risky, lets individual services scale up or down on their own demand, and lets other teams reuse a fully tested service without revalidating it. This is why lifting and shifting an aging monolith captures almost none of the cloud's advantage: the application keeps the same tangled dependencies it had on premises, and the benefit comes from decomposition rather than relocation. Containers make microservices practical. A virtual machine abstracts the physical server, with each machine running its own full operating system, while a container abstracts the operating system itself by packaging application code with its dependencies, so it runs predictably across environments and is far smaller and faster to start. Because one application may consist of hundreds or thousands of containers, orchestration platforms automate installation, scaling, health checks, versioning and rolling updates across a cluster. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.6 A Closer Look at Cloud Services</span>

Kubernetes, created by Google engineers and open-sourced in 2014, outcompeted alternatives such as Docker Swarm, Apache Mesos and Amazon ECS on functionality, a large open-source ecosystem (Prometheus for monitoring, Istio for service meshing) and portability across public clouds, private clouds and on-premises infrastructure, which directly limits vendor lock-in. The Cloud Native Computing Foundation, founded in 2015 under the Linux Foundation, defines cloud native by three properties, namely container-packaged, dynamically managed and microservices-oriented, and its framing makes clear that a cloud-native application is defined by how it is built rather than where it runs, because containerization removes dependence on specific hardware or operating systems. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Cloud standards matter because without them a multicloud environment presents a different interface for every equivalent service, making integration, workload movement and vendor negotiation costly. They form through four routes with different trade-offs between speed and legitimacy. Multinational bodies such as ISO, with over 17,500 standards, are legally grounded but slow and sometimes driven by diplomats. Industry consortiums such as W3C, OASIS, the Apache Group and the Open Group are more streamlined and work even among commercial competitors. Ad hoc groups, often built around open-source projects, move fastest but struggle to resolve contentious decisions, and de facto standards have no governing body at all, becoming standards through sheer extent of use, which is why they often win before formal alternatives catch up. A document functions as a standard, the source argues, only when vendors broadly recognize it, consumers broadly demand it and it is open source, and NIST tracks maturity on a six-stage scale from none to retired. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.5 Standards in a Multicloud World</span>

The goals of cloud standardization are interoperability, portability and security. Kubernetes is the central open-source standard for interoperability, surrounded by Istio for secure service-to-service connections, Calico for cross-cloud networking policy and Helm for packaging and deploying applications, so developers can write a service once and deploy it to any cloud running the same stack, and Knative adds a consistent API wrapper for legacy workloads. Two standards were built specifically for cloud interoperability, the Open Cloud Computing Interface from the Open Grid Forum, a REST-based management API that began with infrastructure and expanded to platform and software, and the Cloud Data Management Interface from SNIA, which defines how applications create, retrieve, update and delete cloud data. Without such interfaces, organizations face API proliferation, which drives lock-in and blocks portability. Portability is harder on the data side than on the application side, because data takes more varied forms and volumes and its handling is governed by its owner rather than by a technical specification. Application and virtual-machine portability rely on the Open Virtualization Format and increasingly on containers, while data portability relies on CDMI and on IEEE P2301, a guide to cloud portability and interoperability profiles that was still under development. Security standards cluster into four areas with named, adopted specifications: authentication and authorization (X.509-based public key standards), monitoring and incident response (NIST SP 800-61), data confidentiality, integrity and availability (KMIP and FIPS 186-3), and security policy management (FIPS 200). <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.5 Standards in a Multicloud World</span>

Many organizations contribute to this work from different angles. The Cloud Security Alliance, founded in 2008, is not a formal standards body but promotes security practice through training and runs STAR, a free public registry in which vendors document their security controls as a form of self-regulation. The Distributed Management Task Force is known for its Common Information Model and runs a Cloud Management Initiative, NIST produced the widely used cloud model definitions and a Cloud Computing Standards Roadmap that inventories standards and gaps, the Cloud Standards Customer Council within the Object Management Group represents customer interoperability requirements, the Open Commons Consortium operates research cloud infrastructure and maintains the Malstone benchmark, the Open Group leads vendor-neutral IT standards and collaborates with the Cloud Security Alliance on secure architecture, and SNIA stewards CDMI. Vertical groups such as the TeleManagement Forum and the Association for Retail Technology Standards extend the work into specific sectors. A complementary layer of open-source foundations supports standardization without issuing specifications, including OASIS, the Apache and Linux Foundations (Linux being the near-universal cloud operating system), the Eclipse Foundation, OpenStack, which Rackspace and NASA originated and over 150 companies now support, and the Open Data Center Alliance. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). ## Open Questions

- The core source was published in 2020 and predates the mainstream rise of AI-native SaaS products built on foundation-model APIs, serverless and edge computing, and the full maturation of Kubernetes. The 2025 McKinsey piece covers market-level disruption from generative AI but does not revisit whether multi-tenancy, platform-as-a-service abstraction or the public, private, hybrid and multicloud taxonomy need updating for those models.
- The standards landscape described, including the Open Cloud Computing Interface, the Cloud Data Management Interface and IEEE P2301, is presented as still emerging, and the sources do not say how adoption of those standards has evolved since.
- The McKinsey guidance on product-led sales and building a new SaaS business is aimed at large enterprises with existing scale and data assets, and the sources do not say how a resource-constrained startup should sequence the six success factors when it cannot pursue all of them at once.
- The sources name service mesh technology such as Istio as part of the Kubernetes ecosystem but do not describe how it works.
