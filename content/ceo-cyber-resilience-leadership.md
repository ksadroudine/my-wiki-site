## Summary

Lessons from 37 CEO interviews on managing cyberattacks, drawn from research comparing executives who led their company through a serious breach against those who hadn't. Covers the five regrets attack-tested CEOs share, and a seven-part playbook for building organizational cyber resilience before an attack happens.

## Concept

Drawing on 37 in-depth interviews with CEOs of large enterprises (averaging $12 billion in revenue) across the US, Europe, and Asia — nine of whom had personally led their organization through a serious cyberattack — this research isolates what changes in a CEO's thinking once an attack has actually happened, by comparing their battle-tested views against those of CEOs who hadn't yet faced one. The clearest and most common regret among attack-tested CEOs was having overemphasized cybersecurity prevention at the expense of organizational resilience: these executives had focused on *whether* an attack would occur rather than *when* it would occur and how the organization would respond, pouring significant resources into technological defenses while lacking basic organizational resilience practices across departments. Cyber resilience, as the CEOs came to understand it only after being breached, is not about avoiding an attack but about an organization's capacity to anticipate, withstand, respond to, and adapt to one — minimizing impact, recovering quickly, and emerging stronger, since even the most technologically advanced organizations will eventually be breached and need to plan for that inevitability. <span class="src">The CEO’s Cyber Resilience Playbook</span>

Four further regrets rounded out the pattern. CEOs who had lived through an attack regretted feeling merely *accountable* for cybersecurity — taking ownership only after something went wrong — rather than proactively *responsible* for it beforehand, a distinction that mattered enough that 72 percent of all CEOs interviewed, attacked or not, admitted discomfort making decisions in the cyber domain; several described wanting to become "co-responsible" alongside their CISO, a shift CISOs in the same research welcomed since they had generally viewed their CEOs as insufficiently engaged. Related to this, CEOs also regretted blindly trusting their cybersecurity and technology teams without building enough of their own understanding to ask informed questions or challenge advice — a gap that widened after an attack forced many CEOs to leave the company's fate in the hands of experts whose reasoning they couldn't fully evaluate; the research found that the more CEOs came to understand cyber resilience, the more concerned they became, and the more concerned they became, the more engaged they got. Nearly all CEOs without direct attack experience considered their organizations well prepared for one, a confidence that only the attack-tested group recognized as dangerous complacency; even carefully built crisis playbooks, they noted, cannot capture how unpredictably a real attack unfolds, since attackers continually adapt and an incident may compromise the very infrastructure (communication channels, for instance) that a playbook assumes will still be available. Finally, CEOs regretted reacting to the barrage of competing stakeholder demands during a crisis — shareholders on financial impact, the board on recovery evidence, regulators on answers, customers on their data — rather than proactively reassuring those stakeholders, and the research identifies three distinct postures a CEO can take in that reassurance role: an amplifier who reinforces external pressure to maintain urgency, a filter who judges which pressures to pass through to the response team versus absorb, and an absorber who takes on stakeholder pressure directly so it doesn't distract the people actually running recovery. <span class="src">The CEO’s Cyber Resilience Playbook</span>

From these regrets, the research distills a seven-practice playbook for CEOs to build resilience before an attack forces the lesson on them. Bringing in an outsider CEO with direct attack experience to brief the leadership team and board provides a relatable wake-up call that pure technologists can't replicate, since it comes from someone who has personally faced the same decisions. Establishing a recurring cyber resilience learning forum — chaired by the CEO, held at least annually and ideally quarterly, deliberately separate from formal risk-governance processes — builds shared understanding between business leaders and cyber experts and normalizes asking naive questions, which moves people toward the kind of informed trust the research found lacking. Commissioning an independent cyber resilience audit annually, treated with the same seriousness as a financial audit, lets the CEO build informed trust with the technical team and surface organizational blind spots the CEO wouldn't otherwise see; the research notes CISOs sometimes hesitate to propose this out of fear of being put on the spot, when in practice it tends to build trust and shared responsibility instead. Identifying the two to five business processes most critical to keeping the organization running — and mapping them to the specific applications and servers that support them — needs to happen before an attack, since without this groundwork every department will insist its own processes are the ones that must be restored first, and decentralized decision-making under crisis needs pre-agreed priorities to avoid infighting. Lining up expert assistance (legal counsel for regulatory compliance, communications specialists, forensic investigators, and in the worst case ransom negotiators) in advance, and preparing a proactive crisis communications plan rather than going dark during an incident, together prevent the CEO from having to build critical relationships and messaging for the first time under active crisis pressure — markets and regulators are notably less forgiving of organizations that appear to have been caught flat-footed or seem to be hiding something. Finally, conducting a full postmortem after any real attack — covering organizational resilience factors like communication and business continuity, not just technical root cause — often exposes outdated processes and inefficiencies that had gone unnoticed, and can become a genuine source of value: one CEO redirected savings from post-attack technology consolidation directly into further resilience investment, while other CEOs described the crisis creating new appreciation for previously invisible critical processes and opportunities to strengthen the organization more broadly, including through sharing lessons with peers and even competitors, since cyber resilience is a domain the research frames as fundamentally noncompetitive. <span class="src">The CEO’s Cyber Resilience Playbook</span>

## Open Questions

- The research doesn't report whether the seven-practice playbook was tested prospectively (i.e., whether organizations that adopted it before an attack fared measurably better), since the underlying interviews are retrospective accounts from CEOs describing what they wish they had done.
