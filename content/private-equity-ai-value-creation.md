## Summary

Private equity firms can build AI capability across many portfolio companies at once, as Apollo Global Management's playbook shows, through pre-investment AI risk assessment, post-acquisition use-case implementation, a startup incubator and cross-portfolio procurement AI. From the acquirer's side, AI readiness has become the key due-diligence lens for buying undervalued IT-services companies and for software buyouts, which face slower growth and need new diligence metrics.

## Concept

<span class="src">Building AI Capabilities Into Portfolio Companies at Apollo</span> (MIT SMR, June 2025) profiles Apollo Global Management's systematic approach to building AI capability across its private equity portfolio, arguing that private equity investors' seriousness about AI is itself a signal of the technology's genuine near-term value-creation potential, since PE firms are unusually disciplined about measuring realized value from every dollar invested. Apollo built a dedicated portfolio operations team roughly five years before this account, driven by partner and co-head of equity David Sambur's conviction that digital capability-building needed greater emphasis across the portfolio; in mid-2021 the firm hired Vikram Mahidhar — previously head of Genpact's AI practice, part of the management team at an AI startup Genpact acquired, and an early AI consultant at Deloitte — as an operating partner to lead what became Apollo's Data, Digital, and AI team, later adding a second senior AI executive, Katia Walsh, as the mandate expanded. Mahidhar's team identified a set of cross-portfolio "value pools" — product engineering, cloud transformation, sales and marketing, finance, AI, operations, and customer care transformation — with AI quickly emerging as the most critical pool since it functions as an enabler for most of the others. Apollo's process for a given company begins before an actual investment decision: an industry- and target-specific assessment of how AI is affecting the business and where that impact is headed over the coming years, which filters out high-risk AI applications before acquisition rather than discovering them afterward. This is followed by detailed diligence, often with external consultants, that identifies specific AI use cases within the target company and builds an implementation plan ready to execute the moment the deal closes — a process requiring buy-in from the most senior levels of both Apollo and the acquired company, including genuine commitment to investing in talent as well as tooling. Rather than making portfolio companies permanently dependent on Apollo's central team, the explicit goal is self-sufficiency: Apollo supplies recommended tools, vendors, potential senior hires, and consultants as needed, with the expectation that each portfolio company eventually runs its own AI capability independently. To supply its portfolio with leading-edge AI tools it might not otherwise access, Apollo Funds partnered with venture capital firm 25madison to launch an AI-native enterprise-software incubator, 25m Evolve, in 2024, specifically to nurture B2B AI startups that could become suppliers to Apollo's own portfolio companies as well as viable standalone software businesses serving other customers; the incubator had grown to 11 companies spanning supply-chain resiliency, manufacturing response time, and reputation management by the time of this account. <span class="src">Building AI Capabilities Into Portfolio Companies at Apollo</span>

Four named portfolio-company case studies illustrate the playbook's results in practice. Educational publisher Cengage — where Mahidhar himself serves on the board — is running eight simultaneous AI projects spanning sales enablement, customer care, content production, sales automation, and new product development, with measured results including a 40% cost reduction in select content-production processes, 15-20% reduction in lead-generation costs through automation, a 15% reduction in customer-care costs, and a 10-15% reduction in software-development costs, alongside two new AI-powered products (a personalized cybersecurity training tool and a generative-AI tutor bot that scaled to 1 million student users). At Yahoo, engineering productivity has improved more than 20% and is trending higher, with developers accepting over 10,000 lines of AI-generated code daily, while enterprise-wide AI copilots deployed across sales, finance, and HR are separately saving an estimated one hour per user per week. Barnes Group, an aerospace and industrial components maker Apollo Funds acquired in January 2025, used generative AI to identify, de-duplicate, and index a previously hard-to-access trove of technical product-specification documents; service technicians can now get immediate, accurate equipment information instead of waiting on headquarters, a change that produced a 5x return on the AI investment within its first year. At Univar Solutions, a chemical and ingredient distributor acquired in 2023, an AI sales agent re-engages dormant customer accounts on the company's ChemCentral e-commerce platform by analyzing historical purchasing behavior and personalizing outreach, achieving a 30% engagement rate in its initial pilot among customers whose purchasing had been sporadic. Beyond individual portfolio companies, Apollo also runs a centralized, cross-portfolio AI system that analyzes purchasing contracts and invoices across more than 40 portfolio companies simultaneously to determine the best price paid for a given product category — in one instance analyzing 15,000 software purchase agreements in a matter of minutes and helping a single portfolio company achieve a procurement cost reduction exceeding 65% — with the resulting proprietary pricing benchmark now feeding back into Apollo's own diligence process for future acquisitions, turning operational AI use directly into investment-decision infrastructure. <span class="src">Building AI Capabilities Into Portfolio Companies at Apollo</span>

<span class="src">The AI opportunity for private equity in enterprise IT services</span> (Arthur D. Little, September 2026) supplies the acquisition-side counterpart to Apollo's portfolio-operations playbook, arguing that enterprise IT services — having lost more than a third of their value since 2021 amid slowed digital-transformation activity, government IT-spending cuts, and broader economic volatility — represent a market reprioritizing rather than shrinking, and therefore a genuine PE entry point. The firm's central empirical case is asymmetric exposure: unlike standalone AI software vendors, whose valuations have been hit hard and fast by new agentic tools, IT-services companies retain a structural moat, since an inherent need for human-mediated implementation, integration, and support persists even as AI reshapes how that work gets delivered — Gartner projects global AI-services spending will reach $478 billion by 2028 (an 18.2% five-year CAGR), and leading systems integrators using AI-enabled code-migration tools are already cutting application-modernization costs and timelines meaningfully, with AIOps letting managed-services providers take on more scope per engineer and shift toward outcome-based SLAs instead of effort-based contracts. A.D. Little identifies five IT-service lines with the strongest growing client demand: application modernization (AI-assisted code migration and test generation to make legacy portfolios AI-ready); data readiness and governance (catalogs, lineage, and vectorization strategies, since reliable governed data is a precondition for enterprise AI); security and compliance for AI (policies, guardrails, red-teaming, and audit trails, driven by regulatory concern over bias and uncontrolled AI implementations); domain copilots and assistive automation (RAG-powered, role-specific frontline support tools); and AI services management (a supervisory FinOps/incident-triage layer coordinating the operational complexity of multiple AI projects moving from pilot to scale simultaneously) — with sector-specific emphasis varying (financial services prioritizing KYC-driven data readiness, healthcare prioritizing privacy-preserving document automation, industrial and public-sector targets prioritizing application modernization). For target selection, A.D. Little's due-diligence framework evaluates four dimensions that closely parallel Apollo's own pre-investment AI risk assessment: capabilities (referenceable AI use cases with measurable outcomes, defensible proprietary IP, and proven repeatable platforms like modernization "factories"); commercial (what share of revenue is genuinely AI-driven — with an explicit warning to watch for adjacent, non-AI projects relabeled as AI revenue to inflate a target's market position — plus partnership balance to avoid overdependence on a small number of co-sell relationships); operations (AI maturity in both internal administration and project delivery, verifiable re-skilling progress, and how easily the delivery pyramid can be reshaped); and regulatory (data residency, sovereign AI, and AI-ethics compliance readiness, plus exposure to future regulation). A.D. Little's three traditional PE playbooks — aggregate and combine (building scale platforms across archetypes and geographies), carve out selected capabilities from existing businesses, and go private (restructuring publicly listed businesses before re-listing or sale, an especially attractive route given current depressed public valuations) — all remain viable, but the firm's closing argument is that picking winners now requires evaluating AI-readiness explicitly rather than assuming traditional scale or client-relationship advantages will persist unchanged, since the pyramid model of large human teams performing coding, testing, triage, and monitoring is being upended regardless of which acquisition structure a PE firm chooses. <span class="src">The AI opportunity for private equity in enterprise IT services</span>

<span class="src">Software Investing in the Age of AI and Slower Growth</span> (Bain, September 2026) applies private-equity discipline to software portfolios. The article argues that AI erodes the predictability that made software a preferred buyout asset, since compute adds per-query cost, pricing shifts from seats toward usage, and margins fall. Its no-regrets moves are to reorient due diligence, refocus value-creation playbooks on how AI can transform the offering through deeper workflow understanding (zero-basing product assumptions rather than adding incremental features), and build bankable AI proof points for the next owner, since the gap in value expectations between buyers and sellers stems partly from poor measurement. <span class="src">Software Investing in the Age of AI and Slower Growth</span>

## Open Questions

- The article doesn't address how Apollo handles portfolio companies where AI initiatives underperform or fail — all four case studies describe successes, leaving open how the firm's playbook adapts when the pre-investment risk assessment or post-acquisition implementation plan doesn't pan out as expected.
- It's not established how much of Apollo's approach is specific to its scale (a large, diversified asset manager with a dedicated AI team of its own) versus transferable to smaller private equity firms without the resources to build equivalent in-house AI capability or launch a startup incubator.
- Neither source directly addresses whether Apollo's operational playbook (building AI capability inside existing portfolio companies) and A.D. Little's acquisition thesis (buying underpriced IT-services assets specifically for their AI-transformation potential) represent the same investment strategy applied to different asset classes, or two genuinely distinct PE approaches to AI value creation that a single firm might need to run in parallel.
- Bain's analysis of post-pandemic buyout returns covers deals that are mostly unrealised, so the final outcomes may differ from the multiples reported.
