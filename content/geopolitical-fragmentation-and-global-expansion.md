## Summary

The global digital economy is splitting into diverging national and regional paths, shaped as much by US-China rivalry, regulation and access to AI computing capacity as by markets and technology. The 2026 Digital Evolution Index covers 125 countries. It finds that the United States and China together account for just over half of digital GDP and pursue structurally different AI strategies, that global digital momentum has slowed since the pandemic and that digital regulation is diverging by region. Chinese companies facing trade tensions and tighter foreign-investment scrutiny are responding by targeting microregions, meaning provinces, cities and economic zones, instead of entire countries. Their approach, which often favors Singapore for its talent, trade infrastructure and neutrality, offers a company-level playbook for expanding globally despite fragmentation.

## Highlights

- The 2026 Digital Evolution Index sorts 125 countries into four quadrants by digital development level and momentum: "stand outs" (high development, fast momentum — US, China, Singapore, South Korea), "stall outs" (high development, slowing momentum — Finland, Germany, Japan, UK), "break outs" (lower development, fast momentum — India, Vietnam, Brazil), and "watch outs" (low development, weak momentum — Pakistan, Nigeria, Ethiopia).
- The US and China alone account for just over half of the aggregate digital GDP of all 125 countries studied, with the two pursuing structurally different AI strategies: the US emphasizes private-sector investment and an estimated 39.7 million petaflops of AI compute (about half the world's total), while China emphasizes government-industry coordination, algorithmic efficiency under compute constraints, and open-source AI leadership — a gap Stanford HAI reports has "effectively closed" on model performance despite the compute disparity.
- Global digital momentum has slowed sharply since the pandemic ended, from 4.3% annual growth pre-pandemic to 2.4% post-pandemic, with lower-income countries experiencing the greatest deceleration — even as AI investment surges, reinforcing a "winner-takes-most" dynamic where countries already strong in digital infrastructure pull further ahead.
- Digital regulation is diverging by cluster rather than converging: the US minimizes federal AI regulatory hurdles, China follows a "move fast but obey the rules" model set centrally, the EU (concentrated among "stall out" countries) is imposing extensive AI and data-protection rules, and break-out economies are assembling hybrid frameworks borrowing from all three — a fragmentation serious enough that classifying markets by regulatory logic (permissive, precautionary, state-directed, hybrid) may become as strategically important as classifying them by growth potential.
- Facing a slow domestic recovery, Chinese companies have adopted "chu hai" (going overseas) as a rallying strategy, but confront a threefold challenge: rising US-China trade tensions and export controls, tightening foreign-investment regulatory scrutiny in North America and Europe, and infrastructure/cultural unfamiliarity in emerging markets they might otherwise target.
- Rather than targeting entire countries, Chinese executives increasingly target "microregions" — subnational units like provinces, cities, or economic zones (McKinsey identified more than 40,000 globally in 2019, each averaging $3 billion in GDP and 180,000 residents) — a mental model that comes naturally given China's own historically decentralized, place-based development pattern, in which 1.1 billion of the roughly 2 billion people worldwide who reached high living standards in the past 20 years were Chinese.
- Three place-based strategies recur across Chinese companies' international expansion: finding new routes to existing markets by relocating supply chain nodes or entire headquarters to strategically advantageous microregions (Shein relocating its headquarters to Singapore for its talent access, trade infrastructure, and geopolitical neutrality); building hubs in specific cities to reach entire emerging-market regions (Chinese firms clustering in Dubai as a Middle East/North Africa gateway, or Addis Ababa/Accra/Nairobi for Africa); and going virtual through digital talent hubs that connect distributed teams without requiring heavy in-country investment (Shenzhen's electronics brands, Hangzhou's Alibaba-trained e-commerce talent pool).
- Singapore recurs as the single most attractive microregion for Chinese companies preserving Western-market access, for three specific reasons: access to talent, trade infrastructure, and geopolitical neutrality — the same three attributes any company assessing a fragmentation-resilient hub location should weigh regardless of its country of origin.

## Concept

<span class="src">What a Fragmenting Digital Economy Means for Global Competition</span> (HBR, Bhaskar Chakravorti et al., June 2026), reporting on the 2026 Digital Evolution Index (a collaboration between Tufts University's Digital Planet research center and Via Science, tracking 185 indicators across 125 countries), argues that 25 years of digitalization connecting countries, supply chains, and markets through common standards and platforms is giving way to a fragmenting "erosion of a singular global digital economy." The index sorts countries into four quadrants by digital evolution state (current development level) and momentum (compound annual growth rate from 2008-2025): "stand outs" combine high development with fast momentum (the US, China, Singapore, South Korea); "stall outs" have high development but slowing momentum (Finland, Denmark, Germany, Japan, the UK); "break outs" have lower development but fast momentum (India, Vietnam, Brazil, Thailand); and "watch outs" have both low development and weak momentum (Pakistan, Nigeria, Venezuela, Ethiopia). The US and China's combined digital GDP alone represents just over half of the aggregate digital GDP across all 125 countries studied, and their AI rivalry dominates the pace of digital investment globally: the US leans on private-sector investment, foundation-model development, and an estimated computational lead of 39.7 million petaflops (roughly half the world's total AI compute) driven by advanced semiconductor design, while China leans on coordinated government-industry investment, algorithmic efficiency engineered around compute constraints, vast domestic data pools, rapid large-scale data-center construction, and open-source AI leadership — a strategic divergence that, according to Stanford HAI, has nonetheless resulted in the US-China AI model-performance gap "effectively closing" despite China's estimated compute disadvantage (roughly 400,000 petaflops). <span class="src">What a Fragmenting Digital Economy Means for Global Competition</span>

Beyond the two superpowers, "stand out" status also includes smaller open economies succeeding through non-scale strategies: Singapore executing on private-sector strength and government-directed digital transformation while explicitly leveraging ties to both the US and Chinese ecosystems; the UAE pursuing state-led investment toward an autonomous-governance AI hub, though regional war has introduced real economic uncertainty; Estonia pioneering e-government infrastructure (its X-Road secure data-exchange layer and e-Residency program); and Ireland positioning itself as a global tech export and innovation-commercialization hub via EU membership and cultural proximity to the US. "Stall out" countries, concentrated in Europe, have built exceptional digital infrastructure and strong institutions but see innovation speed dampened by their own regulatory investment (data privacy, security, transparency policies) — a "neighborhood effect" that nonetheless lowered the cost of cross-border scaling enough to help smaller Central/Eastern European and Baltic economies become surging stand-out and break-out performers in their own right. "Break out" economies combine lower digital evolution scores with the fastest momentum of any cluster, frequently leapfrogging PC-based development entirely via mobile-first infrastructure paired with digital public payment systems (India's UPI hit 22.64 billion transactions in March 2026 alone; global mobile-money transactions surpassed $2 trillion in 2025, doubling in four years, with most growth in Africa). "Watch out" economies lag on both development and momentum, concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa and fragile Middle Eastern and Central/South Asian states, but paradoxically report the highest trust in AI of any cluster (per UN survey data) — a trust-based adoption mindset that could jumpstart future momentum but currently operates with the fewest institutional guardrails. Several cross-cutting patterns compound the fragmentation: AI is reinforcing a "winner-takes-most" dynamic as already-advanced digital economies pull further ahead; global digital momentum has slowed markedly since the pandemic (from 4.3% annual growth pre-pandemic to 2.4% post-pandemic, with lower-income countries decelerating fastest); and digital regulation is diverging by cluster rather than converging toward a single global standard — the US minimizing federal AI rules, China's centrally-set "move fast but obey the rules" approach, extensive EU AI and data-protection regulation concentrated among stall-out countries, and break-out economies assembling hybrid frameworks that borrow from all three, with the 2026 AI Impact Summit's New Delhi Declaration signaling an emerging "Global South bloc" aspiring to co-author its own global AI governance norms. The article's practical implication for business leaders is that classifying markets by regulatory logic — permissive, precautionary, state-directed, or hybrid — may soon matter as much as classifying them by growth potential, and that AI strategies premised on a single harmonized global technology stack will increasingly misread the actual operating environment. <span class="src">What a Fragmenting Digital Economy Means for Global Competition</span>

<span class="src">How Chinese Companies Expand Globally Despite Headwinds</span> (MIT SMR, Shameen Prashantham and Lola Woetzel, February 2025) supplies a concrete company-level playbook for exactly the kind of fragmented environment the Digital Evolution Index describes, drawn from 40 interviews with Chinese business leaders conducted from early 2023 through early 2024. Facing a slow post-pandemic recovery in China's domestic consumer market, Chinese companies have adopted "chu hai" ("going overseas") as a rallying strategy, but confront a threefold set of headwinds that mirror this concept's macro fragmentation thesis directly: rising US-China trade tensions and export controls (the October 2022 US restriction on AI chip exports to China hit not only Chinese semiconductor firms but also Nvidia's and Broadcom's stock prices); tightening foreign-investment regulatory scrutiny in North America and Western Europe (Chinese-owned seed producer Syngenta was ordered to divest 160 acres of Arkansas farmland under a 2023 foreign-land-ownership law); and genuine infrastructure deficits, bureaucratic inefficiency, and cultural unfamiliarity in the emerging markets that otherwise offer fewer trade barriers. The authors' central finding is that Chinese executives are navigating these headwinds by reconceiving their unit of international strategy: rather than targeting entire countries, they target "microregions" — subnational units such as provinces, cities, or defined economic zones (a 2019 McKinsey study identified more than 40,000 globally, each averaging 3,000 square kilometers, $3 billion in GDP, and 180,000 residents). This mental model comes naturally to Chinese business leaders given China's own historically decentralized, place-based development pattern predating even the 1980s economic reforms: of the roughly 2 billion people worldwide who achieved high living standards over the past 20 years, 1.1 billion did so within Chinese microregions specifically. <span class="src">How Chinese Companies Expand Globally Despite Headwinds</span>

Three place-based strategies recur across the companies studied. Finding new routes to existing markets means relocating supply-chain nodes or entire company headquarters to strategically advantageous microregions rather than tactically dodging trade barriers through loopholes — power-drill maker Chervon established a wholly owned Vietnamese manufacturing facility to diversify its supply chain while still serving Western customers, while fast-fashion retailer Shein relocated its entire headquarters to Singapore specifically to preserve its ability to forge Western partnerships that have become increasingly difficult from within mainland China. Building hubs to reach emerging markets clusters Chinese investment in specific gateway cities — Singapore again for Southeast Asia (Shanghai-based Leyan used a Singapore headquarters to market its AI chatbot to Southeast Asian e-commerce platforms like Shopee and Lazada), Dubai for the Middle East and North Africa, and Addis Ababa, Accra, and Nairobi as African beachheads, despite those cities lagging Singapore on trade infrastructure and bureaucratic efficiency and therefore requiring more reliance on local partners to compensate. Going virtual through digital talent hubs uses distributed digital talent to connect resources and opportunities without the heavier commitment of physical market entry — Shenzhen has produced internationally recognized consumer-electronics brands (Anker Innovations, Govee) that sell through Western retail channels under their own brand identity rather than as generic marketplace listings, while Hangzhou, home to Alibaba's Global Digital Talent training program, has generated a local entrepreneurial talent pool capable of building direct-to-consumer brands for Western markets — in at least one case studied, a Chinese-founded company launched successfully in the US within a couple of years without disclosing its Chinese origin anywhere on its website, on the reasoning (per one founder) that "because the product works so well, where we come from is irrelevant." Singapore recurs across all three strategies as the single most valuable microregion specifically because it combines talent access, trade infrastructure, and geopolitical neutrality — the same three attributes the authors argue any company, regardless of national origin, should weigh when selecting a hub location resilient to the kind of geopolitical fragmentation this concept's other source documents at the macro level. The authors close by noting the strategy generalizes beyond Chinese companies specifically: as protectionist policies spread, any company facing restricted access to a given national market may find a microregion-based, place-first approach — rather than a country-first one — the more durable response. <span class="src">How Chinese Companies Expand Globally Despite Headwinds</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source explains how a Western (non-Chinese) company should adapt the microregion strategy given it may lack the "returnee" talent pool (Chinese professionals who lived and worked abroad before returning home) the second source identifies as helping Chinese companies execute multicultural, multilocational teams.
- The Digital Evolution Index source doesn't address how the four-quadrant country classification should be revisited if a "break out" or "watch out" country experiences a geopolitical shock (as it notes happened to Iran, sliding from break-out toward watch-out) — it's unclear how frequently the underlying index itself is expected to be recalculated as conditions change quickly.
