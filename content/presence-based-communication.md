## Summary

As AI can write scripts, polish slides and coach delivery, what distinguishes a business communicator is presence, meaning a verifiable human appearing across a widening set of formats. Trust in AI-generated content erodes because AI cannot explain how it reached a conclusion, which makes confirmed human authorship newly valuable, and audiences increasingly want conversational formats such as moderated panels and podcasts and deliberate self-presentation. Communication has also become a system in which one message moves through several formats, and each of five formats, from written memos to virtual live meetings, needs its own mastery. Casual-feeling formats are not faster to produce, since a panel takes as much planning as a staged talk. What AI cannot do is read a room, so audiences value the sense that the presenter cared enough to make their time worthwhile, and executive coach Andrea Wojnicki advises leaders to shape their career narrative through a present, past and future introduction.

## Highlights

- AI can now generate presentation scripts, polish slides, and give real-time feedback on vocal tone during rehearsal — but audiences still want a real person to show up when the moment matters, since human-to-human connection is what lets them trust both the message and the messenger; presence, not content quality alone, has become the most strategic communication skill in business.
- Trust erodes specifically because AI cannot explain how or why it arrived at a given conclusion, especially when the content carries real consequences — per the 2025 Freeman Trust Report, 95% of survey respondents said they trust brands more after attending an in-person event, and even TED, historically built around formal monologue talks, is shifting toward more conversational formats like demos, interviews, and Q&As.
- Communication has become a system rather than a single moment: a message may move through six different formats (written document, panel discussion, short-form video clips cut from that discussion, social posts) before it actually lands, requiring the same content to be deliberately redesigned — not just repackaged — for each format's specific constraints.
- Presentation stages are shifting from single-speaker monologues toward multi-presenter, moderated conversations, mirroring the broader rise of podcasts and reaction-video formats — but this conversational, seemingly casual shift requires just as much planning as a traditional formal presentation, not less, since aligning a message arc, preparing multiple speakers, planning transitions, and anticipating questions all still require the same underlying strategic work.
- Five distinct communication formats each require independent mastery: written formats (memos and "Slidedocs" that build buy-in before a meeting and serve as durable leave-behinds); virtual live formats (real-time video meetings, now routine for 86% of workers, perceived as trustworthy because delivery is face-to-face even through a screen); virtual asynchronous formats (recorded video/audio updates and pitches, harder to deliver with genuine presence since the speaker addresses a camera rather than a person); facilitated formats (panels and live Q&As designed to feel spontaneous but actually requiring detailed advance planning of tone, timing, and roles); and staged formats (keynotes for high-stakes moments, now designed from the outset to be chopped into shareable clips and quoted across other channels rather than treated as a single, self-contained event).
- It's a myth that casual-feeling formats are faster to produce than formal ones — a panel takes as much planning as a staged talk, a podcast episode can require more editing than a slide deck, and a town hall run without slides can demand more preparation than one with them, since every word, pause, and transition now carries more of the communicative weight.
- What AI genuinely cannot do is read a room — audiences are less interested in being impressed or dazzled by content and more interested in feeling that the presenter cared enough to make their time worthwhile, meaning organizations that treat communication as a core operational discipline (not an afterthought layered on top of "real" work) gain a durable, AI-resistant advantage.

## Concept

<span class="src">Owning the Room in the Age of AI</span> (MIT SMR, Nancy Duarte, July 2025) argues that as generative AI becomes capable of writing presentation scripts, polishing slide decks, and even coaching a speaker's vocal delivery during rehearsal, the actual differentiator between communicators has shifted from content quality to presence — the verifiable, trust-generating experience of a real person showing up, in whatever format "the room" now takes (a sales webinar, a video meeting, a trade-show booth). Duarte, whose presentation-design firm still builds sales materials for large companies, reports a consistent pattern underneath client requests: teams that appear polished on the surface increasingly reveal a deeper "presence problem" rather than a slide-deck problem — an inability to walk into any room, physical or virtual, prepared to make an audience genuinely care. The underlying driver is trust: AI-generated content erodes trust specifically because AI cannot explain how or why it reached a given conclusion, a limitation that matters more as the stakes of the content rise, making confirmation that content was produced by a human newly significant rather than incidental — the 2025 Freeman Trust Report found 95% of surveyed attendees trust brands more after attending an event in person, and even TED, an institution built around the formal solo talk, has begun incorporating more conversational formats (demos, interviews, live Q&As) in response to the same underlying shift in audience expectation. <span class="src">Owning the Room in the Age of AI</span>

The article's central structural observation is that communication has become a system rather than a single event: a single message might move across six different formats — a written document, a panel discussion, short-form video clips cut from that discussion, social posts built from those clips — before it fully lands with its intended audience, and each hop requires deliberate redesign for that specific format's constraints rather than simple repackaging of the same material. This shows up most visibly in the broader shift away from single-speaker monologue presentations toward multi-presenter, moderated conversations, paralleling the wider rise of podcasts and reaction-video content as people increasingly prefer communication that feels conversational rather than performed — internal town halls are incorporating live Q&A and real-time polling instead of one-way information delivery, and presenters are increasingly repositioned as hosts who elevate other voices rather than remaining the sole center of attention. Critically, this apparent shift toward casualness does not reduce the underlying planning burden — it usually increases it: a well-run panel or interactive session requires the same intensity of preparation as a formal keynote (aligning the message arc, prepping every speaker, planning transitions, anticipating likely questions, designing supporting visuals), illustrated by a global services company's interactive "spark tank" team-meeting format, whose planning documentation ran to eleven total pages of prep sheets and process plans despite appearing to attendees as a loose, spontaneous pitch session. <span class="src">Owning the Room in the Age of AI</span>

To actually "own the room" across this widened landscape, the article identifies five distinct communication formats a business communicator now needs fluency across, each with its own demands. Written formats — well-visualized memos or "Slidedocs" used as pre-reads — build buy-in before a meeting even starts and double as both durable documentation and a conversation guide afterward, making decisions easier and faster once clarity is established on the page. Virtual live formats (Zoom meetings, LinkedIn Live, digital town halls) are now central to daily business operations — 86% of workers regularly attend online or hybrid meetings — and are perceived as trustworthy specifically because delivery happens face-to-face, even through a screen, which requires presenters to actively guide the audience and maintain message control rather than simply reading from notes. Virtual asynchronous formats (recorded video or audio updates, sales pitch videos, internal or external podcasts, short-form clips) are a comparatively harder presence challenge, since the speaker addresses a camera lens rather than a visible human audience — Duarte describes personally learning to "feel my heart warm" while speaking into a webcam for recorded staff updates, a skill requiring deliberate practice rather than coming naturally. Facilitated formats (panels, live Q&As) are engineered to feel spontaneous while actually requiring careful advance planning of tone, timing, and role assignment among participants — a well-run panel moderator functions as both a producer managing real-time flow and a presenter in their own right. And staged formats (keynotes) remain essential for the highest-stakes moments (culture change announcements, product launches, strategic pivots) but are now designed from the outset with their downstream repurposing in mind, since the keynote itself is only the first of many touchpoints as it gets chopped into shareable clips, embedded in sales decks, and quoted across investor communications and social channels. The article's closing argument is that AI can accelerate the mechanical work of communication — drafting scripts, formatting slides — but cannot read a room, and that audiences today are less interested in being dazzled by polish than in feeling that a presenter genuinely cared enough to make the shared time worthwhile; organizations that build communication into core operations as a discipline in its own right, rather than treating it as an afterthought bolted onto "real" strategic work, gain an advantage that AI's growing content-generation capability cannot erode. <span class="src">Owning the Room in the Age of AI</span>

## Open Questions

- The article is written from the perspective of a presentation-design consultancy serving large companies and doesn't address whether smaller organizations without dedicated communications resources can realistically build fluency across all five formats, or whether some subset matters more for resource-constrained teams.
- No source quantifies how audiences actually distinguish AI-assisted-but-human-delivered content (a real person reading an AI-drafted script) from fully AI-generated content, or whether the trust benefit the article attributes to "human presence" specifically requires the content itself to also be human-originated.
- The column's guidance is based on coaching experience and carries no published evidence of effect, and the source is only loosely connected to this concept's AI-differentiation argument.
