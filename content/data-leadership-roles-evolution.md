## Summary

Organizations keep building analytics and AI models that never reach production, and a new role, the data product manager, exists to translate business needs into adopted data products. Meanwhile the Chief Data Officer role is proving unstable, which points toward data leadership dissolving into broader business roles, and the same debate extends to whether a dedicated Chief AI Officer or a combined innovation and transformation officer is needed.

## Concept

The earliest of this concept's sources, <span class="src">Five Key Trends in AI and Data Science for 2024</span> (MIT SMR, January 2024), surveyed more than 500 senior data and technology executives across three separate studies (AWS, Thoughtworks, and Wavestone) and identified five trends, two of which anticipate the later, more focused research this concept otherwise centers on. Trend four documented data science fragmenting away from a single generalist "data scientist" role into a wider cast of specialists — data engineers, machine learning engineers, business translators, and data product managers — a trend the later HBR research on data product managers would treat as a settled, distinct role rather than an emerging one, alongside a rise in "citizen data science" as AutoML tools and early LLM-based analysis features let quantitatively literate businesspeople build their own models without a dedicated data scientist. Trend five, more directly relevant, documented data and analytics leadership becoming less independent a full year before MIT SMR's dedicated CDO-instability research: the Thoughtworks survey found 87 percent of respondents reporting confusion about where to turn for data- and technology-oriented services, and 79 percent saying a lack of collaboration among an organization's various tech-oriented leaders had actively hindered the business. The article documented this playing out concretely, not just anecdotally, citing named executives who already held the "supertech leader" role the later CDO research would describe in the abstract — Sastry Durvasula at TIAA, Sean McCormack at First Group, and Mojgan Lefebvre at Travelers, each holding a title like chief information officer, chief information and technology officer, or chief digital and technology officer and reporting directly to their CEO, having absorbed what would otherwise have been a separate CDO or CDAO mandate. (The article's remaining trends — that generative AI was generating enthusiasm far ahead of production deployment, with only 5 to 6 percent of surveyed organizations having any production gen AI application in early 2024, and that data science was industrializing through platforms and MLOps tooling — are broader annual-trends observations outside this concept's specific scope on leadership roles.) <span class="src">Five Key Trends in AI and Data Science for 2024</span>

HBR's research into why so many organizations struggle to convert data science investment into deployed, value-generating systems identifies a specific structural gap: data scientists in legacy companies understand how to build analytical and AI models, but many consider their job complete once a model fits the underlying data well — not once it has actually been adopted, integrated into a business process, and is delivering ongoing measurable value. In response, a growing number of organizations have adopted the concept of "data products" — reusable datasets (and, in a broader definition that also incorporates analytics and AI methods, "analytics products") designed to be analyzed in different ways by different users repeatedly over time to solve a specific business problem, rather than a one-off analytical exercise. Delivering data products successfully at scale, the researchers argue, requires a genuinely new role distinct from both the chief data officer and the data scientist: the data product manager. Like product managers in other domains, data product managers are not expected to possess the full technical or analytical expertise needed to build the underlying model or engineer its data pipeline themselves, nor are they typically skilled at redesigning business processes or retraining affected workers. What they need instead is the ability to manage a genuinely cross-functional development and deployment process, assembling and leading a team with diverse skills, and to communicate effectively with the business leaders whose operations the resulting model or process will change. This role shares real overlap with software product management — coordinating across functions and units, managing diverse teams, exercising influence without formal authority, and understanding customer needs — but carries distinct data-specific requirements as well: a working knowledge of how to capture, extract, and improve the quality of data and integrate it with other sources; a working (if not expert) understanding of analytics and AI, including basic statistics, the major categories of AI and their data/modeling requirements, how machine learning works, the reality that deployed models can "drift" in accuracy over time, and general familiarity with the AI vendor landscape. In practice, data products almost always begin with a minimum-viable-product approach — a deliberately bare-bones initial version meant to generate early customer or user interest — followed by an ongoing iteration cycle producing new versions on a rolling basis; Vista, the marketing and design services company, has put its own spin on this pattern, since its chief data officer found that internal and external customers responded poorly to MVPs that felt unpolished and unfinished, and instead targets an initial "minimum lovable product" before scaling toward a fully mature offering. The payoff from getting this right can be substantial: at Vista, data products have generated an incremental $90 million in profit, much of it recurring annually; at Alabama-based Regions Bank, data products have earned or saved the bank hundreds of millions of dollars. Critically, the data product manager's responsibilities don't end at deployment — they remain accountable for measuring and ensuring the product's ongoing use and value on a quarterly basis, regardless of whether the product serves internal or external customers, since a product that quietly stops delivering value but keeps running is itself a failure mode this role exists to prevent. On what makes a good data product manager, the researchers found broad agreement among the practitioners they interviewed that the role requires both a genuine business orientation and real familiarity with data and analytics, plus the ability to lead a diverse team — but also a consistent view that data scientists themselves generally don't make good data product managers, since they tend to be excessively focused on optimizing a model's fit to its data rather than on the broader adoption and value-realization process (Vista's CDO is a partial exception, finding data scientists useful in the role specifically for highly technical data products). Vista draws its data product managers either from people with deep domain expertise in specialized areas (dynamic pricing, media mix modeling, manufacturing analytics) or from seasoned software product managers who already know how to run the underlying process; one consultant interviewed proposes an alternative structure of three co-equal leaders — a data product manager, a data scientist, and an engineering lead — deliberately designed to maintain healthy tension among the three rather than letting any single leader dominate decisions. Regions Bank took yet another approach, creating a "data product partner" role embedded within specific parts of the bank, responsible for a complete data or analytics solution, sitting in that unit's staff meetings, and performing continuous bidirectional translation between business needs and the technical solutions that can address them — most of these hires came from the business side of the bank but brought a technical and analytical orientation with them. The researchers frame the data product manager as one of a genuinely new class of jobs created by the confluence of digitization, pervasive data, and analytics/AI capability — and argue that even as organizations create increasingly specialized technical roles like data scientist and data engineer, the need for generalists who can combine those specialized roles into effective, value-delivering teams never actually goes away. <span class="src">Why Your Company Needs Data-Product Managers</span>

While HBR's research addresses the operational role needed to turn data into deployed products, MIT Sloan Management Review's later research turns to a more structural instability at the executive level: the Chief Data Officer role itself. The context is a striking paradox — the Data & AI Leadership Exchange's 2025 survey found 98.4% of organizations are increasing their investment in data and AI (up dramatically from 82.2% just a year earlier), and 84.3% have now appointed a CDO or chief data and analytics officer (CDAO), up from just 12% in 2012 — yet despite this apparent institutionalization, only 47.6% of organizations characterize their CDO role as "very successful and well established," with the majority instead describing it as nascent, evolving, or outright failing. The tenure statistics underline the instability directly: more than half of CDOs (53.7%) serve less than three years in the role, and nearly a quarter (24.1%) last less than two — a pattern one executive recruiter described starkly: "I have spoken with more data leaders looking for work in the past year than in all my previous years working in this space." This displacement is happening even as demand for AI skills specifically is rising and the broader US tech unemployment rate sits near a low 2.5%, raising the pointed question of why data leaders specifically are being pushed out at a moment when data's strategic importance has arguably never been higher. The researchers identify three interacting forces converging to seal the fate of many CDOs. The first is strategic value creation pressure: as economic conditions tightened over the past 12-18 months, executive teams began scrutinizing budgets more closely, exposing a frustrating gap between the promised value of data investments and their measurable impact on operating margins — CDOs who had previously operated with an implicit exemption from the profitability targets their peers in sales, operations, and product development faced (a leeway rooted partly in the role's novelty and partly in an assumption that data investment was inherently strategic) increasingly found that leeway evaporating, with one retail group's CFO summarizing the frustration bluntly: "all we have to show for [our data investments] are prettier dashboards." The second force is operating model evolution: as organizations' data maturity increases, there's a consistent trend toward decentralization, with business units seeking direct ownership of their own data assets, talent, and — critically — their own data budgets, a shift that often directly shrinks the centralized CDO function's relevance and resourcing; more than 90% of surveyed data leaders cite cultural challenges, not technology, as the primary impediment to becoming genuinely data-driven, and the researchers observe that in many organizations data teams remain isolated from the C-suite relationships that would let them influence this decentralization on favorable terms, sometimes compounding the problem by treating "the business" as something apart from themselves rather than as their actual constituency. The third force is generative AI disruption specifically: much of an organization's realized data-driven value now comes from deploying and scaling AI (forecasting models, recommendation engines, process automation), yet many CDOs have failed to engage deeply enough with AI to understand and champion its concrete business and productivity potential, becoming instead overindexed on data architecture, buried in dashboard proliferation, or stuck cycling through unscaled AI pilots — a pattern compounded by boards themselves struggling to understand AI's implications (only 51.4% of surveyed board members reported being well versed in data and AI issues and responsibilities). Given this AI-engagement gap, 33.1% of surveyed organizations have found it simpler to appoint a separate, dedicated AI leader rather than expand the CDO's existing mandate, often assigning generative AI responsibility instead to the CTO or CFO — executives who typically bring stronger existing cross-business relationships, deeper familiarity with relevant business processes, and are seen as stronger governance leaders in a domain where balancing AI's risks against its opportunities is paramount; one strategy consultant summarized the resulting reality plainly: "while data leaders will talk about owning the GenAI strategy, in practice, we never work with the CDO; it tends to be the CFO or CTO." Rather than proposing a defense of the standalone CDO mandate against these three forces, the researchers' central recommendation is dissolution: developing C-suite leaders who combine deep domain expertise (gained through roles carrying genuine revenue or customer responsibility) with strong data and analytics capability — executives who go well beyond basic data literacy to genuinely understand the constituent parts of a successful data strategy and can build and manage the teams needed to deliver on it. This path, the researchers argue, is less radical than it may sound, given that 36.3% of data leaders already report into a business leader (CEO, president, or COO) rather than into technology leadership, and they explicitly propose reframing the CDO role not as a career destination in its own right but as a waypoint toward a broader executive position — envisioning, for instance, a CDO becoming CEO of an international business unit as a natural next career step. The researchers sketch three concrete destinations current CDOs are well-positioned to move toward: the commercial and operational leader (COO or CCO), since data is central to unlocking growth and margin expansion in these roles and, unlike the typical CDO, these executives already carry direct P&L accountability that gives their data-driven recommendations real organizational teeth; the technology and product leader (CTO or CPO), roles that already require deep experience with enterprise architecture, vendor management, data integration, and closing the loop between customer signals and product decisions in ways a data-fluent executive is well suited to own; and the governance leader (CFO or chief legal officer), on the logic that these executives already own audit, risk, and board-level information-asset accountability by default, making them natural homes for data governance and data-ethics responsibility that many frustrated CDOs have struggled to secure an exclusive mandate over. The researchers close by noting an interesting tension in their own survey data: 70.8% of respondents believe the CDO will become a permanent C-suite role, despite the extensive evidence their own research documents to the contrary — a gap they interpret not as contradicting their thesis but as reflecting genuine, widely shared belief that data accountability belongs at the C-level, which is a different claim from believing the specific standalone CDO title itself will persist as the vehicle for that accountability; they cite Thomas H. Davenport's competing proposal of an overarching "super CIO" role, reporting directly to the CEO, as one alternative already gaining traction among some organizations pursuing the same underlying goal by a different structural route. <span class="src">The Chief Data Officer Role What’s Next</span>

<span class="src">Do You Really Need a Chief AI Officer</span> (MIT SMR, August 2024), written by four former chief digital officers, applies the same underlying logic the CDO-dissolution research would later formalize to a narrower, more immediate question: should an organization create a Chief AI Officer (CAIO) role at all? Drawing directly on their own CDO experience, the authors lay out genuine arguments on both sides — a CAIO can reduce AI-project fragmentation, elevate transformational (not just incremental) AI ambitions, centralize management of AI-specific legal and ethical risk, and address internal AI-expertise gaps — against real costs: cross-functional turf conflict with the CIO, CTO, COO, and any existing CDO; a structural bias toward AI solutions even when simpler non-AI fixes would serve better; and significant added cost that a weak business case can't justify, illustrated by a CDO office that grew to more than 100 full-time employees before a cost-cutting review reversed course. Their central recommendation is to map the decision to an organization's AI maturity rather than treat it as a fixed strategic choice: a CAIO adds little value at the earliest stage (before enough AI activity exists to coordinate), delivers the most value in the middle stages (consolidating fragmented pilots, then guiding selective scale-up and the unglamorous work of fixing underlying data quality), and becomes actively counterproductive once AI is fully integrated into normal operations, at which point the role should be handed off to existing functions rather than made permanent. For organizations that do proceed, the article specifies conditions for success: a clearly defined mandate with measurable deliverables (not vague aspirations like "make us a leader in AI"), explicit delineation of responsibilities against the CIO/CTO/CDO, fixed and transparent resourcing, and a leader chosen primarily for political, communication, and change-management skill rather than pure technical depth, since a CAIO typically has to lead through influence rather than formal authority.

<span class="src">Why AI Demands a New Breed of Leaders</span> (MIT SMR, April 2025) extends the debate one step further, explicitly citing this concept's other two MIT SMR sources (the CDO-dissolution research and the "supertech leader" trend) as evidence that organizations are already restructuring the C-suite, and proposes a specific synthesis role for what should come next: the chief innovation and transformation officer (CITO). The article's starting premise is that AI implementation is being treated as a primarily technical problem when its harder challenges are organizational and psychological — a 2024 CIO survey found only 28% of CIOs name leading transformation as their top priority despite 85% agreeing CIOs should be changemakers, and a separate survey found 91% of data leaders cite cultural and change-management challenges (not technology) as the primary obstacle to becoming data-driven. The costs of getting this wrong are concrete: Zillow lost $300 million and saw its stock fall more than 20% after its AI-generated property-valuation algorithm failed inside its own homebuying division, and an Air Canada generative AI chatbot gave incorrect bereavement-fare guidance to customers already dealing with a death in the family, illustrating why human-facing AI decisions need explicit ethical boundaries and human-in-the-loop design, not just technical correctness. The CITO role the authors envision would combine technical oversight, ethical alignment, cultural change leadership, and cross-functional integration in a single office — evangelist, strategic innovation lead, and hands-on transformation expert combined — because AI implementation touches every part of an organization simultaneously and a leader confined to any one domain lacks the mandate to manage the whole. The article documents this consolidation already happening under inconsistent titles: PepsiCo's Athina Kanioura (chief strategy and transformation officer, appointed 2020) oversees the company's full digitization including AI; Standard Chartered's Roel Louwhoff (chief transformation, technology, and operations officer) previously held a similar combined role at ING Bank; and TIAA's Sastry Durvasula (chief operating, information, and digital officer) — who also appears in this concept's earlier CDO research as a named real-world "supertech leader" example — explicitly frames employee reskilling and transition as core to his mandate. Boston Consulting Group research cited in the article found chief transformation officer hires increased more than 140 percent from 2019 to 2021, with companies making such hires seeing a significant subsequent increase in total shareholder return, suggesting this consolidation pattern is a rewarded organizational response rather than a speculative proposal. The article closes by identifying "AI persona management" — governing the distinct personalities, roles, and behavioral consistency of AI agents interacting with employees and customers — as an emerging CITO responsibility with no current owner in most organizations' existing leadership structures. <span class="src">Why AI Demands a New Breed of Leaders</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses whether the data product manager role itself faces the same structural instability the CDO role does — if CDOs are increasingly absorbed into broader business leadership positions, it's unclear whether the data product manager role (which the HBR research frames as reporting to or working alongside a CDO/data organization) survives that reorganization intact, gets redistributed into business units, or gets absorbed into product management more broadly.
- The CDO research's three proposed destinations (commercial/operational, technology/product, governance) aren't reconciled with each other — it's unclear what determines which destination is right for a given CDO, or whether an organization should expect to see all three patterns coexist even within a single company as different CDOs move to different functions.
