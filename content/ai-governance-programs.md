## Summary

Effective AI governance matches the type of control to the kind of system and risk, then turns principles into everyday practice through ownership, incentives, routines and a fully designed program. Adaptive governance layers rules-based controls for narrow static systems, ex post alignment testing for opaque or generative systems and ecosystem-level propagation-risk controls for interconnected and agentic systems, while BCG finds that companies with all six agent controls in place capture about three times the value. Research on responsible AI shows why programs fail through accountability, strategy and resource gaps, and offers the SHARP framework, a readiness checklist, the Responsible Innovation and Commercialization principles and Scotiabank's hardwired ethics process as ways to close the gap between stated principles and practice.

## Concept

The adaptive-governance research is based on interviews from 2022 to 2025 with senior governance, risk, compliance, data and product leaders at Microsoft, Barclays, Kyriba, Nasdaq, Lloyds Bank, Danske Bank and the Abu Dhabi Department of Finance, validated against disclosures and filings from more than 40 other financial institutions. It starts from a gap, since government and regulatory frameworks already state principles such as transparency, fairness and accountability, yet organizations struggle to translate them into day-to-day workflows and decisions. The proposed answer reframes governance from a compliance obligation into a strategic, adaptive capability, built on matching controls to the type of AI system and risk and embedding the controls in workflows, decision rights and accountability structures rather than adding a separate oversight function. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

Choosing controls begins with recognizing two structurally different moments of risk. Development risk covers problems introduced before a system goes live, namely biased or incomplete training data, models poorly aligned to the task and insufficient validation, as when an early credit-limit model at one bank produced large swings in decisions from small changes in input data. Deployment risk emerges when a model meets a dynamic environment and real operators, through data drift, in which quality degrades as live data diverges from training data, plausible but false outputs, and users over-trusting output they cannot verify. Nasdaq's market-surveillance systems, which generate hundreds of alerts per second, show it, since the line between abnormal and illicit behavior is often blurred when illegitimate trading is designed to exploit a model's learned patterns and look compliant. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

Two further dimensions of the system matter. The first is whether it is bounded-learning or adaptive. Bounded-learning systems operate within fixed rules and parameters and improve by optimizing how the rules are applied, as credit-scoring models and most pretrained generative AI that does not update during use do, while adaptive systems evolve by feeding production data back into their training and updating the relationships among variables, as algorithmic trading platforms and dynamic fraud detection do. The second is scope, whether errors stay within a function, as in anomaly detection in one transaction stream, or propagate across functions, geographies or industries, as in a cross-border supply-chain optimization platform, a difference the research calls exponential rather than incremental because errors in a wide system interact and amplify into second-order effects absent from any component. Crossing these dimensions with timing yields three layers of control, each necessary but insufficient on its own. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

Rules-based controls form the baseline for narrow, static systems whose logic is explicit and auditable, such as credit scoring, fraud detection and customer-service chatbots. They embed norms and compliance requirements in the model design as constraints, supplemented by validation testing and anomaly monitoring. One bank deliberately uses a statistical rather than deep-learning approach for its credit-limit-increase model so that decisions stay interpretable, and before any model is deployed the analytics team produces a model card covering data checks, meaning completeness, recency, balance and how drift will be detected, decision-logic and edge-case review, including explicit analysis of the gray zone around the threshold where a customer tips into an increase, and bias and discrimination testing for overfitting to profiles or disadvantage to groups. A model-risk unit with credit and regulatory expertise reviews the card independently and internal audit verifies it before launch, and human judgment stays central, since one organization ran each new midmarket lending model against 100 sampled client files and had relationship managers compare their assessments with the model's, investigating divergences to decide whether the model had found an insight or was overfitting, with periodic sample reviews continuing after launch. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

Ex post alignment controls form the second layer, needed when systems, especially deep neural networks, grow so complex that traceability and explainability lose effectiveness, because rules-based controls depend on specifying decision logic that becomes more opaque as models become more sophisticated, and generative AI compounds this through stochastic behavior in which an identical prompt can yield different outputs. Instead of explaining how a decision was reached, these controls test whether outcomes remain legitimate against ethical, regulatory and domain standards, shifting emphasis from preventing errors upfront to detecting misalignment as systems operate and scale. Microsoft evaluates high-stakes models against libraries of expert-defined policies for fair and acceptable outcomes, with evaluators annotating outputs and independent reviewers validating shortfalls, partly automated through continuous monitoring and automatic flagging of deviations. Algorithmic auditing is a core mechanism, first identifying failure scenarios and defining the full use case, who the system serves, who its decisions affect and for what purpose, and then monitoring those risks through decision outputs, input data and internal logic to surface disparate impacts, document recurring patterns and trigger corrective action. Users must also be trained to treat probabilistic recommendations as informed signals rather than truth, knowing when to rely on a system, when to challenge it and how to spot biased output. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

At scale this layer needs structured human review. Nasdaq's cross-functional teams of compliance officers, data scientists and domain experts review flagged activity through case workflows, document the rationale for each conclusion and feed outcomes back to developers to recalibrate thresholds, with escalation committees stepping in when investigations suggest coordinated bad actors or systemic risk and audit trails capturing the chain from alert through decision to model adjustment. A global bank's fraud-detection experience shows that misalignment often comes from workflow breakdown rather than model error, since alerts were routed to the wrong team, duplicated across units or left unresolved because no group owned the next step, and customer-service staff sometimes contacted clients before fraud teams had validated an alert, which the bank fixed by mapping the workflow step by step, assigning clear ownership at each decision point and standardizing rules for when to suppress, investigate or escalate an alert. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

Propagation-risk controls form the third and most demanding layer. Rules-based and ex post controls both treat risk as confined to one system, focused on discrete errors or individual outputs, which is reasonable for isolated systems but leaves governance incomplete once systems are linked through real-time data flows, APIs and automated decisions. Agentic AI makes the gap consequential, since systems that initiate actions, coordinate with other systems and pursue goals across domains can propagate an error from one system into others, and regulators are responding, with the Bank of England flagging deep trading agents that could amplify market shocks or coordinate in ways that evade human detection, and parallel dynamics appearing in healthcare, where biased diagnostic models spread flawed heuristics across hospitals and insurers, and in supply chains, where procurement platforms amplify pricing errors across supplier networks. The risk is hard to govern because it is structurally invisible, since failures travel through hidden interdependencies, so a minor logistics API error harmless alone could combine with an unrelated payment-gateway cyber incident to contribute to systemic breakdown. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

Managing propagation risk requires a shift from a company-centric to an ecosystem-aware perspective with three activities, mapping interdependencies across organizational boundaries, monitoring shared infrastructure for cross-organization vulnerabilities and institutionalizing anticipatory rather than reactive oversight. The European Central Bank's sectorwide cyber-resilience stress tests show the idea at scale by mapping dependencies across clearinghouses, payment systems and financial institutions, monitoring shared infrastructure and simulating how a local disruption could cascade, practices the research says generalize to any interconnected environment. In practice it requires redistributing visibility, accountability and decision rights across an ecosystem and often a cultural shift toward data sharing, coordinated standards and co-investment in shared oversight infrastructure with partners, competitors, regulators and sometimes open-source communities, since no company can achieve resilience alone because resilience is a property of the wider system it depends on. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

Identifying the right controls is not enough, and three practices implement them. First, risk-control protocols must be hardwired into workflows, accountability structures and incentives, with oversight flowing into planning, audit and leadership-review cycles instead of sitting in a separate compliance layer. One bank's AI use-case committee documents for every approved use case the risk tier, based on customer impact, regulatory impact, data sensitivity and model type, the mandatory controls for that tier, such as independent validation, sample testing and post-deployment review frequency, and the decision rights over model changes, then encodes them in the credit-approval system so relationship managers cannot bypass validation or review without sign-off from both business and risk. Second, organizations need mechanisms for conclusive judgment across heterogeneous expertise, methods and risk tolerances, not consensus or homogenized judgment, since domain differences reflect legitimate differences in expertise and appetite. Siloed knowledge undermines rules-based controls, which joint model reviews and shared model cards address, and ex post controls break down when teams hold misaligned risk thresholds or incompatible validation methods, which shared evaluative routines such as incident and near-miss analysis, drift monitoring and structured comparison of intended and actual outcomes address by giving analytic teams, experimenters and use-case owners common reference points, avoiding both premature shutdown and paralysis. Propagation-risk governance needs the same, with shared rules of engagement, escalation rights and lasting decision authority, or interdependency mapping stays a one-off exercise. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

Third, governance must be a living learning system, since AI risks mutate and controls that do not evolve fall behind, which requires assigned responsibility for reviewing incidents and near-misses, documenting what went wrong and translating the lessons into revised policies, recalibrated thresholds and stronger controls. The overarching argument is that competitive advantage will come from governing AI better rather than adopting it faster, by placing oversight where decisions are made, risk propagates and value is created. BCG's Applied AI Index report treats governance as an enabler of value, recommending that boards articulate and review AI risk appetite and keep an enterprise-wide inventory of agents with transparency into the most consequential, and noting that agents that combine data, tools, permissions, memory and other agents can cause risks to emerge dynamically and compound at machine speed. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span> <span class="src">The Formula for Agentic AI Value (BCG)</span>

BCG's survey finds that 42% of companies expect agents to act autonomously by 2030 yet only 5% have agent controls in place, and that companies with all six controls enterprise-wide capture about three times the agentic value of those with one. The six controls are memory and context handling, scope and oversight, evaluation and rollback gates, standardized tool integration and agent registries, named cross-functional ownership with authority to stop an agent, and runtime security, audit and cost guardrails, and central guardrails with federated implementation bring three times as many workflows to scale as fully centralized steering. A large business-process outsourcer is cited as using an autonomy ladder from insight to copilot to autopilot, set at the level of the process rather than the tool. <span class="src">The Formula for Agentic AI Value (BCG)</span>

Responsible AI programs show the same difficulty from the organizational side, since organizations embrace the language of fairness, accountability and transparency while implementation lags, producing biased outcomes, unexplainable decisions and public backlash. New York City's 2023 AI action plan publicly committed to those principles and created an algorithm management and policy officer role, yet within months a chatbot it deployed to advise small businesses was found misinforming users about labor laws and licensing in ways that could lead them into violations, which raised questions about its governance protocols and deployment process as well as accuracy. Interviews with more than 20 AI leaders, ethics officers and executives across technology, financial services, healthcare and the public sector identified three structural gaps rather than simple lack of good intentions. <span class="src">The Three Obstacles Slowing Responsible AI</span>

The accountability gap is the most stubborn, since organizations publish principles but few define who is responsible for embedding them day to day, so responsibility is widely shared but rarely owned. It starts with how principles are written, as one adviser observed that companies define principles from what they already do, which legitimizes current practice without asking for change, and persists in execution, where fairness assessments are sometimes run by the teams who built the models, biases in historical data go unaddressed for lack of a defined fix process, and there is no equivalent of GAAP for auditing algorithmic fairness. Incentives compound it, since most practitioners are evaluated on speed, accuracy and delivery, so efforts drift toward symbolic compliance. The strategy gap arises because RAI functions sit in compliance, privacy or risk departments downstream of product decisions, consulted only after the product is built, so ethics becomes a speed bump instead of a source of trust, pilots stall over usage questions no one raised, and governance programs lose influence. The resource gap is that staffing, training and tools do not match stated commitment, fairness reviews are rarely properly resourced, governance boards fade as competing demands crowd them out and tools such as open-source fairness toolkits go unused because they are not integrated into workflows. <span class="src">The Three Obstacles Slowing Responsible AI</span>

The SHARP framework describes five ways organizations have reconfigured structures, incentives and routines to close the gaps. Structuring ownership at the project level means a named responsible-AI lead embedded in each high-impact initiative rather than confined to legal or compliance, as a financial services company found that naming an individual ended the diffusion of responsibility, since if it is everyone's job it is no one's job. Hardwiring ethics into everyday procedures means building review into existing workflows, as a healthcare analytics company did with an ethics checklist in its DevOps pipeline that requires training-data validation, stakeholder consultation and documented harm assessment alongside automated tests so that it felt like something the team does rather than something imposed. Aligning ethical risk with business risk means expressing ethics in terms executives already track, as a European insurer modeled the cost of an algorithmic-bias failure reaching the media, from client churn to brand damage, and another organization reported RAI indicators on its enterprise risk dashboard beside cybersecurity metrics. <span class="src">The Three Obstacles Slowing Responsible AI</span>

Rewarding responsible behavior means incentivizing it explicitly, as a Nordic energy company added an impact-awareness dimension to performance reviews for AI and data teams to signal what it values and a European telecommunications firm created an award for teams that shelved a promising model over fairness concerns or chose transparency over raw performance. Practicing ethical judgment, not just compliance, recognizes that no process substitutes for human judgment in novel situations, as a public agency ran recurring AI ethics labs to build shared vocabulary and the confidence to raise concerns and a logistics firm added rotating prompts about data use, consent and unintended consequences to its sprint retrospectives, keeping employees active moral agents because overreliance on AI decision tools can crowd out habits of ethical reflection. The finding is that closing the gap is an organizational challenge of people, processes, incentives and routines, with executives enabling the conditions rather than owning responsible AI alone. <span class="src">The Three Obstacles Slowing Responsible AI</span>

A second HBR piece addresses organizations that rush to implement RAI programs before designing them. Leaders reasonably want proof that a use case works before investing in guardrails, since no one installs seat belts before knowing whether the car moves, but once a solution proves its return, the deferred risks become urgent at once and teams improvise a program with no clear model of a robust one, wasting resources and slowing innovation. A narrow mini program scoped to one solution is a reasonable stopgap but becomes unwieldy as use cases accumulate, when an enterprise-wide design becomes unavoidable and should have come first. The checklist poses eight questions to answer yes before implementation. It asks whether the objectives are determined, whether best-in-class, regulatory minimum or ahead of pending regulation, and whether the program will vary by market. It asks whether values such as fairness, safety and transparency are tied to procedures, since abstract terms mean different things to different people, for example by identifying which stakeholders could be discriminated against at each lifecycle stage and what happens when risk crosses a threshold. It asks whether program-level metrics, covering awareness, compliance and impact, are distinguished from model-level metrics and whether it is settled what to measure, who collects it and how often. <span class="src">Designing a Responsible AI Program Start with this Checklist</span>

It further asks whether the cross-functional committee overseeing rollout is trained, since each member's expertise is also a blind spot for risks outside their domain and none is automatically expert at rolling out an enterprise program, and whether the personnel needed to scale and maintain the program exist or are planned, since existing quality assurance and audit staff are not trained for this work by default and training them usually costs less than hiring, although dedicated hires will eventually be needed. It asks whether the interaction with cybersecurity, privacy and IT optimization programs is mapped, so that RAI augments them rather than stifling innovation, whether a strategic roadmap covers design through audit, including deployment cadence, simultaneous or department by department with gating milestones, and stakeholder scope covering vendors, partners and the board, and whether an implementation playbook translates the roadmap into department-specific workflows, risk guidance and the checklists and tutorials people need to follow procedures. The closing distinction is that baby steps belong in implementation, where phasing by department or region suits finite resources, but not in design, which should be as complete as possible beforehand, like designing a four-wing building fully and constructing it wing by wing rather than building one wing and improvising additions, and design takes far fewer resources than implementation. <span class="src">Designing a Responsible AI Program Start with this Checklist</span>

Research on commercializing any emerging technology finds the same dysfunction beyond AI. Interviews with executives and managers across technology scouting, adoption and commercialization found that business imperatives dominate early decisions and legal and regulatory compliance dominate later ones, and almost no one could point to a systematic process for evaluating a technology's broader implications before release, leaving individuals to navigate tensions among compliance, financial return and personal ethical judgment alone while the organization bears the consequences when that judgment is wrong. Air Canada's customer-service chatbot, which fabricated a bereavement-fare policy and led a customer to sue and win, illustrates the accountability and strategy gaps. The authors also document an evasion in which companies offload liability instead of avoiding harm, as one formed legally separate entities for riskier technologies and another took a startup-board observer seat to influence direction without liability, a workaround that their framework is designed to close. <span class="src">Avoiding Harm in Technology Innovation</span>

The Responsible Innovation and Commercialization framework adapts the academic Responsible Research and Innovation tradition into four principles. Anticipation means systematically imagining a technology's possible uses and consequences for the company and affected groups before committing, as when a corporate venture team declined to invest in an engineered microbe after two chemists raised unresolved questions about environmental dispersal. Reflexivity surfaces a team's unexamined industry and organizational assumptions, as when a chemical-company venture manager rejected an ocean-biodegradable plastic because it met only an industry standard that tolerated 30% of the treated plastic ending up on the ocean floor. Inclusion deliberately engages affected outside stakeholders, not to build consensus but to surface perspectives internal assumptions would miss, as a company forced to withdraw a GMO food product after skipping engagement now works with the advocacy groups it once avoided. Responsiveness accepts accountability and prepares a response plan, up to market withdrawal, before consequences appear, as a company that found early safety-literature signals about nanoparticle inhalation during pre-launch testing of a cleaning spray pivoted to another use. The obstacles match those named elsewhere, namely competitive pressure for speed and profit, vague mission statements that produce disagreement about what ethical means and under-resourcing, though individual managers can apply the principles to their own projects and build a record that leads to wider adoption. <span class="src">Avoiding Harm in Technology Innovation</span>

Scotiabank supplies an award-winning example of hardwiring ethics into procedures, at a bank that won both a Most Innovative Use of AI award and a Best Responsible AI Program award at the same event. With Deloitte Canada it built an Ethics Assistant application that evaluates the ethical impact of every new AI or machine-learning use case as the mandatory first step of a project, early enough that a flagged use case can be redesigned, alongside a data ethics policy within the code of conduct that every employee re-attests annually and a mandatory data ethics education program for the analytics organization and others doing advanced analytics. <span class="src">How Scotiabank Built an Ethical, Engaged AI Culture</span>

The article presents responsible AI and effective AI as products of one cultural shift. The award-winning chatbot, whose accuracy rose from 35% to 90% since its late-2022 launch and which resolves more than 40% of customer questions without human intervention, with LLM-generated conversation summaries cutting ramp-up time for human agents on escalated cases by 60-70%, improved because contact-center staff, data and analytics, product design and engineering teams took joint ownership of curating the knowledge base, the same cross-functional engagement that made the ethics program credible. The chief data and analytics officer, Grace Lee, links this to generative AI's accessibility, since in the authors' executive survey the share of organizations reporting an established data and analytics culture doubled from 21% to 43% in a year, which they attribute to generative AI making data quality problems visible and worth fixing for ordinary business users. Extending data ownership from structured data in a single cloud enterprise data model to unstructured knowledge, treated as a data-management responsibility, with plans to spread early wins in the contact center and payments across the bank, shows what hardwiring ethics looks like when resourced, addressing the resource gap as well as the accountability and strategy gaps. <span class="src">How Scotiabank Built an Ethical, Engaged AI Culture</span>
