## Summary

PwC's "five Cs" framework for telcos to escape commoditization and regain pricing power — convergence (bundling), customer experience, commercial innovation, cost excellence, and complexity reduction. Grounded in research across 50+ national mobile markets showing declining ARPU and increasing commoditization, especially in Europe.

## Concept

PwC's research diagnoses telecom operators as caught in a genuine two-sided profitability squeeze: on the revenue side, industry growth is projected at only a 2.9 percent global compound annual rate from 2023 to 2028, held back by slowing macroeconomic growth, saturation in core connectivity markets, and continued uncertainty about how quickly newer revenue streams like 5G services and B2B IoT applications will actually materialize; on the cost side, energy and labor costs are rising broadly, compounded by "hidden" pressure from a widening East-West technology supply-chain divide that reduces the scale efficiencies telcos' equipment vendors can offer. Detailed research across more than 50 national mobile markets confirms the resulting commoditization pressure directly: average revenue per user (ARPU) is declining in real terms in most markets even where nominally still positive, pricing differentiation between operators remains historically low (particularly in Europe, which continues to lag other regions in resisting commoditization despite a slight recent improvement), and — perhaps most strikingly — the number of competing operators in a market (three or fewer versus four or more) shows surprisingly little correlation with how differentiated pricing actually is, a finding that directly challenges the long-standing regulatory assumption that a specific "optimal" number of mobile players determines competitive intensity, and one with real implications for whether regulators should reconsider their historically tight restrictions on in-market consolidation. The financial consequence of this squeeze is stark: major US and EU mobile carriers collectively generated $171 billion in operating cash from 2019 through 2023, but $98 billion of that went to capital expenditure and acquisitions and a further $72 billion to debt servicing and dividends, leaving the industry with essentially nothing left over to fund genuine innovation — and this despite years of costly 5G network investment that has largely failed to move inflation-adjusted ARPU meaningfully upward in most markets. <span class="src">Five commercial imperatives to help telcos regain an edge</span>

Against this backdrop, PwC's prescription for telco CEOs is organized around a "five Cs" framework specifically targeted at rebuilding pricing power — a company's ability to actually influence what it charges in the marketplace, as distinct from simply managing costs or waiting for market conditions to improve. Convergence, the first C, means bundling fixed, mobile, and other services (television, for instance) into a single contract rather than separate ones per connectivity type, which reinforces connectivity's status as an essential, non-discretionary purchase — consumers keep paying for gas, food, and power even as prices rise, and the argument is that broadband connectivity, bundled this way, can command similarly resilient demand. Convergence simultaneously increases share of wallet, deepens loyalty, extends customer lifetime value, and reduces churn, since customers subscribed to multiple bundled services are measurably "stickier." Customer Experience and Service Excellence, the second C, leans specifically on AI to reverse telecom's historically poor reputation in this area — first by pre-emptively avoiding service incidents and outages through better predictive maintenance, and second by reducing the time and friction of incident recovery when problems do occur, for instance through AI-supported call-center agents. Charter Communications is offered as a working example: its CEO describes the company's AI tools helping agents "get to a better answer faster and actually show more empathy along the way," with the broader expected payoff being improved customer and employee satisfaction alongside fewer costly physical service visits and lower churn. Commercial Innovation, the third C, means enriching service bundles beyond basic fixed-mobile convergence by embedding additional — often third-party — elements like content, insurance, or niche segment-specific packages (for instance, a bundle combining data, international calls, and public-transit tickets aimed specifically at short-term visitors). Verizon's myPlan illustrates this in practice, letting postpaid customers personalize their own bundle by adding or removing premium components (video-on-demand, gaming, other subscriptions) for a fixed monthly fee — shifting from a rigid, one-size-fits-all bundle to a customizable subscription model that strengthened the company's competitive position. <span class="src">Five commercial imperatives to help telcos regain an edge</span>

Cost Excellence, the fourth C, covers more classic cost-reduction levers — reducing average labor cost through global delivery models and centers of excellence in lower-cost regions, reducing headcount through automation and AI extended well beyond its traditional back-office confines into customer-facing functions (call centers, sales) and increasingly into core network and software operations — alongside rigorous demand management and strategic sourcing on the external-spend side. Vodafone's VOIS initiative, which centralized back-office and technology functions into global shared-services hubs, and Rakuten Mobile's fully virtualized, cloud-native network with extensive AI automation (which cut both capex and opex by minimizing reliance on proprietary hardware and enabling dynamic resource allocation) are offered as concrete examples of cost excellence functioning as a genuine competitive differentiator, not merely a defensive measure. Complexity Reduction, the fifth C, targets a different and more structural kind of cost: the sheer variety of products, platforms, and underlying technology a telco has accumulated over decades, which compounds cost and slows the organization down even when each individual product or system is well managed on its own terms. The recommended discipline centers on legacy retirement — described as challenging and unglamorous but necessary to escape a self-perpetuating cost spiral — supported by a clear technology-architecture blueprint that guides which legacy components to retire or modernize and prioritizes modular, reusable components going forward. A large Continental European mobile operator illustrates the payoff: bringing product and technology teams together into an agile, sprint-based program to systematically unwind and retire legacy network assets, product platforms, and IT systems took three years to execute, but the resulting reductions in both internal cost and external spend more than justified the multi-year investment of effort. <span class="src">Five commercial imperatives to help telcos regain an edge</span>
