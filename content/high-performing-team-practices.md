## Summary

Research on roughly 6,000 people across high-performing and average teams found that the strongest predictor of team success is a culture of experimentation rather than a concentration of talent. Teams that keep improving share five reinforcing habits, which are trying new approaches, valuing curiosity over ego, surfacing problems early, staying close to the actual work and investing deliberately in individual growth.

## Highlights

- Psychologist and workplace researcher Ron Friedman studied roughly 6,000 people across teams identified by their organizations as either high-performing or average-performing, isolating the habits that most reliably distinguished the two groups — talent concentration was not the strongest predictor; a culture of experimentation was.
- High-performing teams treat trying new approaches as routine rather than exceptional, running small low-stakes experiments constantly instead of waiting for a formal initiative or crisis to justify change; the Oklahoma City Thunder's analytics-driven, defense-first roster-construction approach (built years before it became a "smallball"-adjacent trend across the NBA) illustrates a team culture willing to test unconventional approaches ahead of consensus.
- Curiosity and humility outperform defensiveness and ego on high-performing teams: members treat being wrong as useful information rather than a threat to status, which measurably increases how often problems and disagreements are voiced rather than suppressed.
- The habit of surfacing problems early separates high performers most sharply from average teams, since problems caught early are cheap to fix and problems caught late compound; teams with psychological safety to raise a concern before it becomes a crisis systematically outperform teams where concerns wait for a formal review.
- Staying close to the actual work — leaders and senior members maintaining direct, hands-on contact with front-line execution rather than managing purely through reports and dashboards — correlates with faster detection of both problems and opportunities that would otherwise surface only after significant lag.
- Growth-oriented feedback, delivered frequently and specifically rather than saved for formal review cycles, is illustrated by Adobe's overhaul of its performance-review process and by Spotify's "Confidence" platform, both aimed at replacing infrequent, high-stakes evaluation with continuous, lower-stakes feedback loops.
- Investing in people's growth beyond their current role — treating skill development as a team-level investment rather than an individual's private responsibility — reinforces all four other habits, since teams confident their members are growing take more experimental risks, surface more problems, and sustain curiosity over ego for longer.

## Concept

<span class="src">How to Build a Superteam That Keeps Getting Better</span> draws on psychologist and workplace researcher Ron Friedman's study of roughly 6,000 people distributed across teams that their own organizations had independently classified as either high-performing or average-performing. Rather than starting from a theory of what should distinguish elite teams, Friedman's approach worked backward from the classification, isolating which concrete, observable habits most reliably separated the two groups. The headline finding runs against a common assumption in team-building: talent concentration — stacking a team with the most individually skilled people available — was not the strongest predictor of sustained high performance. A culture of experimentation was. High-performing teams treat running small, low-stakes trials of new approaches as a routine, ongoing habit rather than an exceptional response to a mandate or crisis, which lets them discover better methods continuously instead of only when forced to. <span class="src">How to Build a Superteam That Keeps Getting Better</span>

The article illustrates this experimental culture with the Oklahoma City Thunder's roster-construction philosophy, built on data-driven, defense-first evaluation years before comparable approaches became mainstream across the NBA — a team willing to act on unconventional internal analysis ahead of external consensus validating the approach. Beyond the willingness to experiment, high-performing teams share a related but distinct trait: curiosity and humility take priority over ego and self-protection. Members of these teams treat being shown wrong as useful information about the world rather than a threat to their status, and this orientation has a direct downstream effect — it measurably increases how often problems, disagreements, and uncomfortable observations actually get voiced in the room rather than quietly suppressed to avoid conflict or embarrassment. This connects directly to the habit the article identifies as most sharply distinguishing high performers from average teams: surfacing problems early. Problems caught while still small and cheap to fix stay small; problems caught late, after they've compounded, become expensive and sometimes irreversible. Teams with enough psychological safety that a member will raise a concern before it becomes an emergency systematically outperform teams where concerns only surface once they can no longer be avoided, typically in a formal review that arrives too late to prevent the damage. <span class="src">How to Build a Superteam That Keeps Getting Better</span>

A further habit — staying close to the actual work — describes leaders and senior team members who maintain direct, hands-on contact with front-line execution rather than managing exclusively through secondhand reports, dashboards, and summaries. This proximity correlates with faster detection of both emerging problems and emerging opportunities that would otherwise surface only after a meaningful reporting lag, since problems and opportunities are often visible in the texture of the work itself well before they show up in aggregated metrics. The article's fifth habit, growth-oriented feedback, calls for feedback that is frequent and specific rather than concentrated into infrequent, high-stakes formal review cycles; it points to Adobe's well-documented overhaul of its performance-review process and to Spotify's "Confidence" feedback platform as two concrete examples of organizations deliberately re-engineering feedback delivery around continuous, lower-stakes loops instead of periodic, high-stakes evaluation events. The final habit, investing in people's growth beyond their current role, functions as reinforcement for the other four: when team members trust that the organization is investing in their long-term development rather than treating them as fixed capacity for the current task, they take more experimental risks, surface more problems rather than hiding them to protect their standing, and sustain curiosity over defensive ego for longer, since their sense of security doesn't depend on always being right in the moment. <span class="src">How to Build a Superteam That Keeps Getting Better</span>

## Open Questions

- The article doesn't specify how Friedman's research controlled for confounding factors like resourcing, leadership tenure, or industry — it's unclear whether the five habits are causal drivers of high performance or correlates that themselves depend on some prior organizational advantage.
- The Oklahoma City Thunder example illustrates the experimentation habit but isn't drawn from Friedman's underlying research sample; the article doesn't clarify whether it's an independently sourced case study or an author-added illustration.
