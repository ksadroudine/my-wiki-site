## Summary

Organizations should set the autonomy of an AI agent according to how well a risk is understood rather than how large it is. The Ethical Nightmare Challenge addresses risks that grow as systems become more complex, and a taxonomy of complicated, ambiguous and uncertain problems shows how much supervision an agent needs.

## Highlights

- The "Ethical Nightmare Challenge" asks leaders to (1) identify the ethical nightmares wide-scale AI use could create for their organization, (2) build the internal resources needed to avoid them, and (3) upskill employees to use those resources plus updated professional judgment — agentic AI makes this urgent because it introduces compounding risks that can create business- and brand-defining disasters. <span class="src">Organizations Aren’t Ready for the Risks of Agentic AI</span>
- Risk complexity scales through five stages as AI building blocks combine: Stage 1 connects an LLM to one other generative or narrow AI/database (multi-model AI); Stage 2 connects it to dozens of databases, models, and the open internet; Stage 3 adds the ability to take digital actions like financial transactions (multi-model agentic AI); Stage 4 lets that agent talk to other internal agents; Stage 5 lets it talk to external agents outside the organization — most Fortune 500 companies the author has worked with lack the internal resources to handle even Stage 2. <span class="src">Organizations Aren’t Ready for the Risks of Agentic AI</span>
- As risk complexity scales, five specific breakdowns follow: it becomes impractical to risk-assess every node so cost/benefit triage is needed; a human in the loop can no longer process the volume of data in real time; go/no-go deployment decisions carry enormous weight yet most organizations lack rigorous pre-deployment evaluation frameworks; real-time monitoring becomes essential since no pre-launch review can anticipate everything; and intervention methods must be designed to isolate the specific failing component (e.g., shut off one narrow-AI model) rather than the whole system, unless the risk originates from the system itself. <span class="src">Organizations Aren’t Ready for the Risks of Agentic AI</span>
- The right level of AI agent autonomy is not determined by how large a risk is but by how well that risk is understood — specifically, whether it is the type of risk that improves with more data and context, which yields a three-type problem taxonomy for calibrating supervision. <span class="src">How Much Supervision Should Companies Give AI Agents</span>
- Complicated problems (e.g., a bank automatically propagating a Federal Reserve rate change across loan calculations and account balances) are well-defined and documented, making them ideal for high autonomy and rule-based, deterministic automation with only occasional checks. Ambiguous problems (e.g., a self-driving car navigating an unexpected obstacle, credit-card fraud detection, matching a customer to a financial advisor) have many variables that are knowable and improve with more data, so agents can be given latitude to learn fast with feedback. Uncertain ("wicked") problems (e.g., a pandemic with no established treatment protocol, a Suez-Canal-style supply-chain shock) are difficult to define even in principle, so more data does not help and high autonomy is dangerous — humans remain better suited to these via adaptability and originality. <span class="src">How Much Supervision Should Companies Give AI Agents</span>

- As agents proliferate, effective AI governance requires shifting accountability from evaluating individual decisions to designing and managing the organizational systems and processes that produce those decisions at scale — captured in Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's framing that "the IT department of every company is going to be the HR department of AI agents in the future." <span class="src">How Much Supervision Should Companies Give AI Agents</span>

## Concept

As organizations move from narrow AI through generative AI to multi-model and agentic AI, the risk landscape does not merely grow — it compounds, because each stage becomes a building block for the next. Narrow AI (facial recognition, credit-scoring models) is comparatively containable: its context of use is typically known in advance, a data-science team can perform risk assessment and monitoring, an expert human in the loop reviews outputs at a manageable pace, and if the tool performs poorly it can simply be switched off with contained disruption. Generative AI breaks several of these assumptions at once: the contexts of deployment explode across every department and role, making it nearly impossible to test for all intended uses; in-the-wild monitoring becomes critical because developers cannot foresee every context; human-in-the-loop review still works but requires much more training, since users must learn to fact-check hallucinated outputs; and risk assessment now happens at multiple points in a more complex lifecycle, since enterprises typically procure and then further modify a pre-trained model rather than building one from scratch. Multi-model and agentic AI represent a further qualitative jump: an LLM connected to other models, databases, and the internet is multi-model AI; give it the ability to take digital actions (e.g., financial transactions) and it becomes multi-model agentic AI; let it talk to other agents inside the organization and it becomes internal multi-agentic AI; let it talk to agents outside the organization and risk becomes, in the author's words, "a head-spinning quagmire of incalculable risk." In the author's experience advising Fortune 500 companies on AI ethical risk programs, virtually no organization yet has the internal resources or trained personnel to handle even the second of these five stages, let alone the later ones. <span class="src">Organizations Aren’t Ready for the Risks of Agentic AI</span>

This compounding complexity produces six specific governance failures that organizations must proactively address rather than solve all at once: determining who should perform which risk assessment becomes impractical to do exhaustively at every node, so organizations need a deliberate cost/benefit method for triaging which assessments matter most relative to their risk appetite; the capacity of a human in the loop to meaningfully review outputs drops sharply even at the earliest agentic stage, since there is simply too much data for a person to process in real time; enormous weight falls on pre-deployment go/no-go decisions, yet most organizations currently lack the rigorous testing and evaluation frameworks needed to make those calls with confidence; real-time monitoring becomes indispensable because no pre-launch review can anticipate every way a complex, multi-agent system might go sideways once live; intervention mechanisms must be designed to isolate and shut down the specific failing component — for instance disabling one malfunctioning narrow-AI model rather than the entire system — except in the cases where the risk originates from the system's overall structure and a full shutdown is genuinely required; and continuous, role-specific employee upskilling (well beyond an annual 30-minute compliance video) is essential, since the most successful organizations invest heavily in training before deploying agentic technology, not after problems surface. Framed this way, the "Ethical Nightmare Challenge" — identifying an organization's specific AI risks, building the internal resources to avoid them, and upskilling employees to use those resources plus professional judgment — becomes both harder and more urgent under agentic AI, but the reward for rising to it is the safe, wide-scale deployment of genuinely transformative capability. <span class="src">Organizations Aren’t Ready for the Risks of Agentic AI</span>

Given this expanding risk surface, the practical question every organization faces is how much autonomy to actually grant a given AI agent. The answer, counterintuitively, is not primarily a function of how large the risk is, but of how well that risk is *understood* — specifically, whether obtaining more data and context would reduce it. This yields a three-type taxonomy for calibrating agent supervision. Complicated problems are those that, while detailed, can be fully defined and documented in advance — for example, a bank's automated system propagating a Federal Reserve interest-rate change across loan calculations, account balances, and customer reports. These are ideal candidates for high autonomy via rule-based, deterministic automation (akin to robotic process automation), needing only occasional checks once set up. Ambiguous problems have many variables that are largely knowable, so predictive accuracy improves as more data accumulates — a self-driving car encountering an unexpected road obstacle, credit-card fraud detection, or matching a banking customer to the right financial advisor based on transactional history are all examples. Agents tackling ambiguous problems won't always get it right initially, but they learn quickly with feedback, and humans are generally worse than agents at reasoning probabilistically in these domains anyway. Uncertain or "wicked" problems, by contrast, are difficult to even define — Rumsfeld's "unknown unknowns" — such as a pandemic with no established treatment protocol or a global supply-chain shock like a blocked shipping canal. Because there is little or nothing in an agent's training data to prepare it for genuinely novel situations like these, granting high autonomy here is dangerous; humans remain better equipped through adaptability, originality, and resilience under uncertainty. <span class="src">How Much Supervision Should Companies Give AI Agents</span>

Waymo's self-driving taxi service offers a concrete illustration of applying this data-and-context logic rather than a blanket human-in-the-loop rule. Its Fleet Response operations center monitors rides in real time but, notably, does not remotely take over driving when a vehicle gets into trouble — as one passenger discovered when their car began endlessly circling a parking lot instead of proceeding to the airport. Frequent teleoperation would be both prohibitively expensive (it requires paying skilled engineers to effectively drive remotely) and unsafe given network latency. Instead, Fleet Response operators answer the vehicle's questions about ambiguous situations while the car continues to navigate using all available information, resolving most situations without any human assistance at all. The strategic logic is that by continuously supplying data and context rather than direct control, Waymo trains its agents to become more reliable and resilient over time, which compounds into greater long-term operational leverage than a policy of blanket human override would provide. This example generalizes into a broader governance principle: as billions of AI agents effectively join the workforce as a new form of digital labor, and as the old approach of scaling data and compute hits diminishing returns, the future belongs to agents that can reason at inference time in real-world situations — but earning that capability requires giving agents the latitude to learn and grow rather than constraining them to answering only the simplest questions. Correspondingly, effective AI governance must shift its center of gravity: rather than evaluating the outcome of each individual judgment call an agent makes, leaders need to focus on designing and managing the overall organizational process by which agentic decisions are made and executed — a shift in accountability from individual decision-makers to system designers that Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang captured when he said the IT department of every company is becoming "the HR department of AI agents." <span class="src">How Much Supervision Should Companies Give AI Agents</span>
