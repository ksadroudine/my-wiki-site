## Summary

How enterprises are architecting technology platforms to run AI agents safely at scale — orchestrator and task agents, interoperability protocols (MCP, A2A), and a "composable and compostable" build-partner-buy discipline that avoids technical debt as the agent market keeps evolving. Grounded in Bain and McKinsey QuantumBlack's work with banks and other large enterprises deploying agents in production. BCG's companion "harness engineering" framework adds the governance operating system — specs, constitution, control panel, context hub, and quality gates — that makes agent output reliable enough to deploy in regulated environments. BCG Platinion's companion CIO framework adds the vendor-archetype decision (curated ecosystem vs. open platform) and four funded-commitment questions that determine whether an enterprise's core systems can support agents at all. BCG adds a four-pillar CIO backbone for managing agent sprawl and an agent development life cycle.

## Concept

Two consultancies, working from hands-on enterprise deployments rather than theory, converge on the same core claim: agentic AI represents a genuine structural shift in enterprise technology architecture, not an incremental extension of prior automation waves, and most organizations' current architectures simply cannot support it at real scale yet. Bain frames the distinction precisely: earlier automation tackled discrete parts of a business process, leaving exceptions for humans to handle manually, while AI agents can reason, collaborate, and coordinate actions to accomplish complex, multistep, nondeterministic work end to end — work that has historically depended on human judgment throughout. This capability rests on two complementary agent roles working together: higher-level orchestrator agents function like project managers, breaking a broader goal into subtasks and tracking progress across them, while task agents execute individual subtasks and report results back to the orchestrator, which compiles outcomes and adjusts the overall workflow as needed. Supporting this pattern at enterprise scale requires evolving IT architecture along six principles. Modernizing the core platform means reworking older, batch-based systems to be API-accessible and responsive to real-time events, so agents can actually find and use core business capabilities — a shift that will need to coexist with legacy infrastructure for the foreseeable future, adding near-term architectural complexity even as it removes longer-term constraints. Ensuring interoperability of agentic services means adopting consistent standards (like the Model Context Protocol) across a necessarily mixed portfolio — custom agents built on engineering tools, prebuilt agents embedded in vendor platforms, and dynamically generated agents from data platforms — since frameworks themselves are becoming more agentic, illustrated by a software-development-lifecycle agent that can coordinate an entire team of specialized sub-agents (design, analysis, engineering, quality assurance) from concept through deployment. Distributing accountability means keeping core agentic platforms under central platform-team control while pushing accountability for assembling, training, testing, deploying, and monitoring individual agents out to the business domains that actually own the underlying expertise and knowledge assets. Scaling data access means building the ingestion pipelines most organizations still lack for unstructured sources — documents, emails, voice recordings, images, video, call transcripts — since this is precisely where the knowledge needed for agentic reasoning often resides, especially in manual or exception-driven processes; a European bank illustrates the payoff, having built infrastructure to combine structured and unstructured data into a holistic customer view that now drives automated, personalized engagement marketing. Updating governance and controls means building in real-time explainability, behavioral observability, and adaptive security to maintain customer trust and avoid regulatory or reputational fallout as agents take on more actual decision-making, alongside disciplined management of the volatile compute costs agents introduce, through dynamic resource allocation, edge deployment, and AI-native financial-operations practices. And shifting the engineering paradigm means evolving software engineering and DevOps processes themselves to manage agents' full lifecycle — testing, monitoring, safe deployment as they learn and adapt — while agents simultaneously take over more of engineers' day-to-day development and operations work, freeing human engineers for architecture, strategy, and higher-value innovation instead. Bain estimates 5 to 10 percent of technology spending over the next three to five years could go toward these foundational capabilities (agent platforms, communication protocols, real-time data access, modern security and observability frameworks), with agent-running spend potentially growing to as much as half of total technology budgets over time — economics Bain judges favorable overall, provided investment stays tightly focused on a small number of business domains generating early, visible value rather than spreading thin across the enterprise from the start; South American bank WhatsApp-based real-time PIX payments (where customers simply photograph or describe a payment and an agent interprets, confirms, and authorizes it conversationally) illustrate the kind of agent-as-first-class-citizen experience this focused investment can unlock. <span class="src">Building the Foundation for Agentic AI</span>

McKinsey QuantumBlack's complementary research, grounded in building its own Agents at Scale platform and engagements with three specific financial institutions, addresses the same underlying question from the angle of platform design discipline rather than organizational readiness. All three case studies illustrate agentic deployment already operating at real scale: a European bank automating credit-application write-ups to free up more than 20 percent of relationship managers' time, with reusable "atomic agents" built to scale across personal lending, KYC, risk, and software development; a large financial-services firm running a "digital factory" of agents working alongside more than 100 human developers daily, accelerating code and architectural-requirement work by 50 percent across Cobol and Java modernization; and a global bank reinventing its software development lifecycle entirely around agent workflows, running daily sprint cycles where a "factory of agents" works through the night while humans review and plan by day. The core architectural challenge these deployments share is that agentic applications span the enterprise, each with bespoke needs but also relying on capabilities that should be shared across systems — standards, evaluation, observability, security, protocols — meaning an integrated platform architecture has to function as connective "glue" spanning three layers: existing in-house systems and data, externally purchased agentic services (since the breadth of agentic use cases means a single vendor solution rarely covers every need), and selectively custom-built capabilities for genuinely bespoke requirements. The recommended design philosophy is "composable and compostable": composable means assembling modular building blocks into complex systems rather than building monolithically from scratch, and compostable means individual components can be swapped out and replaced without redesigning the whole system — a discipline meant to capture immediate value and market position while minimizing lock-in and accumulated technical debt as the underlying agentic technology keeps evolving rapidly. Operationalizing this discipline runs through a standard build/partner/buy decision hierarchy: buy ready-built solutions wherever clear market or industry approaches already exist, since this accelerates deployment, reduces development risk, and tends to track industry direction (with a preference for modular, interoperable, ideally open-source options); partner where buying isn't yet a viable option, since the enterprise-grade agentic solutions category is still emerging and most current whitespace won't remain unaddressed for long, aligning with either technology front-runners or providers actively building integrated holistic solutions to share risk, cost, and reward; and build selectively only where genuine differentiation is possible — most often where functionality is highly specific to a particular industry, region, or competitive context — or where an organization deliberately prioritizes greater control over a capability expected to power most of its critical processes, particularly in areas where the market's future direction is genuinely hard to predict and avoiding lock-in to a single major vendor becomes a real strategic concern. In practice, most platform components end up purchased, with custom building reserved mainly for configuration, integration, and orchestration work, plus the specific elements that create genuine strategic differentiation or preserve control an organization judges worth retaining. <span class="src">Creating a future-proof enterprise agentic platform architecture</span>

Three design principles emerged from these engagements as consistently useful for building genuinely composable and compostable platforms. Being relentlessly protocol-focused for interoperability means treating standardized agent-to-agent and agent-to-tool communication as a prerequisite, not a nice-to-have, for multivendor workflows: of more than 20 competing protocols, two have emerged as the leading contenders with no consensus yet on which will ultimately dominate — Model Context Protocol (MCP), which gives agents secure runtime access to external tools and data with strong modularity and reusability, and Agent2Agent (A2A), which enables direct coordination between agents across vendors, supporting cross-platform ecosystems and reducing reliance on any single vendor — used alongside conventional public, partner, and internal APIs that agents can access either directly or through MCP. Because both protocols keep evolving rapidly, engineering and operations teams need genuine familiarity with their lower-level mechanics so security controls and scalability approaches can be layered on top correctly; standardized approaches to both intercommunication and deliberate context isolation (limiting what agents share with each other, to avoid noisy, less-predictable outputs) let software teams retain the ability to migrate workflows to other providers with minimal effort, replace parts of a workflow as market solutions mature without full redesign, and dynamically reprovision critical agents onto different platforms to maintain business continuity during outages, as recent AWS and Azure incidents have demonstrated the real value of. Designing for production from the start addresses a specific, common failure mode: a recent McKinsey State of AI survey found fewer than 10 percent of organizations experimenting with AI agents are actually scaling them, with most initiatives stuck at proof-of-concept or requiring major rework because production requirements were treated as an afterthought rather than designed in from day one. The fix is establishing four architectural capabilities immediately, evolved iteratively through Agile principles rather than built waterfall-style: agentic evaluation, which functions like test-driven development to sustain quality and reduce unexpected behavior in non-deterministic multi-agent systems (one bank building metrics for latency, cost, output consistency using LLMs as judges, factual accuracy via hallucination/bias detection, and overall agent behavior via task-completion and path-convergence measures, on the Phoenix tracing and evaluation platform, complemented by subject-matter-expert qualitative review); marketplaces that make existing tools, agents, and workflows centrally discoverable and reusable, minimizing duplication as agentic systems scale (one bank's central Center of Excellence built a governed shared-component repository that significantly accelerated five subsequent modernization efforts by making teams check for existing solutions before building new ones); structured memory management combining short-term conversational continuity with long-term, database-backed preference and context persistence (an American bank's custom LangGraph/LangMem-based framework enabled seamless recall and personalization across sessions for employee-facing conversational agents, contributing to the same acceleration of subsequent modernization work); and continuous feedback mechanisms that keep agentic configurations aligned with evolving organizational goals. Constantly exploring new solutions and integration strategies acknowledges how nascent the agentic market genuinely still is: dedicating resources to ongoing R&D and experimentation yields two benefits — ongoing simplification, as market maturation lets fewer, more robust components replace earlier multi-agent, heavily-prompted setups (one bank found workflows that needed several heavily prompted agents a year earlier now run on a single, simpler agent, requiring disciplined version-linked prompt-and-specification libraries to manage the churn), and strengthening the platform's own capability through continued experimentation, illustrated by GraphRAG (pairing autonomous agents with knowledge graphs that preserve semantic relationships between entities, improving both retrieval precision and the auditability of deterministic graph traversals — one implementation mapped an entire IT ecosystem of servers, applications, and incident reports onto a knowledge graph that agents traverse via natural-language-to-Cypher translation to diagnose root causes and run predictive fault simulations) and workflow-engine integration (constraining agent autonomy inside deterministic process stages — explicit steps, validations, approvals, standardized timeout/retry/idempotency handling — which improves predictability and auditability without requiring a change to existing operating models, since agents can be reused across workflows much like non-agentic tools already are; one bank wrapped each agent in its SDLC into an Argo CD-orchestrated deterministic workflow with prescriptive, templated output formats at each stage, enabling direct hand-offs between agents without errors and reducing the need for intermediate human review in highly controlled processes like KYC and software engineering). <span class="src">Creating a future-proof enterprise agentic platform architecture</span>

BCG's "Harness Engineering" supplies a governance-focused counterpart to the platform-architecture disciplines above, addressing why agents that work in a demo still hallucinate, misreport task completion, or fail regulatory scrutiny once deployed: the fix is building an "operating system" around agents rather than expecting model quality alone to guarantee reliable behavior. As AI models commoditize, BCG argues competitive advantage shifts to whichever company builds the most effective harness — the layer capturing an organization's specific processes, ways of working, and requirements that competitors cannot simply purchase alongside the same off-the-shelf model. For a large Southeast Asian bank, a fully agentic personalized-advisory platform built with harness engineering (spanning human-assisted and digital channels) tripled the time advisors spent actively engaging clients, producing a 30%+ uplift in wealth-advisor revenue productivity and a four-to-fivefold improvement in customer conversion, alongside a fivefold increase in engineering efficiency and 50% faster design-to-deployment speed — gains BCG attributes to the harness's evaluation, approval, and behavioral-guardrail processes far more than to the underlying model choice, since this is precisely what let the platform deploy safely in a regulated banking environment. The harness has five components, each with a direct analog in a personal computer's operating system: specs function as the application manifest — a machine-testable scope contract defining an agent's purpose and the boundaries of what it may do, including core evaluation criteria; a constitution functions as the kernel — deterministic, non-negotiable rules specifying required and forbidden actions, escalation triggers, and individual accountability; a control panel functions as the process manager — giving humans visibility into what agents did, a compliant audit trail, a transparent escalation matrix, and failure-recovery options; a context hub functions as shared memory — a repository of the background information agents need to avoid confidently guessing when facing missing information (BCG identifies inadequate context as a leading cause of agent failure, sometimes causing ungoverned agents to reach out to external internet sources rather than admit uncertainty); and quality gates function as the system-call and permissions layer, distinguishing four genuinely different gate types (automated gates preventing programmatic failures, evaluation gates where specialized critic agents assess worker agents, human stage gates requiring a named individual's sign-off, and regulatory gates enforcing compliance standards) rather than treating all quality checks as interchangeable. A maturity ladder governs how much human oversight a given output type requires based on agreed organizational risk policy — a client-facing investment proposal might require human approval of every output, while a rough internal draft requires none — with content typically downgraded from "check everything" to "spot check now and then" as trust in the system's track record accumulates. BCG's practical starting guidance mirrors this concept's build/partner/buy discipline directly: start small (a harness has multiple interacting components that must be validated together in a real-world setting before scaling), build rather than buy the harness itself even while buying the underlying model (since it's precisely the harness — an organization's own process logic, quality signals, governance requirements, and institutional knowledge — that a competitor cannot replicate by purchasing the same model), and be selective about which workflows are ready for harness engineering, favoring processes where the work is already well documented, quality is checkable, and existing tools and systems can be readily connected. Regulated industries with well-documented standards (financial services, telecommunications) are especially good early fits, though BCG notes harness engineering also improves reliability in less regulated settings like software delivery — illustrated by a software company BCG helped transition from AI-assisted coding to a fully agentic-native product-development lifecycle, increasing team productivity as much as fivefold by extending harness engineering across the entire process from initial requirements through deployment and operations, disrupting the traditional junior-heavy workforce pyramid in favor of more design- and strategy-focused roles as agents absorb routine execution work under human supervision. <span class="src">Harness Engineering The Operating System for Agentic AI</span>

<span class="src">Four Urgent Questions for CIOs as AI Reshapes Every Function  Insights</span> (BCG Platinion, September 2026) supplies the CEO-facing framing for why the architectural disciplines above are urgent rather than optional: CEOs are already committing to multi-year AI "reshape" plans (demand-sensing agents, autonomous B2B pricing negotiation, AI-run finance closes), but the reshape stalls not because agents fail, but because "the company underneath them was built decades ago for human-led transactions, not agentic-led ones" — the same core-platform-modernization and data-access gaps this concept's Bain and McKinsey sources describe as architectural prerequisites. BCG Platinion crystallizes the platform choice CIOs face into two archetypes: a "curated ecosystem" built around a primary ERP vendor's tightly governed, agent-native environment (prioritizing compliance and reliability — the Apple-like route), or an "open platform" that exposes data and workflows to external agents and orchestration tools (accepting more heterogeneity for faster experimentation — the Android-like route), noting that major vendors are no longer interchangeable within a stack since Salesforce (orchestration-layer bet, open to multiple model providers), SAP (clean-S/4HANA-core bet), Oracle (bundled-infrastructure-plus-OpenAI bet), and Microsoft (Copilot-embedded-everywhere bet) are each pursuing genuinely different agentic strategies — while cautioning that committing to a direction is not the same as committing to a single vendor's roadmap forever, and that negotiating data portability and exit terms up front preserves the same optionality this concept's build/partner/buy discipline values. The article distills the reshape into four questions that function as funded-commitment checkpoints rather than aspirations: whether the organization has a single, agreed version of core information across every system (a specific program closing within 12 months); whether systems are designed for agents to read and write, not just for people to click (the deeper prize being that tacit company knowledge scattered in people's heads becomes something agents can draw on and add to); whether there is an explicit core-systems vendor strategy for the agent platform, rather than a stack assembled bottom-up tool by tool that inherits the integration debt of every individual choice; and whether agents have a defined identity within the security model — audit trails, authorization scopes, and accountability for agent-initiated actions — which BCG Platinion calls "the single highest risk gap in the reshape" when left unaddressed. The recommended sequencing (direction first, then data/integration foundation, then agent governance) and the recommendation to prove the pattern in finance first, where the multi-system reality is most visible and results are most measurable, both extend this concept's "start narrow, prove value, then scale" discipline from the technical platform layer to the C-suite decision sequence that has to authorize it. <span class="src">Four Urgent Questions for CIOs as AI Reshapes Every Function  Insights</span>

Probabilistic agent output breaks the identical-input-identical-output assumption behind regression testing and SLAs, so guarantees must become statistical (for example 95% of invocations within a tolerance band); persistent memory causes behavioural drift that must be detected against version-controlled baselines; and tool half-lives of months make unplanned refactoring costly.
