## Summary

Agentic AI is a structural shift in enterprise architecture, because agents can reason, collaborate and complete multistep, nondeterministic work end to end, while most current architectures cannot yet support thousands of them. The sources converge on a platform that is composable and compostable, built on open protocols such as MCP and A2A, a build, partner and buy hierarchy, designed for production from the start with evaluation, marketplaces, memory and feedback, and wrapped in a governance harness of specs, rules, audit trails, context and quality gates. Multi-agent systems add an orchestration challenge of role clarity, communication protocols and supervisory logic, and leaders must also choose a vendor posture, answer foundational data and identity questions and prevent agent sprawl from becoming the next technology legacy.

## Concept

The sources, which draw on hands-on enterprise work, agree that agentic AI is a structural shift in enterprise architecture rather than another wave of automation. Earlier automation handled discrete parts of a process and left exceptions to humans, whereas agents can reason, collaborate and coordinate actions to complete complex, multistep, nondeterministic work that historically relied on human judgment throughout. Bain describes two complementary roles, orchestrator agents that behave like project managers by breaking a goal into subtasks and tracking progress, and task agents that execute subtasks and report back so the orchestrator can compile results and adjust the workflow. Most organizations' current architectures cannot yet support agents deployed in the thousands, and the architecture must evolve along six principles. <span class="src">Building the Foundation for Agentic AI</span>

The first principle is to modernize the core platform, reworking batch-based systems to be API-accessible and responsive to real-time events so agents can find and use business capabilities, a change that must coexist with legacy infrastructure and adds near-term complexity. The second is interoperability, with consistent standards such as the Model Context Protocol across a mixed portfolio of custom agents, prebuilt vendor agents and dynamically generated agents, since frameworks themselves are becoming more agentic, as when a software-development agent coordinates specialized sub-agents for design, analysis, engineering and quality assurance. The third distributes accountability, keeping core platforms under a central team while business domains that own the expertise assemble, train, test, deploy and monitor their own agents. The fourth scales data access by building ingestion pipelines for unstructured sources such as documents, emails, recordings, images and call transcripts, where much agentic knowledge resides, as a European bank did by combining structured and unstructured data into a holistic customer view for personalized engagement marketing. <span class="src">Building the Foundation for Agentic AI</span>

The fifth principle updates governance and controls, with real-time explainability, behavioral observability and adaptive security to keep customer trust as agents make more decisions, plus disciplined management of volatile compute costs through dynamic resource allocation, edge deployment and AI-native financial operations. The sixth shifts the engineering paradigm, evolving software engineering and DevOps to manage agents' full lifecycle while agents take over more development and operations work, freeing engineers for architecture, strategy and higher-value innovation. Bain estimates that 5-10% of technology spending over three to five years could go to these foundations, including agent platforms, protocols, real-time data access and security and observability frameworks, with agent-running spend potentially growing to as much as half of technology budgets, economics it judges favorable if investment stays focused on a few domains that produce early visible value, as in a South American bank's conversational real-time payments over WhatsApp in which an agent interprets, confirms and authorizes a photographed or described payment. <span class="src">Building the Foundation for Agentic AI</span>

McKinsey QuantumBlack approaches the same question through platform design discipline, drawing on its Agents at Scale platform and three financial institutions. A European bank automated credit-application write-ups to free over 20% of relationship managers' time, using reusable atomic agents that scale across personal lending, KYC, risk and software development. A large financial-services firm runs a digital factory of agents alongside more than 100 human developers, accelerating code and architectural-requirement work by 50% across Cobol and Java modernization, and a global bank rebuilt its software development life cycle around agents, running daily sprints in which a factory of agents works overnight while humans review and plan by day. Because agentic applications span the enterprise yet rely on shared standards, evaluation, observability, security and protocols, an integrated platform must act as glue across three layers, existing in-house systems, externally purchased agentic services, since no single vendor covers every need, and selectively built custom capabilities. <span class="src">Creating a future-proof enterprise agentic platform architecture</span>

The recommended philosophy is composable and compostable, meaning modular building blocks assembled into complex systems, with components that can be replaced without redesigning the overall system, which secures value and market position now while limiting lock-in and technical debt as the technology evolves. It is operationalized through a build, partner and buy hierarchy. Buy ready-built solutions wherever clear market approaches exist, which accelerates deployment, reduces risk and tracks industry direction, favoring modular, interoperable and ideally open-source options. Partner where buying is not yet viable because the enterprise-grade category is still emerging and most whitespace will not stay open, aligning with front-runners or providers building integrated solutions to share risk, cost and reward. Build only where genuine differentiation is possible, typically in functionality specific to an industry, region or competitive context, or where an organization deliberately wants control over a capability expected to power most critical processes and wants to avoid lock-in to a single vendor. In practice most components are purchased, and building is reserved mainly for configuration, integration, orchestration and differentiating elements. <span class="src">Creating a future-proof enterprise agentic platform architecture</span>

Three design principles recur. Protocol focus treats standardized agent-to-agent and agent-to-tool communication as a prerequisite, and of more than 20 competing protocols two lead, the Model Context Protocol, which gives agents secure runtime access to tools and data with strong modularity, and Agent2Agent, which coordinates agents across vendors and reduces dependence on one vendor, both used alongside conventional public, partner and internal APIs that agents reach directly or through MCP. Standardized communication and deliberate context isolation, which limits what agents share to avoid noisy outputs, let teams migrate workflows, replace parts as markets mature and reprovision critical agents onto other platforms for continuity, as recent AWS and Azure outages showed, and since the protocols keep evolving, engineers need familiarity with their lower-level mechanics to layer security and scalability correctly. Designing for production from the start answers the finding that fewer than 10% of organizations experimenting with agents are scaling them, with most stuck at proof of concept or needing major rework because production needs were an afterthought. <span class="src">Creating a future-proof enterprise agentic platform architecture</span>

Four capabilities, built immediately and evolved iteratively, make that possible. Agentic evaluation works like test-driven development for non-deterministic multi-agent systems, as in one bank's metrics for latency, cost, consistency judged by LLMs, factual accuracy through hallucination and bias detection, and behavior through task-completion and path-convergence measures on the Phoenix platform, complemented by expert review. Marketplaces make tools, agents and workflows discoverable and reusable, and one bank's governed shared-component repository accelerated five later modernization efforts because teams had to check for existing solutions first. Structured memory combines short-term conversational continuity with long-term database-backed context, as an American bank's custom LangGraph and LangMem framework did for employee-facing agents, and continuous feedback keeps configurations aligned with changing goals. Ongoing research and experimentation add two benefits. Market maturation simplifies systems, since workflows that once needed several heavily prompted agents now run on one simpler agent, which demands version-linked prompt and specification libraries, and new capabilities strengthen the platform, such as GraphRAG, which pairs agents with knowledge graphs that preserve relationships for more precise, auditable retrieval, in one case mapping an entire IT ecosystem so that agents translate natural language to Cypher to diagnose root causes and simulate faults, and workflow engines, which constrain autonomy within deterministic stages with explicit validations, approvals and standard timeout, retry and idempotency handling, as when one bank wrapped each agent in an Argo CD-orchestrated workflow with templated outputs that reduced intermediate human review in controlled processes such as KYC and software engineering. <span class="src">Creating a future-proof enterprise agentic platform architecture</span>

BCG's harness engineering supplies the governance counterpart, addressing why agents that work in a demo still hallucinate, misreport completion or fail regulatory scrutiny in production. Its answer is an operating system around agents rather than reliance on model quality, and as models commoditize BCG argues that advantage shifts to whoever builds the most effective harness, the layer capturing an organization's processes and requirements that competitors cannot buy with the same model. For a large Southeast Asian bank, an agentic advisory platform built this way tripled advisors' time spent engaging clients, produced a 30%+ uplift in wealth-advisor revenue productivity and 4-5x better customer conversion, raised engineering efficiency fivefold and sped design-to-deployment by 50%, gains BCG attributes to the harness's evaluation, approval and behavioral guardrails far more than model choice, since that is what allowed safe deployment in regulated banking. <span class="src">Harness Engineering The Operating System for Agentic AI</span>

The harness has five components, each analogous to part of a personal computer's operating system. Specs act as the application manifest, a machine-testable scope contract defining an agent's purpose, limits and core evaluation criteria. A constitution acts as the kernel, with deterministic, non-negotiable rules on required and forbidden actions, escalation triggers and individual accountability. A control panel acts as the process manager, giving humans visibility into what agents did, a compliant audit trail, a transparent escalation matrix and failure recovery. A context hub acts as shared memory, since inadequate context is a leading cause of confident but wrong guesses, sometimes sending ungoverned agents to external internet sources instead of admitting uncertainty. Quality gates act as the permissions layer, distinguishing automated gates, evaluation gates in which critic agents assess worker agents, human stage gates requiring a named person's sign-off, and regulatory gates, and a maturity ladder sets how much oversight each output type needs under organizational risk policy, so that a client-facing investment proposal may need approval of every output while a rough internal draft needs none, with content moving from check everything to occasional spot checks as trust accumulates. <span class="src">Harness Engineering The Operating System for Agentic AI</span>

BCG's guidance mirrors the build, partner and buy logic. Start small because the interacting components must be validated together in a real setting, build the harness while buying the model, since the harness embodies the organization's own process logic, quality signals, governance requirements and institutional knowledge, and select workflows that are well documented, checkable and easy to connect, which makes regulated industries with documented standards, such as financial services and telecommunications, good early fits. The approach also improves reliability in less regulated settings, as when a software company BCG helped move from AI-assisted coding to a fully agentic-native lifecycle raised team productivity as much as fivefold by extending the harness from requirements through operations and disrupted the junior-heavy workforce pyramid in favor of design- and strategy-focused roles. <span class="src">Harness Engineering The Operating System for Agentic AI</span>

BCG Platinion explains why these disciplines are urgent, since CEOs are committing to multi-year reshape plans such as demand-sensing agents, autonomous B2B pricing negotiation and AI-run finance closes, but the reshape stalls because the company underneath was built decades ago for human-led transactions. CIOs face a platform choice between two archetypes, a curated ecosystem around a primary ERP vendor's tightly governed, agent-native environment that favors compliance and reliability, the Apple-like route, and an open platform that exposes data and workflows to external agents and orchestration tools and accepts more heterogeneity for faster experimentation, the Android-like route. Major vendors are no longer interchangeable, since Salesforce bets on the orchestration layer and multiple model providers, SAP on a clean S/4HANA core, Oracle on bundled infrastructure with OpenAI and Microsoft on Copilot embedded everywhere, and a direction is not a commitment to one vendor's roadmap forever, so data portability and exit terms should be negotiated up front, in line with the optionality that build, partner and buy values. <span class="src">Four Urgent Questions for CIOs as AI Reshapes Every Function  Insights</span>

The article distills the reshape into four questions that act as funded commitments. Does the organization have a single agreed version of core information across customers, products, suppliers and employees, through a program closing within 12 months. Are systems designed for agents to read and write rather than for people to click, so that tacit company knowledge held in people's heads becomes something agents can use and extend. Is there an explicit core-systems vendor strategy for the agent platform, rather than a stack assembled tool by tool that inherits every choice's integration debt. And do agents have a defined identity within the security model, with audit trails, authorization scopes and accountability, which BCG Platinion calls the single highest risk gap in the reshape since most systems cannot answer who owns an entry when an agent posts it. It recommends sequencing direction first, then the data and integration foundation, then agent governance, and proving the pattern first in finance, where the multi-system reality is most visible and results most measurable. <span class="src">Four Urgent Questions for CIOs as AI Reshapes Every Function  Insights</span>

BCG's operations-focused article warns that the next technology legacy may be created by agent sprawl. Business-led agent building and an engineering-productivity paradox could produce more than 100,000 new technology assets in a 2028 enterprise, with 300-400 core processes, up to 1,300 subprocesses and five to seven agents each, and BCG gives CIOs six to twelve months to retrofit governance, with only about 15% of companies having the backbone in place. Probabilistic output breaks the identical-input, identical-output assumption behind regression testing and service-level agreements, so guarantees must become statistical, for example 95% of invocations within a tolerance band, persistent memory causes behavioral drift that must be detected against version-controlled baselines, tool half-lives of months make unplanned refactoring costly, and a fully agentic workflow can consume around a million tokens a day, with costs able to shift overnight on one vendor's pricing decision. The four pillars are GenAI platform services as a federated control fabric, graduation pathways with machine-readable agent IDs carrying owner, telemetry and runbooks, continuous refactoring, and IT service management adapted from the software development life cycle to an agent development life cycle with guardrails as code, run through a federated, business-unit-led model so the central team does not become a shared-service bottleneck, with the platform team acting as an immune system that sweeps for shadow IT and cost drift. <span class="src">Scaling Agents Without Creating Your Next Tech Legacy</span>

As organizations move beyond isolated agents, multi-agentic systems, in which several agents interact, share data and act, often without direct human oversight, pose a coordination challenge. PwC illustrates it with a smart complaints-management system in which a classification agent detects category, urgency and sentiment in incoming complaints, a routing and escalation agent sends each case to self-service, a human or immediate escalation by severity and customer profile, a resolution agent trained on past cases recommends fixes and learns from feedback, and a sentiment-monitoring agent flags open cases at risk of escalating or triggering regulatory concern. Three forces drive adoption, the volume and velocity of enterprise data that outpaces centralized models and favors acting closer to the source, the need for agility that static monolithic models cannot meet, and emergence, in which agents interacting in complex environments generate novel solutions and become co-creative partners rather than mere automation. <span class="src">Mastering multi-agent AI systems for competitive advantage</span>

PwC argues that the central challenge is organizational design as much as technical integration, and that leaders should orchestrate multi-agentic systems as they would build a high-performing team. Role clarity gives each agent clear boundaries, responsibilities and escalation paths, communication protocols give agents standard ways to share state, make joint decisions and resolve conflicts, serving agent interoperability as APIs serve system interoperability, and supervisory logic adds a human or algorithmic meta-layer that detects anomalies, prioritizes competing actions and applies ethical or compliance filters in real time. Without these, straightforward automation can degrade into misalignment or untraceable decision pathways with real consequences, and three failure modes follow, namely coordination complexity, with duplicated work, cross-purpose action and unintended feedback loops, trust and explainability gaps, which are most dangerous in regulated industries where autonomous decisions must be auditable to regulators and stakeholders, and integration-at-scale friction as a proof of concept meets existing workflows, data environments and control systems. <span class="src">Mastering multi-agent AI systems for competitive advantage</span>

PwC's three must-do strategies are to design with intent, since adding agents without architectural purpose is like adding musicians to an orchestra without a conductor, to balance autonomy with control by explicitly defining how much independence agents receive, especially in finance, legal and public-trust domains, and to invest in explainability and governance from day one so that systems are auditable, traceable and aligned with corporate governance rather than retrofitted. For a first step it recommends a strategic agentic-readiness assessment that finds where decisions slow because of organizational fragmentation and where semi-autonomous coordination could create measurable value, then a controlled pilot with a small constellation of agents under clear interaction rules, evaluated for emergent behavior as well as task performance, treating the orchestration layer as something to iterate on rather than a design fixed at the outset. <span class="src">Mastering multi-agent AI systems for competitive advantage</span>
