## Summary

The infrastructure telecom operators need before real-time, AI-driven network automation becomes possible — better data handling, cloud-native architecture, and rethought compute, networking, and storage. Drawn from Patrick Lopez's six-part AI-native telco roadmap.

## Concept

The starting premise is that AI applications in telecom are not new — predictive maintenance and customer analytics have used AI for years — but the ambition behind "AI-native" networks is qualitatively different: it requires processing vast amounts of real-time network data with enough speed and precision to enable dynamic network optimization, automation, and, eventually, fully autonomous network operation, rather than the periodic, batch-style AI applications that came before. Achieving this is framed not as a matter of simply adopting new AI tools, but of building an entirely different data, infrastructure, and expertise foundation first — the roadmap's central claim is that AI has to become embedded in the network's operational "DNA" rather than layered on top of an unchanged architecture. <span class="src">Strategic Approach for Building AI-Native Telecom Networks</span>

Two prerequisites are treated as foundational, ahead of any specific AI capability: data handling and cloud-native architecture. Data handling means collecting, normalizing, and cleaning data drawn from complex, multi-vendor telecom environments — a real technical challenge in telecom specifically because operators typically run equipment and software from many different vendors with inconsistent data formats and conventions, and AI systems trained or run against unreliable or inconsistent inputs produce unreliable outputs regardless of how sophisticated the model itself is. Cloud-native architecture — open interfaces, APIs, and telecom-specific virtualization and softwarization technologies including Network Functions Virtualization (NFV), Software-Defined Networking (SDN), and Open RAN — is the second prerequisite, because AI-driven automation needs programmable, API-accessible network functions to actually act on; without this layer, an AI system might correctly diagnose a network problem but have no standardized way to actually intervene and fix it. <span class="src">Strategic Approach for Building AI-Native Telecom Networks</span>

Beyond these two foundational layers, the roadmap calls for rethinking three specific infrastructure domains. Compute needs GPUs specifically for AI/ML workloads, deployed with a strategic split: model training — the more compute-intensive, less latency-sensitive workload — placed in private clouds, while inference — the latency-sensitive workload that actually has to respond to live network conditions — is placed at the network edge, close to where decisions need to be acted on in real time. Networking needs to be upgraded with SmartNICs (network interface cards capable of offloading and accelerating data processing directly), high-bandwidth Ethernet (100GbE or 400GbE), and low-latency interconnect protocols like InfiniBand or RDMA over Converged Ethernet (RoCE), because the volume and time-sensitivity of data that AI/ML workloads move can easily overwhelm conventional networking infrastructure not built with this specific traffic pattern in mind. Storage needs to shift to high-performance flash storage with object-storage capabilities, chosen specifically for its ability to scale and to operate coherently across hybrid and multicloud environments rather than being tied to a single infrastructure location. <span class="src">Strategic Approach for Building AI-Native Telecom Networks</span>

Alongside these physical infrastructure changes, the roadmap names two organizational prerequisites. First, operators need to build internal data literacy and AI expertise, specifically framed as necessary to manage proprietary datasets effectively — implying that infrastructure alone, without the internal capability to actually work with the resulting data and models, doesn't translate into functioning AI-native operations. Second, and connected to this, operators are advised to focus on developing proprietary AI: smaller, in-house models purpose-built for their own specific network conditions and needs, rather than depending entirely on third-party general-purpose AI vendors — the stated rationale is retaining control over where data is stored, how it's processed, and how models are trained, rather than ceding that control to an external vendor's infrastructure and roadmap. This includes a practical validation discipline: any vendor AI solution being considered should be tested and tuned against the operator's own real production data before being trusted in production, rather than accepted on the strength of the vendor's own performance claims — a discipline directly relevant given how often AI vendor claims fail to hold up under independent scrutiny (see [ai risk prediction tool failures](#/concept/ai-risk-prediction-tool-failures)). <span class="src">Strategic Approach for Building AI-Native Telecom Networks</span>

The roadmap closes with an explicit expectation-management point: putting these prerequisites in place — data handling, cloud-native architecture, rethought compute/networking/storage, internal expertise, and proprietary AI capability — takes significant time and sustained effort before it produces tangible benefits like measurable cost savings. This is presented as a genuine strategic transformation spanning multiple layers of the network stack, not an incremental technology upgrade that can be scoped and delivered quickly. <span class="src">Strategic Approach for Building AI-Native Telecom Networks</span>
