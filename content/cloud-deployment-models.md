## Summary

The public, private, hybrid, multicloud, community, and government cloud deployment models. Covers what distinguishes a genuinely hybrid or multicloud environment from merely using several cloud services, how the traditional data center's role is changing rather than disappearing, and Bain's market data on why hybrid has become the practical default.

## Concept

Cloud computing is defined functionally as the shared, standardized, and automated delivery of computing resources — applications, compute, storage, networking, development/deployment platforms, and even business processes — as a service, rather than by any single deployment model. Standardization (consistent interfaces) and automation (rules-based provisioning and de-provisioning triggered by business rules, resource availability, and security demands) are what make self-service possible at all; without them, "cloud" would just be remotely hosted infrastructure. Multi-tenancy is the specific architectural pattern that makes public cloud economics work: many customers share the same underlying infrastructure and software instance while their individual configurations and data remain isolated, which is what lets a provider avoid replicating dedicated hardware or software per customer. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.1 Understanding the Cloud</span>

The two foundational deployment models — public and private — differ primarily in ownership, access, and control rather than in underlying technology. A public cloud is owned and operated by a third party for use by others, is highly scalable, and hides the underlying infrastructure from the consumer; some public cloud customers pay a premium for dedicated, non-multi-tenant instances when governance or compliance rules forbid sharing infrastructure, even though they still share some networking. A private cloud is owned and operated by (or exclusively for) one organization, sits behind a firewall, and is built around governance, security, and compliance rather than around openness; increasingly, public cloud vendors sell appliance versions of their own services (AWS Outposts is the example given, alongside GCP-HPE and Microsoft on-premises partnerships) that can be installed inside a customer's own data center, blurring the line between "private cloud" and "public cloud technology run locally." Beyond this basic pair, public clouds further subdivide into commercial public clouds (built for professional/business use and backed by a service level agreement — though SLAs are described as typically written to protect the vendor rather than the customer, with carve-outs for the kinds of outages that account for many real-world incidents), open social community clouds (e.g. Facebook, LinkedIn — free to join, low security, no SLA, and no guarantee the community or its content will persist, since the vendor is contractually accountable to advertisers rather than end users), open technical community clouds (e.g. GitHub — nonproprietary platforms built around open-source collaboration), and government clouds (segregated public-cloud variants meeting the stricter isolation, vetting, and no-shared-infrastructure requirements that follow from the fact that a government data breach can escalate into an international incident, not merely a business one). Private clouds similarly subdivide into privately owned and managed clouds (where a company effectively becomes its own cloud provider — illustrated by a financial services firm running a stringent, indemnified SLA-backed service for partner banks), appliances that replicate a public vendor's service set behind a customer's own firewall, and commercial contractual private clouds operated by a third party under formal SLA for customers who want private-cloud governance without building and running it themselves. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.7 Introducing All Types of Clouds</span>

Hybrid and multicloud are frequently used loosely, but the source draws a precise line: a hybrid cloud specifically requires that private and public (or on-premises and cloud) resources work together toward a shared goal — for instance, a public development platform sending data to a private-cloud or data-center application, or a SaaS analytics platform ingesting data from multiple cloud sources. Using a public cloud for a fully disconnected prototype, using a SaaS application with no data flow back to the data center, or having different divisions each independently standardized on their own single public cloud, are all explicitly *not* hybrid by this definition, even though they involve both public and private resources somewhere in the company. Multicloud, similarly, is not itself a type of cloud but a strategic pattern: using two or more public clouds (often layered with private/hybrid resources) in a coordinated way, motivated by developer choice, vendor risk diversification, cost or performance arbitrage, and resilience against any single vendor's pricing or reliability changes. Its long-run aspirational endpoint — automated, near-instantaneous movement of live, running workloads between cloud providers based on policy (cost, performance, outage response) — is explicitly framed as not yet realized as of this source, constrained by immature multicloud management tooling and by application portability limits, though container technology is identified as the enabling trend making that vision progressively more achievable. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.1 Understanding the Cloud</span> <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.7 Introducing All Types of Clouds</span>

Cloud delivery itself is organized in three nested layers, each built on the one beneath it: Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS) delivers a virtual server equivalent to a physical one — an operating system plus everything the customer builds on top — available in both public (pay-as-you-go, self-service) and private (policy-gated, internally provisioned) forms; Platform as a Service (PaaS) combines IaaS with abstracted middleware, development tooling, and deployment services so the underlying operating system is hidden from developers entirely, letting some platforms (Salesforce's Customer 360 Platform is the example) become a PaaS layer that partners build entire industry-specific applications on top of; and Software as a Service (SaaS) is a complete, multi-tenant business application (Salesforce, Google's G Suite, Adobe Creative Cloud, Zendesk, and similar are cited) that sits on top of both, where the customer cares only about functionality, performance, availability, and security, not the infrastructure or platform underneath. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.1 Understanding the Cloud</span>

Finally, the rise of the cloud does not eliminate the traditional data center; most medium and large companies continue to run one, particularly for systems of record such as accounting, inventory, and core line-of-business applications, and much of a data center's ongoing cost goes to maintaining legacy hardware diversity, facilities, and staff rather than to the workloads themselves. Server virtualization — decoupling software from hardware by running it in virtual machines — has been the main lever companies use to streamline data centers, and many organizations deliberately evolve their virtualized data center into a private cloud as a controlled intermediate stage: a place to get virtual-machine sprawl under control, make applications cloud-ready, and test workloads safely before moving them to the public cloud, so that even after a migration to public cloud, the leftover modernized private cloud becomes the foundation of an ongoing hybrid environment. Data sovereignty — the principle that digitally stored data is legally subject to the jurisdiction of the country where it physically resides — is highlighted as a specific and durable (not merely transitional) reason certain workloads and data remain in a physical data center or private cloud regardless of how mature public cloud security has become, alongside legacy applications with dependencies that simply aren't cloud-ready and cases where no reasonable business case exists for migration. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.1 Understanding the Cloud</span> A related, more granular cost driver behind the broader migration to cloud and virtualized infrastructure is that traditional data centers are commonly overprovisioned well beyond genuine peak demand as an outage-risk hedge, which inflates energy costs disproportionately to the compute capacity actually used — a specific, quantifiable inefficiency that cloud and virtualization architectures are designed to eliminate through elastic, on-demand resource allocation. <span class="src">Management Strategies for the Cloud Revolution</span>

Real-world market data backs up why hybrid, rather than a pure public-versus-private choice, has become the practical default. A 2019 Bain analysis found public cloud had decisively pulled ahead of private cloud commercially — worldwide public cloud revenue was projected to exceed $275 billion by 2021, versus at most $75 billion for private cloud — but Bain's research suggests this dominance reflects practical trade-offs more than genuine preference: many enterprise customers would actually prefer a private solution, but embrace public cloud because it's less expensive, more feature-rich, and easier to integrate, while private cloud is frequently too costly or too difficult to manage internally to justify on its own merits. Given how far public cloud has pulled ahead, Bain finds the real competitive question for private cloud vendors is no longer whether to compete with public cloud head-on, but how to win through hybrid offerings instead — the market has moved past the point where an all-private pitch works even for customers in highly regulated industries, pushing vendors toward emphasizing ease of integration with public cloud services rather than private cloud's traditional isolation advantages. Security occupies an unusual dual role in this shift, functioning as both a persistent concern and, increasingly, an active motivator for public cloud adoption: some executives believe public cloud providers can protect their environment against breaches and data-privacy failures more effectively than their own on-premises operations could manage alone, even though security nominally remains one of the top-cited worries about moving to the cloud at all. Genuine disappointments persist alongside these gains, and they aren't evenly distributed: cost is customers' single largest source of disappointment, with expected savings frequently failing to materialize (a pattern echoed in Bain's separate finding that direct-match "lift and shift" migrations of overprovisioned workloads can actually increase costs), and dissatisfaction with compliance and regulatory issues rose roughly 50 percent between 2016 and 2018 — even as satisfaction with security and reliability specifically improved over that same period, reinforcing that different aspects of the cloud value proposition are maturing at genuinely different rates rather than uniformly. <span class="src">Public vs. Private Cloud The Market Says Hybrid</span>
