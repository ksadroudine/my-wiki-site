## Summary

McKinsey's research-based playbook for taking telecom AI from isolated pilots to enterprise-wide value. Covers the seven-element scaling framework (domain-level workflow targeting, a centralized AI platform, data foundations, change management, operating model, partnerships, risk management), the five-type agent taxonomy, the "AI factory" model for standardizing agentic deployment, a capital-expenditure-specific application using AI simulation to optimize network investment ROI, and two named-operator case studies — KPN's agentic customer-care build and AT&T's governance-led AI-fueled coding program — that illustrate the framework in practice.

## Concept

McKinsey's telco-focused AI research, tracked across successive annual C-suite surveys, documents both accelerating adoption and a persistent gap between adoption and realized value. Reported impact roughly doubled year over year (from about 25% to about 50% of executives "currently capturing impact"), but a later, more granular survey found only 12% of respondents had captured truly sizable impact, with the majority still caught in what the research calls "the money step" — having made real AI investments that have spurred activity and enthusiasm but not yet translated into measurable balance-sheet benefit. This gap is attributed less to the technology itself than to how telcos are deploying it: nearly three-quarters of surveyed leaders report using AI to support only minimal or moderate redesigns of existing processes, rather than fundamentally rethinking how work should be done in an AI-first environment, which means AI often ends up accelerating legacy ways of working rather than delivering the deeper improvements in cost, speed, and effectiveness that a genuine workflow redesign would unlock. <span class="src">Scaling the AI-native telco</span> <span class="src">Telcos’ AI inflection point</span>

Seven elements recur across McKinsey's research as the difference between telcos that scale AI successfully and those that remain stuck running isolated pilots. First, targeting end-to-end domain workflows rather than individual use cases: a single gen-AI use case (say, automatically personalized customer messages) rarely moves a headline metric like churn on its own, but combining several related use cases into one coordinated solution (an audiencing tool, a unified churn model, a real-time next-best-action engine, and automated testing, in one telco's case) can transform an entire workflow and deliver materially better outcomes — the same telco that did this achieved the lowest churn in its national market. Second, building a centralized, modular AI platform rather than letting AI teams build in silos: one North American telco built a platform of roughly fifty reusable services and cut new-use-case build time from months down to about two weeks, while also ensuring consistent architecture and shared learnings across teams — the alternative, illustrated by a case where customer-care and network-operations teams independently built near-identical RAG-based knowledge-management tools without knowing about each other, wastes effort and introduces inconsistent governance. Third, implementing adequate data foundations spanning both structured and unstructured data (contracts, transcripts, technical blueprints, annotations, often scattered across decades of legacy documentation), since data limitations are cited by 30% of executives as a core inhibitor of AI impact today and by 45% as the core inhibitor they expect specifically for scaling AI agents going forward. Fourth, driving adoption through change management built into a use case's design from the very start rather than added afterward — telcos are large, complex organizations with long employee tenure (7.5 years on average, nearly double other industries) and correspondingly high organizational inertia, so engagement plans built in close collaboration with frontline employees and managers, reinforced through structured coaching and adoption tracking, are what actually convert AI capability into realized productivity gains. Fifth, developing a genuine AI operating model and talent strategy that resolves the disconnect between AI development teams, business-unit leaders, and CIO/IT teams — roughly 85% of surveyed telcos report that key AI decisions are made by disconnected teams, which routinely produces missed opportunities for business-backed features and poor integration into existing systems. Sixth, building a genuine AI partnership ecosystem rather than treating vendors as mere suppliers, since keeping pace with AI's evolution in-house is difficult even for telcos with relatively advanced IT organizations, and hyperscaler and specialized AI partners can augment internal data science and developer capacity while helping build telco-specific capability that general-purpose foundation models (whose training data may lack telecom-specific content) don't natively have. Seventh, managing regulatory and ethical risk deliberately rather than as an afterthought — establishing information-security guardrails and a responsible-AI framework covering bias, data governance, and privacy, on the logic that trust, not just capability, is what ultimately determines whether telcos can actually monetize the rich customer and network data they hold. <span class="src">Scaling the AI-native telco</span>

Agentic AI specifically is organized into five architectural forms, useful for telco leaders scoping what kind of agent a given use case actually calls for: simple reflex agents act on preset rules, similar to existing next-best-action systems (e.g. automatically sending a bill-shock warning as a customer nears their data limit); utility-based agents independently weigh several possible actions and choose among them based on context (an email-handling agent deciding whether to auto-reply to an unhappy customer or draft a response for a human rep, depending on the message's tone and severity); goal-based agents work toward an explicit target, like a recommender agent optimizing upsell offers against both customer usage patterns and revenue goals; hierarchical agents decompose a complex task into an ordered set of manageable subtasks, such as an agent that first researches and then drafts a financial report; and multi-agent systems combine multiple autonomous agents working independently or cooperatively toward a shared goal, such as a centralized employee assistant that answers queries, makes recommendations, and auto-completes field notes across several network-related tasks at once. <span class="src">Scaling the AI-native telco</span>

To operationalize scaling at enterprise level, McKinsey's more recent research introduces the "AI factory" model: a common, reusable playbook of platforms, tools, and reference architectures — shared agent frameworks, common model environments, standardized governance controls — built once and reused across the organization rather than reinvented for every new project. Without this kind of standardization, teams tend to independently adopt different tools and design patterns, producing fragmented implementations that are hard to govern, raise technical debt and inference/infrastructure costs over time, and introduce material risk around security, compliance, and data governance. An AI factory's core components include a structured intake process that assesses and prioritizes candidate use cases (in some telcos, an AI agent itself assesses readiness and recommends whether a use case should proceed to proof-of-concept, scale, or be deprioritized) and a library of shared, reusable components (proven propensity-model blueprints, enablement tooling) that let teams avoid re-solving the same problem for every new use case. The underlying philosophy connecting the AI-factory model to the seven-element scaling framework is the same: redesigning workflows genuinely from scratch, around only the "necessary steps," eliminates entire stages, handoffs, and coordination layers that incremental AI-on-top-of-existing-process deployments leave untouched — the difference between telcos that unlock sustained improvements in cost, cycle time, and output quality, and telcos where AI merely shifts a bottleneck rather than removing it. This scale of workflow redesign has direct workforce implications: McKinsey Global Institute research cited in this material estimates roughly 30% of telecom workforce hours could realistically be automated by 2030, but a far larger 91% of all sector jobs will require fundamental redesign and rethinking to make the most of that automation — meaning the dominant effect is role redefinition (new positions like AI workflow architect and AI risk manager emerging alongside declines in traditional DevOps, product marketing, and customer-support roles), not simple headcount reduction. Underlying the entire framework is a discipline about accountability: leading telcos explicitly tie AI initiatives to budget line items rather than treating them as a separate innovation exercise — "if AI isn't in the budget, it isn't real" — rigorously reviewing budgets to identify where AI can deliver measurable, benchmarked impact and adjusting division-level budgets to reinforce accountability for actually delivering it. <span class="src">Telcos’ AI inflection point</span>

<span class="src">Pushing telcos' AI envelope on capital decisions</span> (McKinsey, Brendan Gaffey, February 2025) applies this concept's general scaling logic to one specific, capital-intensive domain: network investment decisions. Telco capital expenditure has grown faster than revenue for a decade — invested capital up more than 77% in the US and roughly 23% globally — while data-traffic growth is slowing (from over 30% down toward 10% in some markets), which together open a genuine path toward lower capital intensity, but only for telcos that can tell which specific planned investments actually move the needle on customer-perceived network experience rather than simply hitting a generic technical threshold like 50 Mbps nationwide. Building on telcos' existing data and AI maturity (digital twins, cloud IT infrastructure, customer satisfaction scoring), the article describes simulating the customer-experience impact of specific network interventions — new macro sites, spectrum additions, hardware upgrades, backhaul changes — before committing capital, starting with a baseline simulation of how customer satisfaction will deteriorate under current traffic-growth trends with no further investment, then layering in specific candidate interventions to see which ones actually close that gap. Telcos applying this approach have optimized capital expenditure plans by 10-15%, repurposed as much as 25% of planned spend toward higher-value investments, and achieved improved ROI within as little as 12 months; one operator that simulated outcomes across more than 1,000 planned interventions found a "long tail" comprising more than 10% of its total capital plan whose customer-satisfaction impact was only about one-seventh the size of its top interventions — capital that pure engineering judgment, anchored to uniform national performance thresholds, had never identified as low-value. The article's practical guidance echoes this concept's own emphasis on data foundations and governance: simulations require customer experience scores as a prerequisite, must be scoped carefully given the significant compute cost of running them at scale, and increasingly draw the CEO and CFO (not just the CTO) into capital-allocation governance, since better visibility into the revenue impact of network decisions changes who needs a seat at that particular table. <span class="src">Pushing telcos' AI envelope on capital decisions</span>

Two named-operator case studies illustrate this concept's abstract framework in concrete deployment terms. <span class="src">How KPN is building an agentic AI engine for customer care</span> documents KPN's build of an agentic voice-AI customer-care system spanning roughly five million calls a year, working with McKinsey and QuantumBlack. KPN's process traces the seven-element framework's early steps almost exactly: it used a custom LLM framework to mine call transcripts and chat logs to identify its highest-impact opportunities (customer verification, order status, technician appointment management, connection troubleshooting), weighing not just cost and complexity but where humans needed to stay in the loop (churn-risk moments like a customer moving residence). On the technical foundation, KPN prioritized conversational experience metrics over technology choice — targeting sub-2-second response latency and "barge-in" support for natural interruption — and built a reusable, three-layer architecture (agent orchestration, agent platform, audio integration) specifically so future use cases could be added without rebuilding from scratch, connecting agents directly to core telco systems via standardized APIs and MCP rather than limiting them to answering questions. Deployment discipline mirrored this concept's change-management emphasis: daily review cycles examined up to 100 real customer-call transcripts each morning, translating surfaced edge cases into updated prompts released the same evening, while a dedicated task force updated procedures and trained employees as their roles shifted toward higher-complexity work — yielding an 86% adoption success rate and a company target of having agentic AI handle 10-20% of customer service calls by 2027. <span class="src">How KPN is building an agentic AI engine for customer care</span>

<span class="src">Forward-looking leadership How this telecom giant puts AI into action</span> (PwC's account of AT&T, via Mobile World Congress) illustrates this concept's governance-and-ROI-discipline elements at much larger scale. AT&T's Chief Data and AI Officer, Andy Markus, credits the company's ability to move past pure experimentation into genuine scaling to three practices this concept's framework also emphasizes: formal ROI business cases for every candidate use case, with resources concentrated on the highest-value ones rather than spread thin ("focus beats volume"); embedding AI into existing workflows rather than layering it onto legacy processes, which required redesigning the underlying processes AI would touch, not just adding a tool on top; and involving risk organizations early, since AT&T has observed peers across industries fail specifically because "the risk organizations are brought in late," killing viable ideas after the fact rather than shaping them from the start. At AT&T's scale — over 100,000 employees on its "Ask AT&T" generative/agentic platform, generating more than 3 billion production API calls and tens of billions of tokens a day — the company has moved to what Markus calls "AI-fueled coding," a disciplined, iterative alternative to one-shot "vibe coding" (which he calls "the death spiral of an enterprise"): the approach has cut the time to build governed data products from four-to-six weeks down to as little as 30 minutes, with architect code review in some cases requiring zero changes. AT&T pairs this with two structured enablement programs — "Permit to Learn," a sandboxed environment where employees can practice building agentic workflows on real company data without touching production systems, and "Level Up," a developer education and metrics program — reflecting this concept's broader finding that adoption requires deliberately engineered enablement infrastructure, not just capable technology. <span class="src">Forward-looking leadership How this telecom giant puts AI into action</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source provides a detailed cost breakdown for building an AI factory from scratch versus the ongoing savings it generates; the "months to two weeks" build-time reduction example is the only concrete efficiency figure captured here.
- The capital-decisions source doesn't specify how the capex-simulation approach's own compute cost compares to the capital savings it generates, beyond noting the cost is "significant" and that simulation scope must be deliberately narrowed to priority sites.
- Neither the KPN nor the AT&T case study reports a cost figure for the underlying platform build itself, so it's not possible to compare either company's actual ROI against the framework's own capex-simulation guidance or against each other.
