## Summary

Apple's manufacturing excellence traces back through an unbroken chain to a nearly forgotten American engineer who taught postwar Japan the quality-management philosophy Steve Jobs later absorbed at NeXT and Pixar — and once Jobs returned to Apple, the company weaponized those same quality lessons into a supply-chain playbook that extracted manufacturing know-how from Japanese suppliers and, in the process, helped build China's industrial capacity into a strategic rival.

## Concept

The FT's two-part investigation traces Apple's manufacturing excellence — conventionally credited entirely to Steve Jobs' product vision and Jony Ive's industrial design — back through an unbroken, decades-long chain of knowledge transfer that begins not in Silicon Valley or southern China but in war-devastated postwar Japan. In 1945, General Douglas MacArthur, tasked with demilitarizing and rebuilding occupied Japan, faced an immediate practical problem: Japan's communications industry was so shattered he could barely issue commands. He dispatched Homer Sarasohn, a 33-year-old American physicist and radar engineer, to rebuild it. Sarasohn found no facilities, no experienced managers, and — after probing further — concluded the country's shoddy manufacturing quality stemmed not from a technical deficiency but a managerial and cultural one: when he asked employees how to improve quality, they murmured among themselves trying to guess what answer would please him rather than answering directly, reflecting a deeply ingrained deference to authority rather than genuine problem-solving engagement. In response, Sarasohn and a colleague, Charles Protzman, spent a month writing a rigorous industrial-management textbook and designed a compulsory eight-week course for Japan's top managers, opening with the deceptively simple question "why does any company exist?" and teaching that quality had to be understood as "a total system" — a "guiding state of mind" diffused throughout an organization and empowering the workers actually closest to production, rather than a specialized inspection function bolted onto the end of a process. When Sarasohn left Japan in 1950, he recommended two successors who would go on to become the most celebrated names in the 20th-century quality movement — W. Edwards Deming (so influential that the Union of Japanese Scientists and Engineers named its top quality award after him) and Joseph Juran (later honored personally by Emperor Hirohito) — while Sarasohn himself faded into near-total obscurity, despite his seminars still being taught in Japan a quarter-century after he left and despite personally training a roster of executives from Fujitsu, Hitachi, NEC, Mitsubishi Electric, Toshiba, Sanyo, and Sharp, plus private Japanese-language sessions for Masaru Ibuka and Akio Morita, the founders of the startup they would later name Sony. Where postwar America, flush with half the world's manufacturing capacity and unable to build cars and radios fast enough to meet surging demand, relegated quality to a narrow inspection department, Japan absorbed and systematized Sarasohn's philosophy across its entire industrial culture — quality became "everyone's job," popularized enough that *Quality for Foremen* became Japan's most popular radio program in the 1950s — and by the late 1970s, Japanese manufacturers were humiliating American industry on quality grounds, from Toyota's "just in time" production system to Sony's leap from well-designed transistor radios to the 1979 Walkman. Sarasohn died in September 2001, weeks before Steve Jobs unveiled the iPod — the very product that would come to exemplify the quality philosophy Sarasohn had spent his career trying to instill, in a country whose own industry he later lamented had "forgotten the very lessons we taught the Japanese." Jobs' own direct encounter with these ideas came not at Apple but at NeXT, the company he founded after being ousted from Apple in the mid-1980s. Initially skeptical of "process" as bureaucratic and antithetical to his preferred "brute force and great people" approach — hiring geniuses, locking them in a room, and applying pressure until brilliant products emerged — Jobs nonetheless commissioned an automated NeXT factory inspired by Japanese manufacturer Alps Electric and recruited HP talent (including Randy Heffner and Jon Rubinstein) already steeped in Japanese-influenced quality methods, since HP had been aggressively pursuing Japanese-style quality since the "Anderson Bombshell" of 1980, when an HP executive discovered Japanese memory chips outperformed American ones by 1,000% at initial inspection. Jobs understood what one NeXT hire, Vicki Amon-Higa, called "small q quality" (a narrow focus on the product itself) but not "big Q quality" (enforcing consistent standards across an entire organization) — a gap Amon-Higa, who had studied Japan intensively since age 15 and helped a Florida utility become the first non-Japanese company to win Japan's Deming Prize, worked to close by bringing in two of Sarasohn's intellectual descendants: Noriaki Kano, who taught Jobs that talent needed to be actively coached and developed rather than treated as purely innate, and Joseph Juran himself, then in his eighties, who convinced Jobs of the value of "seeing everything as a repetitive process, and instrument[ing] that process, and find[ing] out how it's running." These lessons arrived too late to save NeXT, whose products found no market fit and which abandoned manufacturing entirely by 1993, but they resurfaced when Jobs needed to fix a different quality problem at Pixar, the animation studio he had acquired from George Lucas: a culture of exhausted "A-team" superstars working single projects to the point of burnout, alongside an inexperienced "B-team" unable to work in parallel — co-founder Ed Catmull described having "two different standards of quality in the same studio" as corrosive to the entire organization's morale. Jobs brought Amon-Higa back to adapt Japanese process discipline into film production specifically, building a system that let any employee metaphorically "pull the cord" on a production line — a change Catmull would later call Pixar's "defining moment." <span class="src">Apple at 50 The roots of a tech revolution</span>

When Jobs returned to Apple as CEO in 1997, he brought this hard-won, twice-tested synthesis of Japanese quality philosophy directly to bear on a nearly bankrupt company. He immediately tore up Apple's existing New Product Process framework — a concept-to-customer roadmap that one former manufacturing engineer described as having calcified into "incredible bureaucracy" with siloed vice-presidents who weren't communicating — and, working with Jon Rubinstein (who had followed him from NeXT) and fellow Motorola alumnus Glen Miranker, rebuilt it around explicit kaizen (continuous improvement) principles. The rebuilt process, still called ANPP despite being fundamentally reconceived, proved itself dramatically with the iPod: Apple took the product from initial concept to shipping in just nine months by 2001, reflecting a genuinely new cross-functional approach to development. As iPod volumes scaled from hundreds of thousands of units in 2002 toward 50 million just five years later, quality-control discipline that felt sufficient at small scale (a 1% defect rate) would have produced "a ton of waste" at Apple's eventual volume, according to Tony Fadell, who rose from contractor to senior vice president during this period — Apple scaled from one or two production lines to dozens running in parallel, with quality checks built into every station specifically to prevent defects from compounding invisibly at scale. Tim Cook, who joined in 1998 to lead operations, layered in a complementary Toyota-inspired "lean management" philosophy imported by IBM-alumni hires with a "schedule is king" mentality, treating time itself as Apple's central competitive moat rather than viewing careful slowness as a mark of craftsmanship — Cook, who once called himself "Attila the Hun of inventory," reportedly told a product designer directly, "every minute is one million dollars lost." As Apple's manufacturing volumes scaled, its supplier relationships evolved into a genuinely distinctive competitive strategy, one the FT's reporting suggests carries real ethical ambiguity. Apple initially preferred single suppliers per critical component specifically to avoid diluting its engineering ambitions to "the lowest common denominator" that spreading risk across three vendors would force — Jobs preferred identifying the single best possible supplier and then doing whatever was necessary to help that supplier succeed, deliberately pushing suppliers to develop parts, materials, and manufacturing functions that didn't yet exist anywhere. But single-sourcing became untenable once iPod volumes reached the tens of millions, forcing Apple to develop a different tactic entirely: identify the best supplier for a given component (frequently in Japan, where craftsmanship traditions ran deep), then systematically extract that supplier's process knowledge and teach it to competing suppliers until no meaningful difference remained between them. The iPod Classic's mirror-polished, 0.4-millimeter-thin stainless steel back — originally perfected by a single master craftsman in Japan through painstaking hand-polishing — became the paradigm case: as demand outpaced what artisanal production could supply, Apple filmed every detail of the craftsman's technique (wrist angle, polishing pressure, speed, sound, heat-distortion management), converted the observed process into mathematical code, and automated it at massive scale across more than 20 competing manufacturers in China, producing 15,000 to 20,000 iPod casings daily — a tactic suppliers at the time compared directly to "corporate espionage." This pattern recurred repeatedly: Jony Ive's industrial design team would identify a promising Japanese supplier and build a relationship around design ambition and future business volume, only for Apple's operations division — tasked purely with scaling production at minimal cost — to subsequently find that same supplier too expensive and risk-averse, at which point Apple would copy and reverse-engineer the supplier's craftsmanship and redeploy it elsewhere at scale. Sharp, struggling with an LCD-television price war against Korean rivals in the late 2000s, accepted a nearly $1 billion Apple investment to retool its television factory for smartphone-panel production — in exchange, Apple installed a secretive, 50-person office inside Sharp's own factory (staffed not by Californians but by display specialists Apple had recruited from Samsung, Motorola, and Taiwanese companies), gaining what one of the Japanese journalists who documented the episode called access to "Sharp's production line knowhow, manufacturing methods, and cost structure" in its entirety; Sharp's management reportedly described the arrangement as having eaten "a poisoned apple," since Apple's exclusive supply contract prevented Sharp from selling to any other buyer even as utilization swung between above-90% (when Apple needed volume) and idle (when it didn't). Apple's leverage in these arrangements was not merely operational but explicitly contractual: its standard purchase-order terms require suppliers to assign Apple "all ownership rights, title, and interest" in jointly developed intellectual property, regardless of how much of the underlying R&D work and funding the supplier itself contributed — legal ownership Apple could then use, in one former engineer's description, to tell any alternative supplier "everything they need to know to make it," rendering the original innovating supplier fully replaceable. Apple further systematized this extraction through a dedicated team of traveling "subject matter experts" who scoured the globe (spending roughly a quarter of their time physically in supplier factories) for obscure manufacturing techniques behind fine cameras and watches, adapting them for iPhone and MacBook production — patents co-invented by these engineers span an extraordinary and revealing range of unrelated manufacturing domains, from glass-alignment processes to textile-weaving machinery. Some of these experts have expressed genuine discomfort with the practice in retrospect ("I felt horrible about it... We'd screw over the original IP owner. I did this over and over," in one's words), while others frame it as straightforward competitive necessity rather than malice: "It was never like, 'we want to crush the Japanese'... It was all about hardcore business requirements," in a former Apple engineer's account, reflecting the position that suppliers unable to scale a great idea to Apple's required volume simply hadn't "evolved" competitively. <span class="src">Apple at 50 How Asia fuelled its rise to the top</span>

The same extraction playbook that hollowed out Japan's manufacturing leadership over roughly two decades — turning once-dominant consumer brands like Sony, Sharp, and Panasonic into what the FT calls "invisible subcontractors" inside Apple's own products — produced the precise opposite outcome when applied to China, a divergence the reporting traces to a fundamentally different strategic posture on China's side. China's approach dates to the late 1970s, when Deng Xiaoping visited factories in Singapore and Japan and confronted directly how far behind China's own industrial capacity had fallen (the country was, at the time, poorer than sub-Saharan Africa); what China possessed was an enormous, densely concentrated, low-wage workforce, unskilled but willing to learn, that a forceful central state could direct at scale. Rather than erecting physical barriers to prevent people fleeing for Hong Kong's greater opportunity, China created economic opportunity domestically instead — Shenzhen's founding as a special economic zone, paired with deliberately suppressed export costs (competitive wages, an undervalued currency, tax exemptions, subsidized machinery), functioned as a magnet drawing in foreign capital and expertise specifically so China's workforce could absorb "tacit knowledge" — the kind of hands-on manufacturing skill that can only be learned by doing, not read from a manual. Chinese businessmen have their own three-word term capturing the underlying logic from their side of the same transactions Apple was orchestrating: "yang-tao-sha," or "raise-entrap-slaughter." Western corporations, including Apple, had long organized around what's known as the "smiling curve" of product development — high value concentrated at the concept-development end and the sales/branding end of a product's lifecycle, with low value assigned to manufacturing in the middle — and Apple mastered exploitation of that curve so completely it became a $3.7 trillion company earning more than $100 billion in annual profit. China's strategic bet inverted that same logic at the national level — what the reporting terms the "grumpy curve" — deliberately embracing the low-value "belly" of the curve specifically to absorb capability, calculating that durable, scaled industrial knowhow would ultimately prove more valuable than any single company's inherently ephemeral profit stream. One former Apple operations manager summarized the calculation from China's side directly: "if we're making it all, that's power." Aware that the same knowledge-leakage dynamic that had proven so effective against Japan could equally threaten Apple's own position in China, Apple began building dedicated, Apple-exclusive manufacturing campuses through Foxconn starting in 2006 (the first in Guanlan, followed by Chengdu and Zhengzhou) — but the containment strategy has proven only partially effective: Apple today produces roughly a fifth of the world's smartphones yet has no remaining capacity to manufacture at scale without China, even as Chinese domestic smartphone brands now hold roughly half of global smartphone market share and China controls strategic chokepoints across lithium-ion batteries, rare-earth metals, and printed circuit boards. BYD's trajectory captures the pattern with particular clarity: the company began as an Apple supplier of metal casings around 2008 and became one of Apple's most important assemblers of the iPad plus a supplier of titanium-alloy components — the same manufacturing training that turned Sony and Sharp into invisible Apple subcontractors instead helped BYD build an entirely independent electric-vehicle company that today outsells Tesla, the clearest available demonstration of the "grumpy curve" bet paying off. Apple, for its part, disputes this account of its supplier relationships entirely, stating in response to the FT's reporting that "the entire premise of this story is false and doesn't reflect how we work with our supply chain partners." Whatever the balance of legitimate competitive practice versus ethically questionable extraction, the FT's broader argument is that Apple's true most consequential product may not be the iPhone but the supply chain itself — one the company built with extraordinary sophistication over a quarter century, drawing directly on a chain of quality-management knowledge transfer stretching back to Homer Sarasohn's 1945 seminars in occupied Tokyo, but one Apple ultimately no longer fully controls, having played a central role in building up the very Chinese industrial capacity now positioned as both indispensable partner and emerging strategic rival. <span class="src">Apple at 50 How Asia fuelled its rise to the top</span>

## Open Questions

- Apple's formal, on-the-record denial ("the entire premise of this story is false") is not reconciled in either article with the extensive, detailed, named and anonymous-sourced testimony (former engineers, a senior supply chain executive, Japanese journalists, academic researchers) supporting the extraction-playbook account — readers should weigh both the specificity of the sourcing and the fact that Apple's rebuttal is a single blanket statement rather than a rebuttal of any specific claim.
- Neither article addresses whether Apple's current relationship with Indian manufacturing (mentioned briefly as a hoped-for alternative to China) is repeating, avoiding, or adapting the same knowledge-extraction playbook used historically with Japan and China — the piece frames India as a current US policy hope without assessing whether the underlying dynamics documented for Japan/China are structurally likely to recur there.
