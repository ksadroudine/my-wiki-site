## Summary

Generative AI is compressing marketing research timelines from months to days by enabling synthetic consumer "digital twins," AI-moderated interviews at scale, and fast analysis of unstructured qualitative data. Validation studies and a live commercial deployment at Colgate-Palmolive both show synthetic and AI-collected data closely track human results, but researchers still need human oversight to catch bias, low-quality shortcuts, and synthetic data's limits. Interviews at P&G, PepsiCo and Novartis add the knowledge-management side, showing that GenAI helps customer insights flow only where culture, data standards and agency ownership are fixed.

## Highlights

- Traditional marketing research costs tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars and takes weeks to months; generative AI compresses this by handling the labor-intensive middle stages (data collection, analysis) while humans remain in charge of problem definition, research design, and final judgment.
- Consumer "digital twins" — synthetic, LLM-generated personas built from demographic, psychographic, and behavioral data — let researchers simulate concept tests, pricing moves, and campaign reactions before committing budget to a live study; a University of Wisconsin study found LLM-generated qualitative responses matched or exceeded human responses in depth and insightfulness, and a B2B cybersecurity study using the startup Evidenza's synthetic decision-makers found 0.75-0.88 correlations against a real human survey.
- In a rigorous double-blind validation study, EY withheld its actual annual brand-survey results from Evidenza, which had independently generated survey answers from more than 1,000 synthetic personas matching EY's target audience (CEOs of $1B+ companies); the synthetic results were "95% the same" as the real ones, with strong correlations and often near-identical numbers.
- AI-moderated interviews — where an LLM asks discussion-guide questions, scores each answer for depth and clarity, and probes further when the score falls short — let companies collect qualitative data at a scale and speed impossible with human moderators (one vendor, Outset, completed 100 interviews in days rather than weeks) and can reduce social-desirability bias, since some respondents are more forthcoming with an AI interviewer than a human one.
- Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) partially fixes two specific weaknesses of LLM-only synthetic survey data — insufficient response variation and inconsistent answers across related questions — by grounding the model in a company's own existing qualitative data rather than relying on its generic training alone.
- A four-category framework for generative AI's market-research opportunities: supporting current practices (making existing data collection and analysis faster and cheaper), replacing current practices (synthetic data standing in for traditionally collected survey/interview data), filling existing gaps (providing empirical evidence for the many managerial decisions historically made without any formal research, since 30% of one survey's respondents reported using gen AI this way), and creating new kinds of data and insights (digital twins as a genuinely new research modality, not just a cheaper version of an old one).
- A Columbia Business School/Stanford/Google DeepMind research effort built digital twins from a few hours of interview and survey data per person, then had the twins retake the same surveys; the twins' answers replicated each real participant's own answers on a second sitting two weeks later with 85% accuracy — a rare direct benchmark of digital-twin fidelity against a person's own test-retest reliability, not just against other people's answers.
- Both sources converge on the same limitations: LLMs inherit demographic and cultural biases from their training data (skewing toward liberal, well-educated, non-religious, under-65 viewpoints in one study); synthetic survey responses show less variance and are more sensitive to question wording than human responses; AI-generated pricing-experiment data has shown implausible demand curves because models struggle to treat price as genuinely randomized rather than correlated with other store variables; and generative AI still cannot reliably predict emotional or sensory reactions (a fragrance company found AI panels could not replicate how real people respond to actual scents).
- Synthetic data and AI-collected human data both raise a data-integrity risk from the opposite direction of typical AI concerns: it becomes cheap to produce credible-looking but low-quality research, and human survey respondents themselves could use LLMs to fabricate more convincing answers in order to earn compensation, making fraudulent data harder to detect.
- The same efficiency gains that free junior researchers from rote synthesis and coding work also threaten the training ground those rote tasks traditionally provided, since automatable tasks historically taught junior talent the deep analytical thinking and contextual judgment senior researchers rely on.
- Colgate-Palmolive built cohorts of "digital consumer twins" to test new product concepts and, early in the effort, ran its own internal validation comparing the virtual consumers' opinions against real humans' opinions, finding a high level of concordance — corroborating, from a live commercial deployment rather than an academic study, the digital-twin fidelity the other sources establish experimentally.
- At Colgate-Palmolive, one GenAI system surfaces unmet consumer needs from RAG-augmented synthesis of internal insight reports and external market data, feeding a second proprietary system that generates new product concepts (copy and imagery) in minutes, which are then screened against digital-twin cohorts before the strongest ideas go to real-consumer validation — collapsing a process that once required narrowing to a handful of concepts before any testing could begin.
- Companies using retrieval-augmented generation on their own customer insights should manage knowledge flows (how insights are created, analysed, stored and accessed), not just storage; Novartis's Sherlock system, with curated Knowledge Zones and a flag for region-limited findings, saved more than $29 million in primary market research costs in one year. <span class="src">How GenAI Can and Can’t Help Manage Customer Insights</span>
- Specialised GenAI tools make qualitative analysis feasible (a 30-plus interview global study that took six weeks can be synthesised in a day), and PepsiCo's Ask Ada platform combines creative testing on real or synthetic customers, social listening, predictive modelling and a conversational interface. <span class="src">How GenAI Can and Can’t Help Manage Customer Insights</span>
- Four non-technical obstacles persist: business units without common brand and category definitions, insights outside strategy and culture, agency relationships that blur data ownership, and analytics teams seen as low-status order takers. <span class="src">How GenAI Can and Can’t Help Manage Customer Insights</span>

## Concept

<span class="src">How Gen AI Is Transforming Market Research</span> (HBR, May 2025) lays out a four-category framework, based on two years of research and direct engagement with companies, for how generative AI is reshaping market research. The first category, supporting current practices, applies gen AI's four core capabilities — synthesis, coding, human interaction, and writing — to each stage of the research pipeline (opportunity identification, data collection and analysis, reporting), making existing work faster and cheaper rather than fundamentally different; a survey of more than 170 market research practitioners found 45% already using gen AI this way and another 45% planning to, most commonly to synthesize interview transcripts (62%), analyze data (58%), and write reports (54%). The second category, replacing current practices, centers on synthetic data — AI-generated data that mimics real people's preferences and behavior — which 81% of survey respondents already used or planned to use; the article's standout validation case is a double-blind study in which EY withheld its actual annual brand-survey results from the startup Evidenza, which had independently generated answers from more than 1,000 synthetic CEO personas matching EY's target audience, arriving at results "95% the same" as the real ones. The third category, filling existing gaps, uses gen AI as an always-on research engine for the many managerial decisions organizations make without any formal empirical backing due to time or budget constraints — 30% of survey respondents reported using gen AI to guide decisions that previously wouldn't have used external data at all. The fourth and most novel category, creating new kinds of data and insights, centers on digital twins — virtual replicas of individual customers built from proprietary or public data — used to test marketing pitches and materials before they ever reach a real person; a Columbia Business School effort (with Stanford and Google DeepMind collaborators) built digital twins from a few hours of interview data per person and found the twins' survey answers replicated each real participant's own answers on a second sitting two weeks later with 85% accuracy. The article closes by cataloging generative AI's limitations for this use: training-data bias that skews synthetic respondents toward particular demographic and political profiles, sensitivity to survey question wording and answer-option ordering, "confounding" problems that produce implausible demand curves in AI-simulated pricing experiments, and a persistent inability to model emotional or sensory reactions realistically (illustrated by a fragrance company's failed attempt to replace a human scent-testing panel with an AI one).

<span class="src">Gain Consumer Insight With Generative AI</span> (MIT SMR, April 2026), published roughly a year later and explicitly citing the HBR article, narrows the same underlying territory into two specific technique families and grounds them in the authors' own published research. Digital twins, the article's first focus, are validated through a study replicating a 2019 market-research project for a Fortune 500 food manufacturer: the authors generated synthetic respondents matching the original human respondents' profiles, then had crowd workers compare the synthetic and human answers blind, finding LLM-generated qualitative responses rated as good as or better than human ones on depth and insightfulness, and LLM-generated quantitative survey responses correctly replicating the direction and magnitude of human answers (though a plain LLM, without grounding, showed less response variation and less internal consistency than real human answers — a weakness partially fixed by retrieval-augmented generation drawing on existing qualitative data). The article's second focus, AI-moderated interviews, formalizes a three-step interviewer/scorer/prober loop — the AI asks a discussion-guide question, scores the answer for depth and clarity on a 1-100 scale, and probes further if the score falls short — which the authors found preserves the meaning and essence of human interview data while being deployable at a scale and speed (and across a wider geographic and cultural range) no human moderator could match; independent human raters scored the AI-collected data significantly higher on depth and insight than typical human-moderated interviews. Consistent with the earlier article's limitations catalog, this piece adds its own risk analysis specific to marketing research: because generative AI makes it just as easy to produce convincing low-quality research as high-quality research, and because human survey respondents could themselves use LLMs to fabricate more persuasive answers for compensation, synthetic and AI-assisted data raises new data-integrity risks that traditional research methods didn't face at the same scale. Both articles converge on the same prescription: treat generative AI as a research collaborator whose speed and scale should free human experts to spend more time on question framing and insight curation, not as a replacement for the human judgment that determines whether a given finding is actually meaningful.

<span class="src">The GenAI Focus Shifts to Innovation at Colgate-Palmolive</span> (MIT SMR, January 2025) supplies a concrete, real-world commercial deployment of the digital-twin and RAG techniques the other two sources establish through academic and vendor research. Colgate-Palmolive's data-science organization deliberately targeted innovation as a high-value generative AI use case, building a two-stage system: one GenAI application synthesizes consumer insights from the company's full internal history of insight reports (augmented via retrieval-augmented generation with proprietary research, search trends, and syndicated data, which the company's analytics chief notes lowers hallucination risk relative to relying on a model's general training) to surface unmet consumer needs, and a second proprietary system turns those needs into new product concepts — copy and imagery for a new product idea, generated in minutes with a human in the loop. The resulting concepts are then screened against cohorts of "digital consumer twins" before the strongest candidates proceed to real-consumer validation, collapsing a process that previously required narrowing down to a small handful of concepts before any testing could begin. Early in the initiative, Colgate-Palmolive's own researchers ran an internal validation comparing the digital twins' opinions against real consumers' opinions and found a high level of concordance, and separately found AI-augmented product concepts performed as well as or better than concepts developed by humans alone on multiple measures — both findings corroborating, from a live commercial deployment, the digital-twin fidelity the Wisconsin study and the Columbia/Stanford/DeepMind research establish experimentally elsewhere in this concept. The company gates access to these tools behind mandatory training on guardrails, human-in-the-loop practice, and responsible-AI principles through an internal "AI Hub," and tracks usage value through built-in KPI surveys rather than assuming productivity gains are self-evident. <span class="src">The GenAI Focus Shifts to Innovation at Colgate-Palmolive</span>

Read together, all three sources converge on the same picture from complementary vantage points: the HBR and MIT SMR pieces establish, through survey data and controlled validation studies, that generative AI's synthetic data and AI-moderated research techniques reliably approximate real human responses well enough to be useful; Colgate-Palmolive's experience shows what it looks like when a real company builds these techniques into a live, ongoing product-innovation pipeline rather than a one-off research study, and independently reaches the same conclusion about digital-twin fidelity that the academic sources reach through more rigorous experimental design.

<span class="src">How GenAI Can and Can’t Help Manage Customer Insights</span> (MIT SMR, July 2026) complements this concept's research-technique focus with interviews at eight consumer companies and several vendors about turning insight content into usable knowledge. P&G buys storage and access software but built its own GenAI analysis and categorisation to get "sharp, pointed answers" rather than document links, and frames AI as augmenting rather than replacing practices such as home visits. The authors argue earlier knowledge-management tools failed for cultural reasons that persist: a global consumer goods company with autonomous units in over 100 countries has "pockets of knowledge, very incoherent" and little tool adoption, whereas PepsiCo created a Global Insights Council of 15 leaders to build "one nation" of research and insists the client must own agency-produced results and the learning from them. Library-style usage (poor prompts, little contribution of new insight) and budget cuts show that democratising access does not by itself create demand, so strategy-level work such as understanding consumer demand "still requires humans". <span class="src">How GenAI Can and Can’t Help Manage Customer Insights</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses how organizations should handle the compounding risk that synthetic data trained partly on other AI-generated content (as vendors and academic panels reuse and share synthetic datasets over time) might drift further from real human behavior with each generation, a concern raised elsewhere about AI model collapse but not connected here to market research specifically.
- The HBR article notes concerns about entry-level job losses and a shrinking training pipeline for junior researchers, but neither source proposes a concrete organizational fix, leaving open how research teams should structure junior roles once the rote synthesis and coding tasks that once trained them are automated.
- The MIT SMR piece draws on eight companies and several vendors, many of them vendor-supplied cases, and reports Novartis's $29 million saving without detail on how it was measured.
