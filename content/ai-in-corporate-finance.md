## Summary

AI reliably outperforms humans at tactical finance tasks such as short-term budget allocation, forecasting and variance analysis, but strategic financial planning under uncertainty still depends on human judgment. Most finance functions remain stuck at the pilot stage because the constraint is leadership practice and culture rather than technology, although process-level redesign shows large potential gains in areas such as order-to-cash.

## Highlights

- A management simulation testing AI against seasoned managers on budgeting decisions for a hypothetical automotive-parts manufacturer found AI consistently outperformed humans at optimizing tactical budget allocation by learning from past performance data.
- AI's tactical budgeting performance depended heavily on the quality of the strategic context it was given: when participants selected KPIs poorly aligned with strategic goals, AI optimized narrowly for those flawed metrics rather than compensating for the misalignment, illustrating the "surrogation effect" where over-optimizing a proxy metric produces decisions misaligned with actual strategic intent.
- Uber's finance function illustrates the tactical/strategic split working in practice: AI-driven financial platforms handle real-time tactical budget adjustments across 600+ cities, but local teams routinely override AI predictions using market knowledge to keep tactical decisions aligned with long-term goals — a balance credited as pivotal to Uber's first-ever $1.1 billion operating profit in 2023.
- Despite years of heavy AI investment, CFOs privately report the same pattern: proofs of concept that never leave the sandbox, promising pilot models that sit unused once quarterly pressure hits, and dashboards that are refreshed but rarely shape actual decisions.
- The reason similar AI technologies transform other corporate functions but not finance is not primarily a technology gap (data quality, tool integration, model trust) — it is that finance's professional ethos is built around avoiding surprise and defending a single "right" number, which actively discourages the exploratory, uncertainty-embracing behavior AI adoption requires.
- Four recurring leadership practices distinguish finance teams that successfully embed AI: staying alert to weak external signals as shared team responsibility (not a specialist task), running small bounded experiments evaluated by what was learned rather than only by accuracy, replacing single-point forecasts with multiple explicit scenarios tied to concrete decisions, and actively diffusing local AI-enabled practices across teams and geographies rather than letting them stay isolated wins.
- A CFO's influence on AI adoption operates less through direct technology decisions and more through the tone set day to day: demanding guaranteed ROI before any pilot starts reliably kills experimentation, while showing genuine interest in what a team learned from a trial — even one that didn't outperform the existing model — legitimizes the exploratory work AI adoption actually requires.
- Both sources converge on the same underlying principle even though they examine different mechanisms: AI's value in finance is not self-executing — it depends on humans supplying the strategic framing, context, and organizational permission that determines whether AI's tactical strength gets pointed in a useful direction.
- Hackett's modeled "AI World Class" benchmarks claim a performance gap of up to 75% between organizations that redesign work around AI and those that automate existing processes, spanning 16 end-to-end processes. <span class="src">The AI Enterprise Performance Gap (Hackett Group)</span>
- In order-to-cash, modeled AI World Class performance cuts process costs 52-59% and staffing per $1 billion of revenue 56-64%, raises automated credit scoring from 41% to 97% and electronic order receipt from 29% to 68%, shortens dispute resolution from 48 to 27 days and cuts average delinquent days from nearly 10 to about 1.5. <span class="src">The AI Enterprise Performance Gap (Hackett Group)</span>
- Hackett reports engagements delivering a 60% faster booking cycle through quote-to-cash redesign and more than $20 million of potential annual revenue recovery from AI agents extracting contract terms in contract-to-payment, and advises a four-step path of baseline, prioritization, future-state operating model and roadmap. <span class="src">The AI Enterprise Performance Gap (Hackett Group)</span>

## Concept

<span class="src">How AI Can Help Your Company Set a Budget</span> (HBR, November 2024) supplies a specific, empirically tested answer to where AI should and shouldn't lead in finance. The article opens with a puzzle: companies like Unilever, BASF, and Covestro have invested heavily in AI budgeting tools yet still struggle with forecasting accuracy amid volatile costs and inflation. The authors' answer is a tactical-versus-strategic split. Tactical budgeting — short-term resource allocation, cost management, performance tracking — is well-defined and data-driven, and AI should simply replace human decision-making there, since it is faster, more consistent, and free of human bias. Strategic budgeting — long-term planning, aligning resources with a future vision under genuine uncertainty — remains a domain where AI struggles, subject to the "surrogation effect" (from Michael Harris and Bill Tayler's 2019 HBR work) in which AI narrowly optimizes whatever performance indicator it's given, even when that indicator is poorly aligned with the actual strategic goal. The authors tested this empirically via a management simulation: seasoned managers and an AI algorithm both made budget-allocation decisions for a hypothetical automotive-parts manufacturer across multiple cycles. AI consistently outperformed human participants when given a well-defined strategic context (clear goals, well-chosen KPIs), but underperformed when managers themselves had selected KPIs misaligned with their stated strategy — AI faithfully optimized the flawed metric rather than correcting for the misalignment. Uber is offered as a real-world illustration of getting this balance right: AI-driven platforms handle real-time tactical budget adjustments across the company's 600+ cities, while local teams retain authority to override AI predictions based on market knowledge, a combination credited as pivotal to Uber's first-ever $1.1 billion operating profit in 2023.

<span class="src">Why AI Isn’t Transforming Finance Yet</span> (MIT SMR, June 2026) addresses the broader and, in some ways, prior question the budgeting article leaves implicit: given that the tactical/strategic logic is reasonably well understood, why do most finance functions still fail to realize it in practice? Drawing on multiyear research with CFOs and more than 300 senior finance survey respondents, the authors argue the constraint is leadership practice, not technology maturity. Finance's professional identity is built around reliability and avoiding surprise — a great deal of the function's ethos is shaped by "the imperative of not being caught out" — which sits in direct tension with the exploratory, uncertainty-embracing behavior AI adoption actually requires. The article identifies four recurring leadership practices, illustrated through anonymized vignettes, that distinguish finance teams that successfully embed AI: shared vigilance (a manufacturing company's finance team built a weekly ritual of surfacing external signals as collective responsibility rather than a specialist task); routine experimentation (a consumer-services company's finance director reframed pilots as bounded learning trials judged by what was learned, not just accuracy, which the CFO reinforced by asking "What did we learn that we could not have learned otherwise?" even after an underperforming trial); scenario-based strategic conversation (a utilities provider's finance team replaced single-point forecasts with multiple explicit scenarios, shifting executive discussion from "which number is right" to "what should we do if this scenario starts to materialize"); and active diffusion of local practices (a retail group's store-level anomaly-detection routine spread from one country to others once the organization created space for teams to share how they worked, not just what they delivered). The CFO's role in all four patterns is described as "indirect and decisive" — less about specific technology decisions than about the tone set through everyday questions, since demanding guaranteed ROI before any pilot starts reliably kills experimentation, while genuine curiosity about what a team learned legitimizes it.

Read together, the two sources describe complementary layers of the same underlying challenge. The budgeting article specifies a concrete allocation of labor between AI and humans within one finance process — AI should lead tactical execution, humans must supply strategic framing — and demonstrates empirically what happens when that framing is absent (AI optimizes the wrong thing faithfully). The finance-leadership article explains why so few organizations actually achieve that allocation in practice: not because the logic is unclear, but because finance's control-oriented culture and the CFO's everyday leadership tone determine whether teams feel safe enough to do the experimentation, signal-scanning, and scenario-thinking that supplying good strategic framing to AI actually requires.

The Hackett Group's June 2026 study argues that finance's value comes from end-to-end process redesign rather than isolated task automation, because upstream improvements such as better credit decisions and cleaner orders reduce downstream collections and dispute work and compound across the process. Collectors in the modeled world spend 75% of their time contacting customers rather than 41%, which Hackett frames as a redesign of the role. It notes SG&A costs outpacing revenue growth across much of the Fortune 200 and positions chief financial officers at the center of transformation, and says most AI business cases focus on individual productivity rather than process change. <span class="src">The AI Enterprise Performance Gap (Hackett Group)</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses how the tactical/strategic split described in the budgeting article should be operationalized within the four leadership practices described in the finance-transformation article — for example, whether "routine experimentation" as described applies equally to tactical AI deployment (where the budgeting article argues AI should simply lead) or is really only relevant to the strategic side.
- The budgeting article's management simulation used a single hypothetical company scenario (automotive-parts manufacturing); neither source addresses how the AI-outperforms-on-tactical-tasks finding might vary across industries with different volatility profiles or regulatory constraints.
- The figures are modeled comparisons of peer-median and "AI World Class" scenarios from Hackett's proprietary benchmark data, not observed outcomes from a defined sample, and the report is complimentary research from a firm marketing related services.
- The study covers transactional order-to-cash processes, not the strategic planning tasks where earlier sources in this concept find human judgment still decisive, so it does not test that boundary.
