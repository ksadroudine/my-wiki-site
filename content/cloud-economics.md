## Summary

How to evaluate the real cost and benefit of moving a given workload to the cloud, including the CapEx-versus-OpEx trade-off and the hard-to-quantify indirect costs of running an application in a data center. Explains why cloud migration pays off for some workload types (commodity, bursty, massively scaled) but not others.

## Concept

Evaluating the economics of a cloud migration is harder than it first appears, because initial cloud costs for a narrow use case (testing, one application, some storage) are often genuinely cheap, which can make management unprepared for costs rising sharply once usage expands into full production scale — a pattern that is usually a logical consequence of the workload's actual characteristics rather than a sign that the vendor is overcharging. The clearest cases where a move to cloud delivers unambiguous economic benefit fall into three patterns: temporary or bursty capacity needs (where building permanent infrastructure for a short-term spike would be wasteful), Software-as-a-Service replacements for inconsistent internally-run tools (particularly where inconsistency itself — different sales teams running different versions of the same tool — is actively costing the business deals or accuracy), and massively scaled but relatively simple applications, such as email, where a cloud vendor's standardization and scale advantage is structurally difficult for any single company's data center to match. Conversely, workloads with unusual data-sovereignty or governance constraints, aging monolithic applications with deep dependencies, or specialized workloads used occasionally by a small group are often better left where they are, since the potential cloud savings are outweighed by migration cost, compliance risk, or simply the absence of any real economic benefit once the workload's actual usage pattern is considered. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.16 Breaking Down Cloud Economics</span>

A foundational financial distinction underlying most cloud-versus-data-center debates is CapEx versus OpEx: capital expenditures are investments in owned or leased assets (servers, real estate, networking equipment, software licenses) that a company depreciates over time, while operating expenses are the ongoing costs of running the business day to day, a category most cloud services (IaaS, PaaS, SaaS) fall into. Because organizations are typically far more constrained in how much capital expenditure they can commit to than in ongoing operating spend, shifting cost from CapEx to OpEx frees capital for other, potentially more valuable investments, and increases organizational agility — a company can cut its losses on an underperforming initiative or ramp up quickly on a promising one far more easily when the underlying spend is operating expense rather than sunk capital investment. This is a genuinely separate consideration from raw unit-cost comparisons (a cloud virtual machine can look more expensive than a depreciated physical server over a three-year horizon and still make overall business sense once this flexibility benefit is properly weighed). <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). The deeper difficulty in making a fair cloud-versus-data-center comparison is that most organizations simply do not have an accurate picture of what a given application actually costs to run on-premises. A comprehensive accounting has to include server and storage costs (amortized total cost of ownership, not just purchase price, and complicated by the fact that a single server often supports multiple applications simultaneously), network costs, backup and archive costs, business-continuity and disaster-recovery costs, the full data center infrastructure overhead (electricity, floor space, cooling, building maintenance), software maintenance costs, and operational support personnel costs — most of which resist clean attribution to any single application or workload, since the same staff, servers, and infrastructure typically support many applications at once. This is also why removing a handful of workloads from a data center rarely produces proportional savings: most of the underlying fixed costs don't meaningfully shrink until the removed footprint is large enough to actually reduce floor space, cooling requirements, or staffing levels — a data center running at 40 percent utilization won't get materially cheaper by migrating a small slice of its workload to the cloud, even though the same migration might be highly cost-effective from the migrated workload's own narrow perspective. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.16 Breaking Down Cloud Economics</span>

Once an organization moves toward an actual hybrid deployment rather than a simple on-premises-versus-cloud comparison, an additional layer of cost categories comes into play that a basic CapEx-vs-OpEx analysis will miss entirely: the ongoing management overhead of coordinating multiple environments rather than just one; data transfer fees, both for the initial migration and for ongoing movement of data between environments; application customization costs to make a workload actually function correctly in the new hybrid configuration; integration costs to connect the migrated application with everything else it depends on; and additional compliance auditing costs layered on top of whatever auditing already applied to the on-premises environment. Given how genuinely hard all of this is to model precisely, vendor-provided calculators — both simple usage estimators and more elaborate total-cost-of-ownership calculators that project five-year savings versus a data center deployment — are useful for orienting a rough comparison but should be treated with real skepticism, since many are marketing tools built to favor a cloud-positive conclusion rather than neutral financial instruments; the appropriate response is a documented internal policy, agreed in advance, about what categories of workload and data must stay in a traditional data center or private cloud (and why) rather than making migration decisions purely on the basis of an individual calculator's output. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition).
