## Summary

Intelligent choice architectures (ICAs) combine predictive and generative AI to produce and refine sophisticated sets of decision options rather than a single "best" answer, extending behavioral economics' choice-architecture concept into an AI-driven system. As ICAs mature, they force organizations to redesign decision rights and accountability structures, shifting the central leadership question from who should decide to how decision environments themselves should be designed and governed. MIT CISR research on an AI Decision Matrix and HBR research on why RACI-style decision-rights tools fail add practical guidance on who or what should decide.

## Highlights

- Intelligent choice architectures (ICAs) extend Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein's behavioral-economics concept of "choice architecture" — intentionally organizing the context in which people decide — into an AI-driven system that generates, refines, prioritizes, and presents choice sets, learning from outcomes through feedback loops rather than remaining static.
- The research's central finding is that organizations using AI to generate sophisticated sets of options, rather than a single "optimal" recommendation, achieve superior outcomes across sectors — the deliverable of good AI-assisted decision-making is better choices, not just better decisions.
- Five categories of ICA design were identified: nudge algorithms that guide toward better decisions while preserving perceived autonomy, personalized decision environments tailored to an individual's role and history, predictive choice modeling that surfaces likely outcomes and trade-offs, complexity management that distills large data volumes into actionable insight, and embedded ethical decision-making guardrails.
- A large international bank's AI pilot that fed meeting transcripts and agendas into an LLM for automatic summarization and next-step generation produced dramatically higher meeting-satisfaction scores than the bank's prior survey baseline, and improved follow-up coordination on subsequent meetings — illustrating ICA principles applied to a mundane, universal organizational pain point.
- The RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) project-management framework is itself a form of choice architecture for decision rights; combining it with AI ("intelligent RACI") could predict optimal responsibility allocations from real-time data, adjust decision rights fluidly as project contexts shift, and proactively flag decision bottlenecks.
- As ICAs mature from decision-support tools into semi-autonomous decision-making agents, they create a "learning-authority dilemma": when an ICA's demonstrated decision-making ability exceeds the formal decision rights it has been granted, organizations need dynamic governance frameworks that can deliberately expand its authority while maintaining oversight, rather than freezing the ICA's authority at its original design point.
- At a pharmaceutical company, an ICA used for revenue forecasting evolved through three stages — a decision-support tool, then a collaborator whose joint output with finance managers produced the most accurate forecasts, then an increasingly autonomous forecaster that improved without further human intervention — illustrating in miniature how decision rights can shift toward an AI system as its demonstrated competence grows.
- The research identifies "meta-decision rights" as the emerging center of organizational power: not who gets to make a given decision, but who has the authority to design and govern the choice-generating systems themselves — meaning leadership accountability increasingly attaches to the quality of the decision environments leaders build, not just the individual decisions made within them.
- MIT CISR's AI Decision Matrix, built from interviews with 30 executives, sorts decisions by ambiguity and risk into routine, consequential, exploratory and strategic types and assigns humans and AI different roles across framing, acting and learning, always with human oversight. <span class="src">A framework for determining when AI can make decisions</span>
- One New Zealand, running over 50 AI agents, illustrates the matrix: an audience-segmentation agent cut segmentation time 60% under marketer review, pricing decisions stay human-in-the-loop as a non-negotiable constraint, and network-resilience decisions remain human-led although 15 task agents and orchestration agents supply analysis. <span class="src">A framework for determining when AI can make decisions</span>
- Best practice includes business units owning the framing of each AI use case, an AI centre of excellence owning data, orchestration and responsible-AI policy, a named human owner for every agent, and managing AI as a portfolio of business decisions rather than use cases. <span class="src">A framework for determining when AI can make decisions</span>
- HBR identifies four mistakes that make RACI-type decision-rights tools fail, based on work with more than 100 companies: assigning roles before clarifying goals, treating rights as a static list from one senior leader, misunderstanding what each role means (half of 30 consultancy partners thought the "responsible" person had the final say), and letting hierarchy override roles. <span class="src">What Companies Get Wrong About Decision Rights</span>

## Concept

<span class="src">Intelligent Choices Reshape Decision-Making and Productivity</span> (MIT SMR, October 2024), the first installment in a research collaboration between MIT SMR and Tata Consultancy Services, introduces the core concept: an intelligent choice architecture (ICA) is an AI system, combining generative and predictive capabilities, that produces and refines sophisticated sets of decision options rather than a single recommended answer. The article grounds this in Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein's Nobel Prize-winning behavioral-economics work on "choice architecture" — deliberately organizing the context in which people make decisions — arguing that AI strengthens the connection between the design of a decision environment and the decisions that emerge from it, because AI systems can learn and improve their choice sets through outcome feedback in a way static choice architectures cannot. The article identifies five categories of ICA design (nudge algorithms, personalized decision environments, predictive choice modeling, complexity management, and embedded ethical guardrails) and illustrates practical impact through a large international bank's pilot: feeding meeting transcripts and agendas into an LLM for automatic summarization produced substantially higher meeting-satisfaction scores than the bank's prior survey baseline, and the resulting summaries also improved follow-up coordination on subsequent meetings. The article closes by identifying decision rights as the central organizational design question ICAs raise — proposing "intelligent RACI" (combining the classic Responsible/Accountable/Consulted/Informed framework with AI that predicts optimal responsibility allocation and adapts it as project contexts shift) as an early example — and explicitly previews a follow-up article and long-form report to come.

<span class="src">The Great Power Shift: How Intelligent Choice Architectures Rewrite Decision Rights</span> (MIT SMR, January 2025) is that promised second installment, drawing on interviews with senior leaders across six major industry groups to examine how ICAs concretely reshape decision rights once deployed at scale. The article's central argument is that as compound AI systems (combining predictive and generative AI) become more sophisticated choice architects, organizational focus shifts from decision execution to decision design — executives become accountable for the decision environments their staff operate within, not just for individual decisions. It documents three structural shifts already occurring: power flowing to whoever designs the best decision environments, whether human or machine (illustrated by Sanofi's use of ICAs to systematically counteract sunk-cost bias in R&D portfolio decisions); network effects that make ICAs more valuable the more they're used, since each transaction or decision improves future accuracy (illustrated by Mastercard's fraud-detection ICAs, which improve for the entire payments ecosystem with every transaction processed); and real-time optimization contexts — like algorithmic trading, programmatic advertising, and a pharmaceutical company's revenue-forecasting ICA, which evolved from decision-support tool to human-AI collaborator to increasingly autonomous forecaster — where ceding real-time authority to the ICA outperforms retaining it exclusively with human managers. This maturation creates what the article terms the "learning-authority dilemma": when an ICA's demonstrated decision-making capability exceeds its formally granted authority, organizations need dynamic governance frameworks capable of deliberately expanding that authority rather than either freezing it prematurely or ceding it without appropriate oversight. The article's culminating concept is "meta-decision rights" — as ICAs mature, the most consequential organizational power shifts from the right to make a given decision to the right to design and govern the systems that generate decision options in the first place, reframing the central leadership question from "who should decide?" to "how do we architect better ways to decide?"

Read together as a deliberate two-part research series, the articles build from foundational concept to organizational consequence: the first establishes what an intelligent choice architecture is and demonstrates its value through concrete pilots, while the second traces what happens as ICAs mature and gain real organizational traction — a fundamental redistribution of decision rights toward whoever, human or machine, is positioned to design the best decision environment, with a wholly new category of "meta-accountability" emerging for the leaders responsible for those designs.

<span class="src">A framework for determining when AI can make decisions</span> (MIT SMR, September 2026) gives the concrete allocation rule this concept's decision-rights thesis lacks. The matrix pairs ambiguity (how clearly data determines the answer and whether people agree) with risk (consequences and reversibility), and each of the four resulting decision types implies a different split. Routine decisions can have framing codified and action and learning automated with humans close by until confidence builds; consequential decisions keep humans monitoring execution and managing exceptions; exploratory decisions let framing evolve through human-AI interaction under strong governance; strategic decisions keep humans leading framing and learning while AI facilitates action. <span class="src">A framework for determining when AI can make decisions</span>

<span class="src">What Companies Get Wrong About Decision Rights</span> (HBR, July 2026) addresses the human side of the same question and why decision-rights documents often fail. In a medical-technology company, 12 executives crowded a meeting that a RACI had assigned to four, ending 90 minutes later without a decision. The authors recommend setting measurable goals and subgoals first, co-creating roles with those affected (RACI sessions are conversation starters, with one accountable person and two to four responsible people), defining behavioural descriptions for each role, tailoring roles to the topic so the best-informed person is accountable, challenging senior leaders to name just four decisions a year only they should make, and embedding roles in meeting agendas and performance guides. The lessons carry over to human-agent teams, where agent ownership and role clarity face the same hierarchy and ambiguity problems. <span class="src">What Companies Get Wrong About Decision Rights</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither article specifies concrete criteria or thresholds for resolving the "learning-authority dilemma" — how an organization should decide when an ICA's demonstrated capability warrants an expansion of its formal decision rights, beyond citing the pharmaceutical revenue-forecasting example as an illustration after the fact.
- The promised "long-form report" mentioned at the end of the October 2024 article is not itself in the vault, so it's unclear whether further empirical detail beyond these two articles exists to substantiate the six-industry-group interview research cited in the January 2025 piece.
- The AI Decision Matrix draws on 30 interviews and one detailed telecom case, and does not say how decision types should be reclassified as an agent's track record improves.
