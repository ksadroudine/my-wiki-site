## Summary

Organizational bottlenecks come in two kinds. A task bottleneck arises when a task waits on the output of another task, and it depends on how centralized and how complex the work system is. A resource bottleneck arises when the needed resource is not available, and it depends on how flexibly resources can be reused, called fungibility, and on how much spare capacity, or slack, exists. A study of 28 large US airlines from 2002 to 2020 found that performance is best when each pair of factors is aligned, not when any one factor is minimized, and that task and resource bottlenecks interact. Southwest Airlines' storm meltdown in December 2022 and JetBlue's Valentine's Day failure in 2007 show what happens when factors are misaligned or slack is too thin. Managers can follow a five-step process of locating bottlenecks, classifying them and addressing each according to its type.

## Highlights

- Bottlenecks manifest as tasks stalled for one of two reasons: a task bottleneck (the task depends on output from another task that hasn't been completed yet) or a resource bottleneck (the resources needed to complete the task aren't available) — resource bottlenecks can be solved by investing in more resources, but task bottlenecks cannot necessarily be solved by simply throwing more resources at the problem.
- Task bottlenecks depend on two work-system factors: centralization (the degree to which many activities flow into or out of a few connecting tasks, creating dependencies — a hospital with one shared blood lab serving every specialty is centralized; separate labs per specialty is decentralized) and complexity (the number of tasks in a sequence before it repeats — diagnosing a rare infectious disease is more complex than treating common appendicitis).
- The research, based on 28 of the largest US airlines across 1,201 quarterly observations from 2002-2020, finds performance is best not by minimizing centralization or complexity independently but by aligning them: low-complexity work performs best when centralized (short cycles need less coordination, so centralizing is simpler and cheaper than running redundant parallel tasks), while high-complexity work performs best when decentralized (long cycles carry more failure risk, so running similar tasks in parallel reduces the chance of one blockage stalling the entire system).
- Resource bottlenecks depend on two resource-portfolio factors: fungibility (the degree a resource can be applied to multiple uses — an infusion pump is more fungible than a dialysis machine, which is essentially single-purpose) and slack (how much of a resource is available at any given time, providing a buffer against demand surges) — a company benefits from more slack only when its resources are also fungible, and benefits from fungibility only when those resources are also available (slack), since a resource that is either present-but-inapplicable or applicable-but-absent doesn't help.
- Southwest Airlines' response to a severe December 2022 winter storm illustrates misaligned task-and-resource characteristics compounding each other: unlike rivals operating a hub-and-spoke model, Southwest's point-to-point model left planes and crews scattered across airports with none in position at some locations, and because its cycle lengths were long (high complexity) without slack resources available to reposition quickly, recovery time stretched into nearly 3,000 cancellations on a single day, followed by three more days of thousand-plus cancellations.
- JetBlue's 2007 Valentine's Day operational meltdown (affecting over 130,000 customers and resulting in the airline's "customer bill of rights") illustrates the downside of too little slack: insufficient personnel to handle both thousands of simultaneous rebookings and flight-crew reassignment turned a weather disruption into a systemic operational failure — but the article cautions that excess slack is also costly, since idle resources incur costs even when they're never needed.
- Task and resource bottlenecks interact rather than operate independently: even a well-aligned task system will produce task bottlenecks if resource bottlenecks exist elsewhere (since needed resources can't be allocated to unblock tasks), and even well-aligned resources (fungible and available) will produce resource bottlenecks if task misalignment keeps incomplete tasks holding their assigned resources hostage, preventing reallocation.
- Managers can follow a five-step practical process: locate and identify specific bottlenecks and classify each as task- or resource-driven; for task bottlenecks, evaluate whether the delay has a hidden coordination benefit (aggregating information for better decisions) before concluding it's pure waste, then determine the sequence's complexity and redesign centralization to match (short/simple stays centralized, long/complex gets decentralized or shortened); for resource bottlenecks, determine whether resources are missing because they're unavailable (add slack) or inapplicable (add fungibility), while remembering that both excess slack and excess specificity carry real costs.

## Concept

<span class="src">Improve Workflows by Managing Bottlenecks</span> (MIT SMR, Samina Karim, Chi-Hyon Lee, and Manuela N. Hoehn-Weiss, December 2024) argues that organizational bottlenecks — a legal review process delaying high-value contracts, a computing-resource shortage slowing a digital initiative — are best managed not by addressing each one piecemeal as it appears, but by taking a holistic view of how an organization's work systems and resource portfolios are designed and aligned with each other. The research, based on the scheduled US domestic passenger airline industry (28 of the largest carriers, 1,201 quarterly carrier-time observations from 2002 through 2020 drawn from US Department of Transportation public data), finds the underlying dynamics generalize to any sector where organizations accomplish activities using specific resources, not just aviation. Bottlenecks manifest in one of two forms: a task bottleneck, where a task is stalled because it depends on the output of another task that hasn't yet been completed (teams waiting on legal or compliance approval, for instance), or a resource bottleneck, where the resources required to complete a task simply aren't available (a construction project with only one crane and multiple competing demands for it). This distinction matters practically because resource bottlenecks can usually be solved by investing in more resources, while task bottlenecks generally cannot — simply adding resources to a task-dependency problem doesn't remove the dependency itself. <span class="src">Improve Workflows by Managing Bottlenecks</span>

Task bottlenecks depend on two properties of a work system. Centralization describes the extent to which many activities in a system flow into or out of a small number of connecting tasks, creating dependencies between otherwise-separate activities — a hospital where every specialty runs its own blood lab is decentralized, while a hospital with one shared lab serving every specialty is centralized; centralization can slow individual processing but also delivers real coordination benefits, since running one shared test and distributing results to multiple groups is more efficient than each group independently requesting the same test. Complexity describes the number of tasks that make up a work sequence before it repeats — evaluating one acquisition bid before restarting the process for the next bid is comparatively simple, while diagnosing and treating a rare infectious disease involves a much longer, more variable sequence of tasks before repeating, and longer sequences carry more risk that any single task fails and ripples through the entire cycle, lengthening overall completion time. The research's central finding is that companies perform best not by minimizing centralization or complexity in isolation, but by aligning the two: low-complexity work (short sequences of similar, well-understood tasks) performs best centralized, since coordination needs are modest and centralizing is cheaper than running redundant parallel tasks; high-complexity work (long sequences with more failure points) performs best decentralized, running tasks in parallel — dividing workers into subgroups so that if one subgroup stalls, another can still complete its portion — since parallel execution reduces the risk that a single blockage stalls the entire system. Software R&D projects that modularize components to develop independently before integration exemplify this high-complexity, decentralized pattern in practice. <span class="src">Improve Workflows by Managing Bottlenecks</span>

Resource bottlenecks depend on a parallel pair of properties in an organization's resource portfolio. Fungibility describes the degree to which a resource can be applied across multiple uses and contexts — an infusion pump, usable for delivering various fluids to different patients, is highly fungible, while a dialysis machine, built specifically to treat kidney disease, has low fungibility (high specificity). Slack describes how much of a resource sits available at any given time, providing a buffer to absorb unpredictable demand surges — a crane may be scarce during a construction boom and freely available in a slow season, and while having slack is obviously valuable when demand spikes, idle resources still incur real ongoing costs, so excess slack carries its own downside. The research finds these two resource properties are similarly interdependent rather than independently valuable: an organization gains performance from having more slack resources only when those resources are also sufficiently fungible, and gains performance from resource fungibility only when the fungible resources in question are actually available (i.e., also slack) — a consulting firm with an available energy-sector specialist gains nothing when the assignment needs a banking specialist (available but not fungible), and gains nothing from a consultant fungible across banking and automotive if that consultant is already staffed on another case (fungible but not available). <span class="src">Improve Workflows by Managing Bottlenecks</span>

Because resources are needed to complete tasks, and completing tasks frees up resources for the next task, task bottlenecks and resource bottlenecks compound each other rather than operating independently — a well-aligned task system will still produce task bottlenecks if resource bottlenecks exist elsewhere in the system (needed resources simply can't be allocated to unblock the stalled task), and well-aligned, fungible-and-available resources will still produce resource bottlenecks if task misalignment keeps incomplete tasks holding their assigned resources hostage rather than releasing them for reallocation. A real-world comparison from December 2022 makes the interaction concrete: when a severe US winter storm forced weather-related cancellations across every major carrier, Southwest Airlines experienced a dramatically worse ripple effect than its rivals — nearly 3,000 cancellations on December 26 alone, followed by three more days of thousands more — specifically because its point-to-point route network (versus rivals' hub-and-spoke models) had scattered planes and crews across many airports with none positioned at some locations, and because its long flight-recovery cycles (high complexity) combined with insufficient slack resources meant repositioning crews and aircraft took far longer than its more centralized, better-buffered competitors. JetBlue's February 2007 Valentine's Day meltdown, which affected more than 130,000 customers and directly led to the airline adopting a formal "customer bill of rights," illustrates the opposite resource failure: insufficient staffing to simultaneously handle thousands of passenger rebookings and flight-crew reassignments turned a weather event into a systemic operational collapse, underscoring that operating "too lean," with very little slack, carries genuine disaster risk even though excess redundancy is also costly and should be avoided. <span class="src">Improve Workflows by Managing Bottlenecks</span>

The article translates these findings into a five-step practical process for managers. First, continuously scan for and identify specific bottlenecks, classifying each as task-driven (waiting on another task's output) or resource-driven (missing an applicable, available resource). Second, for task bottlenecks, evaluate whether the delay actually serves a hidden purpose — aggregating information for better coordination, decision-making, or quality assessment — before concluding it's pure waste to be eliminated; if there's no such benefit and the cause is poor communication about how long prior tasks take, the fix is better information-sharing between the people responsible for sequential tasks. Third, determine the complexity of the sequence in which the bottleneck sits and redesign centralization to match it: short, simple sequences should stay centralized, while sequences that are long and prone to frequent delay should be redesigned toward more parallel, decentralized execution (or shortened outright to reduce complexity). Fourth, having addressed task-system design, turn to whether missing resources — rather than task-sequence design — are actually the delay's root cause. Fifth, for genuine resource bottlenecks, determine whether the problem is unavailability (add slack, remembering that too much slack is also costly) or inapplicability (add fungibility, illustrated by low-cost carriers like Southwest and JetBlue deliberately narrowing the number of aircraft types they operate to simplify pilot training and increase plane-crew pairing fungibility, or restaurants cross-training staff on both customer-facing and back-of-house tasks). The article closes by stressing that managers should not only look for bottlenecks themselves but actively empower employees to identify and report them, since front-line staff often notice emerging bottlenecks well before they become visible at the management level. <span class="src">Improve Workflows by Managing Bottlenecks</span>

## Open Questions

- The article doesn't address how frequently an organization should reassess its centralization/complexity and fungibility/slack alignment as business conditions change — whether this is a one-time design decision or requires the kind of continuous monitoring the article's "empower employees to report bottlenecks" guidance implies.
- The airline-industry data source is not further specified for non-aviation sectors beyond the article's assertion that the findings "are generalizable to any sector" — no cross-industry validation study is cited to confirm the same alignment patterns (low-low and high-high performing better than misaligned combinations) hold outside the specific dynamics of scheduled passenger air travel.
