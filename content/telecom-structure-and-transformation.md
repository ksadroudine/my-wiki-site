## Summary

Telecom operators are restructuring both the shape of the business and the way their technology and teams are organized, because vertically integrated telcos suffer a conglomerate discount and carry a costly legacy of complexity. At the strategic level the options are delayering into a network company and service companies, in-market consolidation, or a shift toward a utility-like model, each with documented gains and real risks, and these sit alongside workforce, cyber, regulatory and sustainability priorities. At the operating level, business architecture, IT excellence and agile operating models together determine whether operators realize cost and speed benefits and whether they can scale AI, which amplifies rather than replaces sound architecture, data and integration fundamentals.

## Concept

A traditional, vertically integrated telecom operator bundles businesses with different economics, namely capital-intensive network infrastructure with long, utility-style payback, higher-value and higher-risk product innovation with faster payback, and commerce and customer-service channels that need skills closer to retail. Running all three under one management team dilutes leadership focus and forces compromises across different planning horizons and capital needs, which is a large part of why investors apply a conglomerate discount even when the operator owns valuable, high-multiple infrastructure assets. <span class="src">Seven urgent innovation priorities for telecom CEOs</span>

Delayering responds by separating the operator into a NetCo or InfraCo that operates network infrastructure, and may become a carrier-neutral wholesale access provider serving other operators, and one or more ServCos focused on products, sales and care that can specialize by customer type, offering, business model, investment horizon or skill set. Turkcell generated USD 150 million, 8% of revenue, within three years of moving digital services into stand-alone platforms, O2/CETIN in the Czech Republic saw a 27% rise in market capitalization in the year after its split, and some operators report up to a 10-percentage-point EBITDA margin uplift and a tenfold increase in enterprise value multiple. e& took a more elaborate route, splitting into five verticals that include two geographic telco units, a consumer digital unit, an enterprise digital unit and a capital-investments arm, which improved talent attraction and management focus. The record is nevertheless mixed, since delayering typically takes two to three years and over USD 1 billion in committed capital and no operator that has separated into NetCo and ServCo has yet ranked among the industry's top shareholder-return performers. <span class="src">Mapping the routes to renewed success for telcos</span> <span class="src">Bright Spots for Telcos in a Downturn</span> <span class="src">The critical bets on the future of telco value creation</span>

The downsides are structural. A ServCo loses automatic preferential access to network resources, so price differentiation, cross-subsidization and control over product development tied to network integration become harder under an arm's-length commercial relationship, and a disintermediated NetCo loses the customer insight that informed decisions on deployment, densification and maintenance. Separation can also work against seamless omnichannel bundling at a time when the industry is trying to become more customer-centric, and regimes such as the EU's GDPR complicate aggregating and monetizing customer data across separate entities. Success therefore depends on conditions that must be engineered, including genuinely independent units focused on pure-play growth, close collaboration wherever legally permitted on data, best practice and governance, full performance transparency through three to four differentiated KPIs per entity with no hidden cross-subsidies, distinct talent and incentive models, sharper investor narratives that attract each entity's natural investor base, and openness to M&A, joint ventures and partnerships that the integrated structure foreclosed. <span class="src">Mapping the routes to renewed success for telcos</span>

Consolidation is the more traditional alternative or complement. Orange Spain's 2024 combination with MasMovil targets EUR 490 million of synergies after four years while serving nearly half of Spain's fixed and mobile subscribers, and the 2025 merger of Vodafone UK and Three UK, which created the UK's largest mobile operator, targets GBP 700 million by its fifth year. In-country consolidation can unlock synergies above 15% of combined capital expenditure and 30% of combined operating expenditure, but consolidation without disciplined cost transformation and rigorous post-merger integration can destroy value, so it deserves the same discipline as any other major capital allocation decision rather than being treated as an automatic win. <span class="src">The critical bets on the future of telco value creation</span>

A third, less drastic option is to shift the operator's archetype toward a utility-like model that deliberately trades growth ambition for efficiency, reliability and lower risk. It prioritizes leaner operations over volatile customer-facing growth, reduces capital intensity through investment sharing such as network sharing, joint infrastructure entities or third-party investment in wholesale capacity, and seeks regulatory support such as government funding, subsidized spectrum pricing or cost-based pricing under a regulatory-asset-base framework like that used for electricity and gas. Close to three-quarters of surveyed telco leaders say their organization would benefit from moving away from the integrated archetype within five to ten years, and the largest share of those favor the utility-like shift over delayering, even though it implicitly concedes that market-beating growth is not a realistic near-term goal in favor of the dependable cash flows income investors reward in utilities. <span class="src">Mapping the routes to renewed success for telcos</span>

PwC's research places business-model reinvention among seven urgent CEO priorities and names three others that structural moves do not address. The first is workforce renewal, with 46% of telecom employees doubting that their employer will exist in ten years and 32% planning to change jobs within twelve months, compounded by retiring senior network engineers and new skill demands from cloud, AI and IoT. The second is resilience to cyber risk and shifting regulation, with cloud-related threats the top security concern and regulatory deal scrutiny rising sharply, including a 50% increase in US CFIUS national-security reviews since 2020. The third is the sustainable carrier, which must address Scope 3 emissions along the supply chain while GenAI-driven compute demand adds pressure on net-zero commitments. <span class="src">Seven urgent innovation priorities for telecom CEOs</span>

Beneath the strategic choices lies the operating question of how technology and teams are organized, and Strategy& frames it as a complexity cost trap. Pressure from cost transformation, evolving customer expectations, multi-technology re-platforming and next-generation infrastructure investment leaves operators carrying the accumulated burden of legacy technology maintenance, rising labor costs and volatile external spend. Business architecture is proposed as the remedy, a blueprint spanning value-creation opportunities, business capabilities and underlying technology that gives leadership a shared taxonomy for aligning investment with strategy. Strategy& recommends building a capability map, embedding business architecture in strategic and financial planning as a distinct discipline, and creating a legacy relief factory that retires outdated technology in agile increments. <span class="src">Calling All Telecom Business Architects</span>

McKinsey's IT Excellence Diagnostic supplies empirical evidence. Benchmarking more than 20 operators across six dimensions, namely business functionality, operating model, engineering excellence, IT architecture, cloud, and data and AI, it finds that top-quartile operators spend nearly 30% less on IT relative to revenue, but only when they improve consistently across all dimensions, and one operator that transformed IT programmatically cut IT spending by as much as 40%. Most cloud migrations follow a simplistic lift-and-shift approach that fails to capture cost benefits, so operators with the highest adoption rates post the worst cost-efficiency ratios, while genuine leaders build cloud-native systems or substantially re-architect applications. More than 60% of operators have adopted agile methodologies but most do not realize the full benefit, and leaders industrialize a proof of concept in three to six months against 1.5 years for peers because of stronger architecture and engineering practices such as automated deployment pipelines and integrated product catalogs. <span class="src">Transforming telecom tech How IT excellence drives innovation and cost efficiency</span>

Interviews with six telcos, Cogeco, Entel, Magyar Telekom, Masmovil, Safaricom and Tesco Mobile, two to five years into agile transformations, examine the operating-model dimension. All six changed four elements in parallel, structure through cross-functional squads and tribes instead of functional silos, workflows through a unified quarterly business review linking strategy to team-level OKRs, talent by shifting from role-based to skill-based models, and culture as leaders moved from directive control to coaching, and companies that tried to limit change to one element eventually had to touch all four to see impact. Several interviewees tie the agile model to effective AI deployment, since Masmovil's cross-functional teams found and scaled use cases area by area and only end-to-end-owned units, rather than siloed function-specific pilots, can apply AI and agents to the processes with the greatest potential. <span class="src">Inside agile telcos Organizing to value and what it takes to succeed</span>

Deutsche Telekom shows disciplined execution of both architecture and operating model inside one company. Its CIO, Peter Leukert, describes an IT organization supporting a fiber rollout targeting about 17.5 million homes passed in Germany by 2027 and a customer experience benchmarked against over-the-top players, with time to market as the key value metric. The team built a custom operational support system for fiber services in nine months instead of buying a product, later licensing it to external partners, and reports a 50% productivity increase in customer service from better digital tooling, with GenAI expected to add another 50%. He insists the key is integration rather than building or training models, and the company uses TM Forum's Open Digital Architecture to build shared software once and deploy it across its eleven European operating countries, which delivers the modularity and reuse that business architecture aims for. <span class="src">Deutsche Telekom’s CIO on how new approaches to IT can drive growth</span>

Across these sources the argument runs from strategy to execution. Structural choices such as delayering, consolidation or a utility-like shift change what the operator is, while business architecture supplies the blueprint of what to build, IT excellence and agile operating models determine whether cost and speed benefits are realized, and the common finding is that AI adoption amplifies rather than substitutes for IT fundamentals. Clean data, disciplined integration, sound governance and architectural simplification decide whether AI use cases scale into measurable EBITDA impact, not the choice of model. <span class="src">Calling All Telecom Business Architects</span> <span class="src">Deutsche Telekom’s CIO on how new approaches to IT can drive growth</span> <span class="src">Transforming telecom tech How IT excellence drives innovation and cost efficiency</span> <span class="src">Inside agile telcos Organizing to value and what it takes to succeed</span>

## Open Questions

- None of the sources reports a telco that reversed a delayering decision and recombined its network and service entities, so it is not known whether such reversals occur or when they might make sense.
- The sources do not say how an operator starting from scratch should sequence business-architecture design against an agile operating-model transformation, or whether the two should proceed in parallel. <span class="src">Calling All Telecom Business Architects</span> <span class="src">Inside agile telcos Organizing to value and what it takes to succeed</span>
