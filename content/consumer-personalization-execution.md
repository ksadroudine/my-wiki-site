## Summary

Forrester's operational framework for executing consumer personalization — strategy (four questions and four depths), data (four data types and static-versus-dynamic frequency), and technology (five functional categories — recognition, insights, decisioning, delivery, optimization). Grounded in survey data showing consumers resist personalization early in the customer lifecycle even as companies over-invest there.

## Highlights

- Consumer sentiment on personalization is inconsistent with how companies actually deploy it: 54% of US online adults won't share personal information for more relevant advertising and only 27% want personalized emails, resistance concentrated in the early discover/evaluate/commit stages of the customer lifecycle — yet 42% say personalized experiences are an important part of loyalty programs once a relationship is established, and willingness to share data varies sharply by industry (only 12% want personalized banking advice versus 28% open to sharing for better retail experiences), since consumers extend more trust where less-sensitive information is involved. <span class="src">The State Of Consumer Personalization, 2022</span>
- Personalization strategy starts with defining depth along a spectrum: segmentation (bucketing customers into defined groups, the most established and rudimentary approach), discrete interactions (single-moment, next-best-offer suggestions triggered by one action), preset personas or customer-determined journeys (mapping a customer to a track like "sensitive skin" or "toddler stage" that shapes ongoing communication), and contextual understanding that anticipates individual needs in real time — the deepest form, formalized as Next Best Experience (NBX) and enabled by real-time interaction management. <span class="src">A Strategy Guide to Consumer Personalization</span>
- Before touching data or technology, Forrester's strategy framework requires answering four questions in order: what's the actual consumer need (led by customer research, not assumption); what's the specific business objective and KPI (since "we can personalize" isn't a strategy); what's the tactic and where in the customer lifecycle it applies; and who needs to be involved — personalization's central organizational paradox is that everyone owns it (insights, data/analytics, CRM, IT, marketing) yet no single function does. <span class="src">A Strategy Guide to Consumer Personalization</span>
- Data is personalization's fuel, and it comes in four types distinguished by provenance and how known versus unknown the customer is: zero-party data (proactively, explicitly shared by the customer — preferences, purchase intentions, personal context), first-party data (a company's own transactional, demographic, behavioral, and implied-interest data), second-party data (shared between two partnering companies, such as a financial-services firm and a retailer combining data sets), and third-party data (purchased affinity or contextual data, increasingly falling out of favor as privacy regulation tightens) — plus a second axis of static data (infrequently updated, like birth date, used to build audience segments) versus dynamic data (updated constantly, like location or energy use, used to trigger a specific personalized moment). <span class="src">A Data Primer for Consumer Personalization</span>
- Companies consistently hit the same three data problems in execution: missing data (a needed attribute simply isn't captured, turning a quick test into a new data-collection project), siloed data (departmental and even within-department silos mean, for example, social media's learned preferences never reach CRM or email), and non-actionable data (a database is centrally aggregated but too stale, disjointed, or unclean to drive real-time decisions) — with 80% of B2C CMOs (up from 45% two quarters earlier) reporting that rising consumer privacy protections are changing their view of what personalization can even involve. <span class="src">A Data Primer for Consumer Personalization</span>
- There is no single "personalization technology" category — only 19% of B2C marketers using enterprise martech say one vendor covers everything they need, and the average program stitches together eight discrete technologies across five functional categories: recognition (pinpointing who the customer is, from customer data platforms to zero-party-data solutions to identity resolution), insights (customer/journey/digital-intelligence analytics that turn data into a next-best-offer or -action decision), decisioning (rules-based through ML/AI systems, real-time interaction management, and journey orchestration platforms that determine the actual next-best-experience), delivery (channel-specific and cross-channel tools — email, SMS, app, commerce, customer service — that execute the interaction), and optimization (marketing resource management, measurement, and experience-optimization platforms that close the feedback loop). <span class="src">A Technology Overview of Consumer Personalization</span>
- When evaluating personalization vendors, Forrester recommends asking which of the five functional categories a tool actually supports (versus its marketing claims), what depth of personalization it can execute, what level of identity resolution it achieves (full resolution isn't always necessary — segmentation-level tactics don't need it), how automated its decisioning genuinely is (rules-based decisioning "still omnipresent" behind AI marketing claims, and human supervision remains necessary), how it measures and optimizes impact (blending customer-facing metrics like NPS with company metrics like incremental sales), and how deeply it integrates with the rest of the martech ecosystem rather than operating as a disconnected point solution. <span class="src">A Technology Overview of Consumer Personalization</span>

## Concept

Forrester's survey research paints a picture of genuine mismatch between how eagerly companies pursue personalization and how consumers actually experience it. Personalization is often equated by consumers with intrusive marketing rather than genuine service — one research participant described it bluntly as coming "with privacy violations," while another wanted brands to simply "leave me be." This resistance concentrates specifically at the start of the customer relationship: 54 percent of US online adults are unwilling to share personal information in exchange for more relevant advertising, and only 27 percent want personalized emails at all, with individualized messaging facing the most pushback in the early discover, evaluate, and commit stages before any real relationship exists. That resistance softens meaningfully once trust is established — 42 percent of US online adults consider personalized experiences an important part of loyalty programs specifically, and consumer comfort with data sharing varies sharply by how sensitive the information involved actually is: only 12 percent of people who recently opened a bank account want personalized banking advice, compared to 28 percent willing to share information for a better retail shopping experience, since financial services and healthcare carry inherently more sensitive data than retail. The strategic implication is that companies overinvesting in aggressive personalization early in the customer lifecycle, before any relationship exists to justify it, are working directly against the grain of consumer sentiment — the deeper, more individualized forms of personalization are something companies need to earn through demonstrated value, not something they can front-load. <span class="src">The State Of Consumer Personalization, 2022</span>

Getting the strategy right starts with defining what depth of personalization is actually appropriate for a given moment, since not every customer interaction warrants the same level of individualization. Forrester lays out a spectrum running from segmentation (bucketing customers into broad, predefined groups — the most established approach, dating back decades, and still the right tool for many use cases), through discrete interactions (single-moment, next-best-offer or next-best-action suggestions triggered by one specific action, like a browsed product prompting a related recommendation), to preset personas or customer-determined journeys (mapping a customer into an ongoing track — a beauty brand's "dry skin" persona, or a car-seat maker's "toddler" life stage — that shapes an extended sequence of future communications), up to contextual understanding that anticipates individual needs in real time, the deepest and most demanding form, formalized under the separate concept of Next Best Experience (NBX) and requiring real-time interaction management technology to actually deliver. Rather than jumping straight to "what data do we need" and "what tech should we buy," Forrester insists strategy has to come first, structured around four questions asked in a specific order: what does the consumer actually need (grounded in real customer research rather than internal assumption, since "we can personalize, so we should" is explicitly rejected as a justification); what is the specific business objective and its measurable KPI, defined only after the consumer need is established; what is the specific tactic, and at which point or points in the customer lifecycle it should apply, whether as a single tactic at one stage or several tactics orchestrated across the whole journey; and who needs to be involved, since personalization sits inside customer insights, data and analytics, CRM, IT, and marketing simultaneously — meaning that, in practice, everyone owns personalization and yet no single function truly owns it, a genuine organizational paradox that has to be resolved deliberately rather than left to default to whichever team happens to control the budget. <span class="src">A Strategy Guide to Consumer Personalization</span>

If strategy is personalization's blueprint, data is its fuel — and Forrester organizes consumer data along two independent axes. The first axis is provenance and degree of customer "knownness": zero-party data is the most valuable and rarest type, proactively and explicitly volunteered by the customer (preference-center selections, stated purchase intentions, personal context, and how they want to be recognized), inherently signaling permission and intimacy; first-party data is a company's own directly collected transactional, demographic, behavioral, and implied-interest data (time on a product page, apps frequented, loyalty and customer-service activity), generally making a customer more known than unknown across their history with the company; second-party data results from two companies deliberately combining their respective customer data sets under partnership (a financial-services firm and a retailer cross-referencing which household income bands correlate with certain purchases, for instance); and third-party data, purchased or licensed from outside sources like affinity segments or contextual data, is explicitly described as falling out of favor as data-deprecation and privacy regulation intensify. The second, independent axis is data frequency: static data (birth date, gender, parental status, mailing address) updates rarely enough that refresh speed barely matters, and is used to build defined audience segments; dynamic data (location, temperature, energy consumption, biometric activity) must be captured and acted on in near-real time or genuinely real time, and is what actually triggers a specific personalized moment rather than merely defining who's eligible for one. In practice, Forrester finds companies running into the same three failure modes repeatedly, often simultaneously: missing data, where a needed attribute simply isn't captured, turning what should be a quick test into an entirely new data-collection and CRM-schema project; siloed data, where departmental and even within-department silos (a classic example being social media learning a preference that never reaches CRM or the email team) prevent any single, complete view of the customer from forming; and non-actionable data, where information does get centrally aggregated but remains too stale, disjointed, or unclean to actually drive real-time or near-real-time decisions. These problems are intensifying rather than easing: 80 percent of B2C CMOs surveyed said rising consumer privacy protections are actively changing their view of what marketing personalization can even involve, up sharply from 45 percent just two quarters earlier — meaning the practical data foundation for personalization is getting harder to build even as strategic ambition for it grows. Forrester's recommended sequence for tackling this is to identify exactly what data a specific tactic requires, locate where that data currently lives across the organization's inevitable silos, build a concrete plan to access and activate it (respecting each data owner's governance and integrity requirements along the way), and finally verify data quality, since running personalization off unreliable or improperly matched "unknown person" data produces measurably less accurate results than personalization built on properly resolved, first-party data. <span class="src">A Data Primer for Consumer Personalization</span>

Even with strategy and data in place, execution demands technology — but Forrester is explicit that there is no single "personalization technology" category to buy: personalization capability is instead scattered across many overlapping technology categories, and only 19 percent of B2C marketers who use enterprise marketing technology believe a single vendor can supply everything they need, with the average program stitching together roughly eight discrete technologies. Forrester organizes the technology landscape into five functional categories that any complete personalization stack must cover, whether through one platform or several coordinated ones. Recognition technology pinpoints who the customer actually is, spanning customer data platforms (which unify contact information, purchase history, and preferences, though typically incompletely), zero-party-data and preference-center solutions, identity resolution tools that link a customer's identifiers across channels and devices, and journey-mapping platforms — an area where "creating a single view of the customer across channels" remains, per Forrester's own 2023 survey data, among B2C marketers' top execution challenges. Insights technology turns that recognized data into understanding of the customer's context — journey stage, behavior, intent — via customer analytics, journey analytics, and digital intelligence platforms, aiming toward the ideal of Next Best Experience decision-making grounded in everything known about a customer rather than a short-term sales-driven heuristic. Decisioning technology is where that understanding actually becomes a specific next-best-offer, -action, or -experience determination, spanning the full range from simple rules-based logic through machine-learning and AI-driven models, real-time interaction management (RTIM) systems, creative adtech that dynamically assembles and tests ad variants, and customer journey orchestration platforms that adjust a customer's path in near-real time based on their behavior. Delivery technology is personalization's "last mile," the channel-specific and cross-channel tools — advertising technology, cross-channel marketing hubs, commerce technology, and customer service platforms — that actually execute an interaction across email, SMS, app, website, social, in-store, or contact-center touchpoints, each with its own formatting requirements and quirks. And optimization technology runs continuously in the background across marketing resource management, marketing measurement and optimization, and experience-optimization platforms, closing the feedback loop by measuring both customer-facing value (like Net Promoter Score) and business value (like incremental sales) to continually refine future insights and decisioning — sometimes using reinforcement learning to improve decisioning models automatically over time. Given this fragmentation, Forrester's recommended vendor-vetting checklist asks which of the five functional categories a given tool genuinely supports (as opposed to its marketing language); what depth of personalization it can actually execute, matched against the organization's own data maturity and business goals; what level of identity resolution it achieves, since not every use case (simple segmentation, for instance) actually requires full resolution; how automated its decisioning genuinely is, with an explicit warning to be skeptical of vendors overpromising AI, since rules-based decisioning remains "omnipresent" underneath much of what's marketed as AI-driven, and human supervision of any automation remains necessary; how it measures and optimizes personalization's impact, blending customer-facing and company-facing metrics; and to what degree it actually integrates with the rest of the organization's CX, marketing, sales, commerce, and customer-service technology ecosystem, since deeper integration is generally preferable but not always necessary depending on how much and how quickly data genuinely needs to flow between systems. <span class="src">A Technology Overview of Consumer Personalization</span>

## Open Questions

- The sources don't reconcile how a company should sequence investment across the five technology categories when budget is limited — whether, for instance, recognition (data foundation) should always precede decisioning (the more visible, ROI-attributable layer) in practice.
