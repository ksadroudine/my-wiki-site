## Summary

A cloud strategy decides where each workload belongs, what the move will cost and how each workload will migrate, and it works best as a staged, jointly owned journey rather than a one-time decision. The concept follows that journey from the unplanned, department-driven origins of cloud adoption and a five-stage planning process, through the economics of capital versus operating expense and the hidden costs of data centers and hybrid environments, to the seven migration strategies from rehost to retain and the program-level traps and assurance practices that determine whether a transformation succeeds. A recurring finding is that carelessly executed lift-and-shift migration often raises costs, while rightsizing and workload-by-workload choices lower them.

## Concept

Cloud adoption in most organizations did not begin as a strategy. It began when departmental developers, frustrated by slow internal procurement, turned directly to inexpensive, elastic, self-service public cloud resources. This solved the local problem but created an aggregate one, because small bills added up to significant uncontrolled spend and security and governance went unmanaged while nobody tracked the full footprint. IT's first reaction, treating the trend as a threat and in some documented cases working to undermine the credibility of cloud services, is presented as the familiar pattern of incumbent functions resisting any technology that challenges an existing power structure. What forced deliberate strategy was competition, since a generation of companies built around the cloud from the start, without the lead time of physical infrastructure, made adoption stop being optional for incumbents in competitive markets. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.17 Planning Your Cloud Strategy</span>

The business imperative also shapes how to plan. Legacy applications resist a simple lift and shift because their monolithic architecture and web of dependencies mean a wholesale move consumes significant compute and storage while delivering no strategic advantage, and the code stays just as hard to adapt to new business requirements after the move. The benefit requires modernizing first, by removing dependencies and redesigning the application as modular, reusable services. Transformation also does not require betting everything on becoming a digital native. The source's example is a century-old furniture retailer facing cloud-native online competitors that builds a hybrid model, layering online ordering, in-store pickup and data-driven personalization over its existing strengths of design consultation and local reputation. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.2 Embracing the Business Imperative</span>

A deliberate strategy is described as a five-stage journey rather than a single decision. The first stage is an honest assessment of the current IT strategy and infrastructure, using the metaphor of a garage built for two cars that gradually filled with unrelated clutter. The diagnostic question is whether the estate is a well-orchestrated, flexible system or an accumulation of disconnected silos, and the assessment requires a joint task force of business and IT leaders because it must also catalog where business-critical data lives. The second stage is imagining where the business and its competitors will be in six months and in three years, and it is explicitly not a one-time exercise, since a plan calibrated to one snapshot goes stale as fast as the market changes. The third stage is exploring options directly, through peer benchmarking and hands-on use of free trials and open-source tools, on the logic that direct experience produces better questions even if a service provider later helps with execution. The fourth stage is creating the hybrid cloud plan jointly, oriented around microservices rather than monolithic applications, grounded in the organization's knowledge of its industry and its key partner, supplier and customer relationships, and accounting for latency, cost, reliability and regulation, including jurisdiction-specific data residency law, when deciding where each workload should run. The fifth stage is a genuinely staged rollout that starts by bringing already adopted public cloud workloads under management and negotiating better collective vendor terms, then picks a few visible, well-suited early projects, such as a cross-departmental SaaS application, a container experiment or a time-boxed development project, to build momentum before broader rollout. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.17 Planning Your Cloud Strategy</span>

Execution requires planning across three service categories that are consumed and delivered differently. Consumer-facing services reach customers, employees and partners, and everything beneath them is hidden from that audience, who care only that the service is predictable. Provider services are the fuller infrastructure, platform and software stack, service management platform and integration services sourced from vendors to support corporate-grade security and service levels. Service creation services are the run-time services used to build new offerings from public and private platforms combined with the company's or its industry's intellectual property, which is the layer where differentiation is built rather than consumed. Placement should follow the nature of the workload instead of one blanket policy, so that a workload with stringent security needs suits a private cloud, or a public cloud carrying a specific security warranty, while a purely temporary capacity need suits commodity public cloud. Cost-benefit analysis resists a universal formula for the same reason. A SaaS application can cost more in annual fees than the hardware and maintenance it replaces and still be the right choice because of the productivity and responsiveness it enables, while a company whose core business is itself a set of cloud-like services may find in-house capability cheaper than any vendor once it has the scale to buy inputs more cheaply than an intermediary. The source's broader argument is to view the hybrid cloud through business agility and technical feasibility together rather than optimizing either alone. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.17 Planning Your Cloud Strategy</span>

Evaluating the economics is harder than it first appears. Initial costs for a narrow use case such as testing, one application or some storage are often genuinely low, so management can be unprepared when costs rise sharply as usage expands, which is usually a logical consequence of the workload's characteristics rather than vendor overcharging. A move delivers an unambiguous benefit in three patterns. These are temporary or bursty capacity needs, where permanent infrastructure for a short spike would be wasteful, SaaS replacements for inconsistent in-house tools, particularly where inconsistency itself costs deals or accuracy because teams run different versions of the same tool, and massively scaled but relatively simple applications such as email, where a vendor's standardization and scale are hard for any single data center to match. Workloads with unusual data-sovereignty or governance constraints, aging monoliths with deep dependencies and specialized workloads used occasionally by a small group are often better left in place. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.16 Breaking Down Cloud Economics</span>

The foundational financial distinction is between capital expenditure, which is investment in owned or leased assets such as servers, real estate and licenses that is depreciated over time, and operating expense, the ongoing cost of running the business, which covers most cloud services. Organizations are typically more constrained in capital than in operating spend, so shifting cost from the first to the second frees capital for other investments and improves agility, since an underperforming initiative can be cut or a promising one ramped up far more easily when the spend is operating rather than sunk capital. This is separate from unit-cost comparison, because a cloud virtual machine can look more expensive than a depreciated physical server over three years and still make business sense once this flexibility is weighed. The deeper difficulty is that most organizations lack an accurate picture of what an application costs to run on premises. A full accounting must include server and storage costs (amortized total cost of ownership, complicated by one server often supporting several applications), network, backup, archive, continuity and disaster-recovery costs, data center overhead such as electricity, floor space and cooling, software maintenance and operations staff, most of which resist attribution to a single application. It also explains why removing a handful of workloads rarely produces proportional savings, since fixed costs do not shrink until the removal is large enough to reduce floor space, cooling or staffing, and a data center running at 40 percent utilization will not get materially cheaper by moving a small slice to the cloud. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.16 Breaking Down Cloud Economics</span>

A hybrid deployment adds cost categories that a capital-versus-operating comparison misses entirely, namely the overhead of managing several environments instead of one, data transfer fees for both the initial migration and ongoing movement, application customization to work in the hybrid configuration, integration with everything the application depends on, and extra compliance auditing. Given how hard all of this is to model, vendor-provided usage estimators and total-cost-of-ownership calculators are useful for rough orientation but should be treated skeptically, because many are marketing tools built to reach a cloud-positive conclusion. The recommended response is a documented internal policy, agreed in advance, on which categories of workload and data must stay in a traditional data center or private cloud and why, instead of deciding migration case by case from one calculator's output. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). The 7 Rs framework offers a menu of migration strategies on the premise that different workloads warrant different approaches depending on their complexity, business criticality and the organization's tolerance for cost, risk and disruption. It evolved in three stages. Gartner introduced the original five Rs early in the cloud era, AWS added Retire to formalize the idea that planning should start by asking whether an application still delivers value at all, and AWS's current model added Retain to acknowledge that not every application can or should move, giving organizations a legitimate way to keep systems on premises as a deliberate choice. <span class="src">The 7 Rs of Cloud Migration 7 Strategies Explained</span>

The seven strategies span a spectrum from minimal to maximal change. Rehost, or lift and shift, redeploys an application and its dependencies onto infrastructure as a service as it is, which is the easiest and lowest-risk option and needs the least in-house cloud-native expertise but limits access to capabilities such as autoscaling. Relocate, the newest addition, moves an entire collection of servers from an on-premises platform such as VMware or Kubernetes to a managed cloud equivalent such as Amazon EKS or Google GKE without rewriting code or disrupting live operations, which is the fastest strategy but caps scalability and makes it hard to scale back down later. Replatform, or lift and reshape, applies targeted platform-level optimizations while leaving core code and architecture untouched, letting teams modernize selectively and saving time and cost compared with a full refactor. Refactor, or re-architect, rebuilds a workload from the ground up for serverless computing, autoscaling and distributed load balancing, often splitting a monolith into microservices. It is the most future-proof and typically yields substantially lower operating costs, but it needs the largest investment, extensive training and continuous cost monitoring, and is not recommended for many applications at once. Repurchase, or drop and shop, replaces an internally managed system with a third-party service, typically SaaS, converting fixed IT cost into consumption-based subscription and unlocking cloud-native capability quickly, though it can be expensive for low-usage applications and cedes the pace of updates to the vendor. Retire terminates or downsizes applications that no longer deliver production value, which costs least and eliminates spend on idle resources, but is risky if done without confirming that nothing else depends on the workload. Retain keeps a workload where it is because it depends on an application that must migrate first, has no immediate business case, is expected to gain a SaaS option from its vendor, or must stay in a local data center for compliance or security, and it risks becoming a permanent excuse to avoid beneficial modernization if not reassessed periodically. <span class="src">The 7 Rs of Cloud Migration 7 Strategies Explained</span>

In practice an organization migrating a large portfolio applies several Rs at once, matching each workload's complexity, cost sensitivity and tolerance for disruption to the strategy that suits it, because the trade-offs in investment, resulting flexibility and resulting cost differ greatly between a quick rehost of a stable legacy system and a full refactor of a strategic, heavily used application. Bain's analysis warns that the easiest strategy is also the one most likely to disappoint if executed carelessly. In a survey of more than 350 IT decision makers, the most common complaint was that expected savings had not materialized, and direct-match migration of an overprovisioned workload ran 10 to 15 percent more expensive in the cloud because the existing excess compute and storage capacity simply moves along. The remedy is to treat migration as a chance to rationalize workloads, by assessing usage patterns and peak demand to inform downsizing, identifying storage patterns and proximity requirements, and rationalizing core-processor counts to reduce processor-tied licensing, which commonly surfaces fragmentation, duplication, incomplete records and zombie servers that were never decommissioned. Done well, rightsizing cuts costs by 30 to 60 percent, and a TSO Logic analysis found that a company with 105,000 server instances and 20 terabytes of on-premises storage would have faced a 22 percent cost increase from a direct-match rehost but a 36 percent reduction from rightsizing first. Savings also vary by workload, because nonproduction workloads gain more than production ones (their volatile usage matches cloud elasticity) and nonvirtualized workloads gain more than virtualized ones (virtualization has already captured some of the utilization gains). The implication is that rehost and poorly executed rehost are not the same strategy, and even the easiest option requires real workload analysis. <span class="src">Optimize, Don’t ‘Lift and Shift,’ When Migrating to the Cloud</span>

Microsoft's glossary places the 7 Rs within a broader taxonomy of data migration, of which cloud migration is only one of five overlapping categories, the others being storage migration, database migration (usually requiring conversion because the format changes), application migration and business process migration, such as moving data during a merger or product launch. It also distinguishes migration, which moves data from one place to another, from conversion, which transforms data from one format to another so a new system can read it. Strategies such as refactor and repurchase often require both, and a project can include conversion as one step without the sequencing being obvious in advance. Microsoft outlines a standard three-phase process that generalizes across all seven strategies, namely planning, which discovers and maps existing data, assesses organizational impact and designs the approach and rate, implementing, which builds the solution and transfers data while monitoring for quality problems, duplicates and anomalies, and validating, which audits the migrated data for accuracy before the old system is decommissioned. <span class="src">What Is Data Migration</span>

BCG frames the planning problem from a program-risk angle. Spending with the three largest public cloud providers rose 337 percent over five years to $211 billion in 2023, and organizations that execute the transition well can cut infrastructure costs by up to 40 percent, raise productivity by up to 50 percent and speed time to market by up to 60 percent, yet a smooth migration remains difficult. Five traps commonly derail transformations even when the business case is sound. Treating the cloud as another data center through application-by-application lift and shift forfeits the productivity gains of platform services, an integrated DevSecOps team and infrastructure-as-code tooling. Failing to plan and manage costs leaves organizations accustomed to a capital-based data center model without the skills or monitoring to stop volatile operating spend from spiraling. Neglecting the timing of legacy decommissioning makes costs escalate while both environments run in parallel. Losing control to the cloud provider or systems integrator, chosen mainly on projected savings rather than proven experience migrating complex legacy workloads, extends timelines and leaves the organization unable to build its own capability or challenge its partners. Letting parallel programs proliferate across uncoordinated vendors erodes standardization, procurement economies of scale and internal skills. <span class="src">Cloud Transformation, Without the Risk</span>

Five corresponding success factors counter the traps. The first is treating the transition as a business transformation rather than an infrastructure program, anchored in a communicated vision for speed to value and resilience and in well-governed decisions about what, when and how to migrate. The second is a migration factory approach, in which each workload type gets a defined migration pattern that is piloted and then scaled in parallel across teams while lessons are fed back into the pattern. The third is active management of consumption through a FinOps function spanning business, technology and finance, which guards against over-consumption and against under-consumption that forfeits discounts tied to committed spend. The fourth is strict vendor oversight with internal staff making the key decisions, partners working alongside rather than in their own silo, agile contracts with shared productivity incentives and deliberate investment in in-house skills. The fifth is a proactive program assurance capability from the outset rather than a risk function that intervenes too late. BCG formalizes these into a de-risking framework with five elements, scope and objectives, business value and economics, governance and organization, solution and deliverables, and planning and execution, each carrying explicit go or no-go questions to be answered at three checkpoints: pressure-testing the strategy before the transition begins, testing scalability after a minimum-viable-product migration and before moving the remaining workloads, and promptly reassessing if execution stalls. It estimates that committing roughly 3 percent of the program budget to this assurance can save more than 30 times its cost, and it advises executives to demand complete answers on how scope aligns with business objectives, how risk will be managed, how value will be shown within budget, whether architecture governance is defined across the delivery lifecycle and whether the organization is building cloud skills for the long term rather than completing a one-time migration. <span class="src">Cloud Transformation, Without the Risk</span>

A companion set of do's and don'ts turns this planning into tactics. The do's are to build toward cloud-native architecture from the start rather than retrofit it, to establish data consistency and a common data catalog before integrating across silos, to choose and standardize cloud services deliberately, favoring vendors that support portability standards such as Kubernetes, Istio and Docker, to build a unified service management view across all deployment models, to plan for portability, including an inexpensive way to move data out of a vendor relationship, before a problem forces the issue, and to treat security as a first-class element of the plan. The don'ts are to avoid relying on a single vendor, since any vendor can suffer a catastrophic outage, which argues for replicating critical applications across regions or providers, to avoid over-committing to bulk-license discounts before confirming that usage will reach the committed volume, because vendors rarely let a contract be scaled down, to avoid assuming the cloud removes IT's duty to monitor vendor performance, since the business blames IT for an outage whoever's infrastructure failed, to avoid letting the on-premises data center atrophy, because it remains viable for the workloads it suits and needs continued optimization, and to avoid skipping SLA fine print, since no vendor compensates for lost business and at most refunds the fee for the disrupted service. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). ## Open Questions

- The planning sources date from 2020 and frame the exploration stage around free trials and peer benchmarking without explaining how to compare vendor total cost of ownership at scale, and the economics chapter lists which cost categories to account for but gives no benchmark prices.
- The sources do not say how to sequence a large migration across the 7 Rs when many applications qualify for more than one strategy, beyond the general advice that workload complexity and business case should drive the choice.
- The Microsoft glossary entry does not say which of the five data migration categories or which of the 7 Rs most often require heavy data conversion, so the interaction between the two frameworks remains abstract rather than empirically grounded.
