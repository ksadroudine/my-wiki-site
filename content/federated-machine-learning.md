## Summary

Federated learning lets several organizations train a shared AI model on their combined data without any of them handing over raw data, because the training algorithm travels to each organization's data instead of the data traveling to a central location. Combined with encryption, it has allowed Zurich Insurance to improve a prediction model using Orange's data and has let competing hospitals and banks cooperate without revealing their records. The sources classify an organization's data into four types, poor, horizontal, vertical and rich, according to how many samples and how many features per sample it holds. The type of data an organization holds shows what kind of partner would improve its own AI systems, and organizations with rich data can still gain by contributing it to train other organizations' systems while keeping full ownership.

## Highlights

- Federated learning, combined with specialized encryption, lets an AI model or algorithm be trained using data from multiple decentralized servers controlled by different organizations while preserving the privacy of the individuals or organizations whose data is used — in effect, sending the algorithm to the data rather than sending the data to the algorithm.
- Zurich Insurance Group used a commercial federated learning platform to improve a predictive algorithm with data from telecom company Orange, without Orange ever releasing any data — the collaboration delivered a 30% improvement in Zurich's AI predictions and a significant revenue increase, while giving Orange a privacy-preserving way to monetize its data.
- Federated learning enables cooperation even between direct competitors: pathology departments at competing private hospitals train a shared diagnostic algorithm on their combined tissue-image data sets, with each hospital benefiting from a higher-performing algorithm than its own smaller data set could produce alone, and ownership shares of the resulting algorithm allocated based on each hospital's data contribution.
- An organization's data falls into one of four types based on two dimensions — sample count and feature count per sample: "poor" data (few samples, few features), "horizontal" data (many features per sample, but too few samples — needs more samples via horizontal learning), "vertical" data (many samples, but too few features per sample — needs more features via vertical learning), and "rich" data (sufficient on both dimensions).
- The right federated-learning partner depends on an organization's own data type: organizations with vertical data (many samples, thin per-sample detail) should seek partners in different industries who observe different things about the same population (a telecom and an insurer both serve overlapping customers but record entirely different variables about them); organizations with horizontal data (rich per-sample detail, too few samples) should seek partners within their own industry, even direct competitors, since more samples of the same variable set is what's needed.
- Organizations already sitting on rich data still have a reason to pursue federated learning: they can monetize that data by contributing it to train other organizations' AI systems while retaining full ownership and never releasing the underlying data itself — the Mayo Clinic's Solutions Studio program offers exactly this kind of access to global, federated, de-identified data.
- Banks use federated learning with multiparty homomorphic encryption to check whether a prospective client has been flagged by a competing bank as unreliable, without either bank disclosing its actual client list to the other — reducing the cost of "know your customer" compliance, which otherwise runs to roughly 3% of banks' operational costs globally.
- Federated learning also has an underused internal application: within a single organization, data-sharing barriers between legal entities, jurisdictions, or business units (often imposed for compliance or ethics reasons) can block one group from data another group already owns, and federated learning lets the organization use that data across such internal barriers while remaining compliant with the restrictions that created them in the first place.
- Six factors determine which organizations to pursue as federated-learning partners: the state of one's own data (poor/horizontal/vertical/rich); the resulting structure of what to seek (cross-industry for vertical data, same-industry for horizontal data); a logical starting point (one algorithm, one trusted partner, before scaling); the potential for data monetization; likely technical challenges (unstructured data needing conversion to machine-readable form, disparate labeling conventions across organizations requiring catalog synchronization); and employee buy-in, since resistance to collaborating with external organizations — especially competitors — is identified as the single most critical obstacle to federated learning success.

## Concept

<span class="src">Know Your Data to Harness Federated Machine Learning</span> (MIT SMR, José Parra-Moyano and Karl Schmedders, October 2024) opens from a competitive-strategy observation: deploying AI no longer guarantees an edge on its own, since what actually differentiates companies is access to diverse, extensive, high-quality data that improves their AI system's performance relative to competitors — but privacy concerns routinely limit how much unique, relevant data an organization can actually put to use. Privacy-preserving federated learning addresses this directly: combined with specialized encryption techniques, it lets an AI model or algorithm be trained using data held on multiple decentralized servers controlled by different organizations, all while respecting the privacy of the individuals or organizations whose data is involved — the technique is best summarized as sending the algorithm to the data, rather than sending the data to the algorithm. Zurich Insurance Group's collaboration with British telecom company Orange illustrates the mechanism concretely: using a commercial federated learning platform, Zurich's predictive algorithm was trained and its predictions improved using Orange's data, without Orange ever having to release that data to Zurich — the resulting 30% improvement in Zurich's AI predictions translated into a significant revenue increase for Zurich, while Orange gained a new, privacy-preserving way to monetize its data asset. A large bank's credit unit achieved a comparable result, improving its loan-default prediction accuracy by roughly 10% using data owned by one of the largest global telecommunications companies. <span class="src">Know Your Data to Harness Federated Machine Learning</span>

Real-world applications span both cross-industry and, counterintuitively, within-industry collaboration — including between direct competitors. Pathology departments at competing private hospitals, each struggling to compile a sufficiently robust data set alone, train a shared diagnostic algorithm on their combined tissue-image data; every participating hospital ends up with a higher-performing, potentially lifesaving diagnostic tool than its own in-house data set could have produced, with ownership shares of the resulting collectively developed algorithm allocated based on each hospital's proportional data contribution — a genuinely new, data-driven business model, since the shared algorithm can also be commercialized and rented to third-party hospitals, with resulting revenue split by ownership share. The financial sector applies a related but distinct pattern using multiparty homomorphic encryption: participating banks can check whether a prospective client has been flagged by a competing bank as unreliable or otherwise problematic, without either bank disclosing its actual client list to the other, reducing the cost of "know your customer" compliance processes that otherwise account for roughly 3% of banks' global operational costs while improving the customer's experience. Hospitals use the same encrypted-computing pattern for dermatology diagnostics trained across multiple countries' data sets, and for simpler distributed analytics producing personalized reference intervals for healthy versus sick patients, letting doctors diagnose against a personalized threshold rather than a one-size-fits-all population reference. <span class="src">Know Your Data to Harness Federated Machine Learning</span>

The article's central practical contribution is a framework for identifying which external partner an organization should actually pursue, built on two dimensions of its own data: the number of samples (clients, patients, policies — whatever the AI system aims to understand) and the number of features recorded per sample. Crossing these dimensions yields four data types. "Poor" data has both too few samples and too few features per sample to be useful as-is. "Vertical" data has many samples but too few features recorded about each — a large customer base, thinly described — and requires "vertical learning" (adding more features per sample) to become genuinely useful; the fix here is partnering with organizations in different industries, since companies serving different purposes for overlapping populations tend to record entirely different variables about the same people (the Orange-Zurich case is the given example: the telecom records communications and mobility data, the insurer records risk aversion, consumption, and major life events, about what can be a substantially overlapping customer base). "Horizontal" data has many features recorded per sample but too few samples — deep description, thin population — and requires "horizontal learning" (adding more samples with the same feature set) to become useful; the fix here is partnering within one's own industry, even with direct competitors, since same-industry organizations tend to already record comparable features and what's genuinely missing is simply more observations. And "rich" data has sufficient volume on both dimensions already; an organization in this position doesn't need external data to improve its own AI, but federated learning still offers a monetization opportunity — contributing that rich data to train other organizations' AI systems while retaining full ownership and never releasing the underlying data itself, the model the Mayo Clinic uses through its Solutions Studio program, which offers global, federated, de-identified data access. A less obvious but genuinely valuable federated-learning use case operates entirely within a single organization: data-sharing barriers between legal entities, jurisdictions, or business units — often erected deliberately for compliance or ethics reasons — can prevent one internal group from accessing data another group within the same company already owns, and federated learning lets the organization use that data across its own internal barriers while remaining fully compliant with whatever restriction created them in the first place. <span class="src">Know Your Data to Harness Federated Machine Learning</span>

Beyond identifying the right partner, the article names practical obstacles that recur regardless of which data type an organization is starting from. Unstructured data (faxes, PDFs, handwritten or scanned reports) needs to be converted into a usable, machine-readable format before it can enter a federated learning pipeline, and business leaders should estimate this structuring cost in collaboration with the technical team that will actually train the resulting AI system. Disparate labeling conventions across collaborating organizations — different terms for the same underlying feature — commonly require the participating organizations' data catalogs to be synchronized before training can proceed, extending expected project timelines. But the most critical obstacle, per the article, is people rather than technology: resistance to collaborating with an external organization, particularly a direct competitor, can stall or derail federated learning exploration entirely, which is why the authors stress that individuals across all collaborating organizations need to actively engage with one another throughout implementation rather than treating federated learning as a purely technical integration project. The article distills six factors business leaders should weigh when deciding which organizations to approach: their own data's current state (poor, horizontal, vertical, or rich); the resulting partner-search logic that state implies (cross-industry for vertical data, same-industry — including competitors — for horizontal data); starting with one specific algorithm and one trusted partner rather than attempting a sweeping multi-partner rollout immediately; the potential to monetize already-rich data by supplying it to others' training processes; anticipated technical challenges around data structuring and label harmonization; and genuine employee buy-in, since federated learning is ultimately a transformational, cross-organizational undertaking that requires widespread support and clearly assigned roles and resources to succeed, not simply a technology deployment. <span class="src">Know Your Data to Harness Federated Machine Learning</span>

## Open Questions

- The article doesn't address what happens when collaborating organizations' data quality or labeling practices are fundamentally incompatible even after harmonization efforts — it treats the technical challenges (unstructured data, disparate labels) as solvable friction rather than potential dealbreakers.
- It's not established how an organization should value its own data contribution when negotiating an ownership-share or revenue-share arrangement with a federated learning partner, beyond the general principle that shares should reflect each party's contribution to the resulting model's accuracy.
