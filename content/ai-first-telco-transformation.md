## Summary

BCG's case for telco CEOs to move from isolated, cost-cutting AI pilots to organization-wide transformation. Covers the productivity gains available in customer care and network operations, the growth opportunities an AI-first redesign unlocks (hyper-personalization, dynamic pricing, network-as-a-service), and the 10-20-70 rule for prioritizing people over technology. A companion BCG value-creators report situates this AI-first agenda within seven broader levers of telco shareholder-value creation. A BCG interview with Singtel’s chief AI officer adds an operator view on turning AI into enterprise value.

## Concept

BCG's case for telco transformation starts from a direct challenge to CEOs: AI has the potential to rewrite a telco's investment case, but only if leadership forces a genuine strategic rethink away from today's isolated, cost-cutting AI pilots and toward organization-wide transformation focused on value creation. The prize for getting this right is framed in blunt financial terms — a 15-percentage-point uplift in EBITDA and a 10-percentage-point uplift in EBIT — a scale of improvement the source argues is overdue in an industry where conventional efficiency programs are yielding diminishing returns and ARPU growth has proven elusive through other means. <span class="src">The AI-First Telco</span>

The productivity case rests on reimagining two core domains well beyond their current AI deployment. Customer care, done properly, goes far beyond deploying chatbots to divert calls away from human agents: AI agents should authenticate customers and detect intent to power an always-on omnichannel digital concierge, calls that genuinely need a human should be supported by real-time scripts and coaching drawn from best-performer models, and the same system should perform root-cause analysis on customer issues and generate insights that get shared across the company rather than discarded after each call closes — together delivering an estimated 45 to 60 percent productivity gain. Network operations follow a parallel logic: rather than deploying AI to fix isolated pain points, a series of reusable assets and agents should operate across the full tech stack, from sensor agents monitoring live telemetry to agents that schedule field engineers and provide on-site diagnostic advice, all coordinated by a higher-level "agent orchestrator" that runs diagnostic root-cause-analysis and preventive-maintenance agents — an approach estimated to deliver 40 to 50 percent productivity gains. Smaller but still meaningful productivity improvements are available in IT/digital platforms (software development, IT operations) and corporate functions like procurement and HR, with the source's overall estimate landing at a 30 to 40 percent productivity gain across the transformed organization. <span class="src">The AI-First Telco</span>

What distinguishes this AI-enabled scenario from telcos' many prior rounds of efficiency-focused transformation is that it comes paired with a genuine growth opportunity, not efficiency gains alone. The source's AI-first scenario projects a 2 to 3 percent revenue increase, driven by hyper-personalized customer value management and dynamic pricing that lifts ARPU, alongside new growth engines like network-as-a-service (NaaS) — letting enterprise customers consume network bandwidth as a flexible, software-defined service rather than a fixed capacity commitment. Together, these growth levers account for roughly 30 percent of the total projected EBITDA improvement, meaningfully changing the investment case's shape relative to a pure cost-cutting program. A further, easily overlooked benefit is reduced revenue leakage: automated revenue management helps ensure that value-added services delivered to enterprise customers are actually captured and billed correctly, an area where manual processes routinely under-bill for services already delivered. <span class="src">The AI-First Telco</span>

Realizing these benefits requires reshaping the organization, not simply deploying AI point solutions on top of an unchanged structure: the value comes specifically from connecting multiple AI initiatives so that assets and agents are reused across the enterprise rather than rebuilt for each use case, with the "agent orchestrator" role coordinating more specialized agents to handle increasingly complex, cross-functional tasks like enterprise-wide root-cause analysis. Only a genuinely strategic approach — not a collection of disconnected pilots — can make this complex "observe-plan-act" cycle actually connect key processes across the business. There is a real cost to this: running AI systems at this scale increases recurring operating costs, and an honest investment case has to account for that. Even so, the source projects the organization retains 70 to 90 percent of the net benefit after accounting for those costs, translating to a projected five-year internal rate of return of 20 to 35 percent — a genuinely attractive return profile if the transformation is executed as a coherent redesign rather than a collection of point deployments. <span class="src">The AI-First Telco</span>

The recommended path to becoming AI-first starts with a readiness self-check: securing C-level and business alignment, committing to a three-year investment horizon rather than expecting quarter-by-quarter payback, and ensuring each major solution has clear profit-and-loss accountability rather than being funded as a diffuse innovation exercise. The core resourcing discipline is captured in a "10-20-70 rule": only 10 percent of transformation effort should go to algorithms, 20 percent to technology, and a full 70 percent to people — a direct, quantified statement of the same "implementation, not the model, is the hard part" lesson found across the broader telecom AI literature. Getting the underlying data ready is treated as a hard prerequisite for consistent agent performance, not a parallel workstream that can lag behind. Organizations should also accept that some capability requires internal development even in an era of abundant off-the-shelf AI tooling, since vendor solutions can't always deliver the specific business context, reusability, and architectural fit a telco's own workflows require. And above all, the source's central directive is "don't deploy — reshape": the strategic, enterprise-wide benefit comes specifically from redesigning end-to-end workflows with AI embedded from the start, which inherently forces cross-functional integration across analytics, operations, and customer engagement as a single orchestrated system, rather than from deploying AI as a bolt-on to processes that remain organized in silos. Finally, the source stresses that timing matters asymmetrically between this transformation's two benefit types: productivity improvements are, in principle, available to any operator willing to execute well, but the revenue-growth opportunities described here will be captured specifically by the first movers in each national market — meaning the cost of delay falls disproportionately on the growth side of the investment case, not just the efficiency side, and telcos able to overcome a persistently pessimistic low-growth, dividend-only investor narrative stand to benefit disproportionately from moving first. <span class="src">The AI-First Telco</span>

<span class="src">A New Formula for Success</span> (BCG, Vaishali Rastogi, February 2024), drawn from the same author's companion Telecommunications Value Creators Report, situates the AI-first agenda within the sector's broader shareholder-value-creation picture and independently corroborates this concept's central resourcing discipline. Analyzing 59 public telcos, the report finds the industry's median five-year annualized total shareholder return improved from 3% to 6% between 2019 and 2023 — but still trailed the S&P 1200's 13% TSR, ranking telcos 31st among 33 tracked sectors, with 85% of the industry's $572 billion net value creation coming from dividend payouts ($485 billion) rather than market-capitalization growth (just $87 billion), and more than 40% of telcos actually ending the period smaller by market cap than they started. Against this backdrop, the report identifies seven "ingredients" successful value creators combine: defending the core (overlaying AI-driven personalization onto existing mobile/fixed/TV products, as T-Mobile US does with its Uncarrier strategy), driving growth outside the core (MTN's Mobile Money financial-services brand grew from 28 million to 64 million users across Africa from 2019 to 2023), maximizing asset leverage (SoftBank and KDDI sharing radio-access-network infrastructure in Japan), radically optimizing costs (BT Group cutting its tariff count by 30% and workforce from 130,000 toward as few as 75,000 by 2030), adopting core-to-cloud transformation (Telstra's T22 program consolidating its business support systems), deploying next-generation network architecture (KDDI's disaggregated, multi-vendor IP infrastructure), and — the ingredient this concept treats in full depth — turning GenAI into impact. On that seventh ingredient specifically, the report restates the identical "10-20-70" resourcing rule this concept's primary source already documents (10% of effort on algorithms, 20% on tech stack and data, 70% on the operational transformation itself) as an independent finding from the same author's parallel research stream, reinforcing rather than merely repeating the "people over technology" thesis. It also supplies three concrete GenAI use cases beyond the customer-care and network-operations programs already detailed here: Deutsche Telekom and SK Telecom, both members of the Global Telco AI Alliance, are partnering directly with OpenAI, Google, and Meta to build a telco-specific large language model for a next-generation digital customer assistant; GenAI-personalized sales email tools are replacing time-consuming, boilerplate outreach with instantly generated, customer-data-driven pitches, freeing sales reps for more time in front of customers; and GenAI coding assistants are generating code snippets and documentation while helping automate test-case generation and bug resolution within telco IT departments. The report's own estimate is that GenAI-enabled customer service alone could eventually reduce service interactions by 50% or more while simultaneously improving customer satisfaction — a scale of impact consistent with, though somewhat more conservative than, this concept's primary source's 45-60% customer-care productivity-gain estimate. <span class="src">A New Formula for Success</span>

<span class="src">Turning AI Into Enterprise Value</span> (BCG alumni interview) provides an operator's complement to BCG's consulting case above. Bhanawat describes a leadership shift from solving problems to building the system that solves them, a focus on a small number of non-negotiable outcomes with clear ownership, and an ambition to make intelligence a reusable capability embedded in daily decisions rather than a set of standalone use cases. He also recommends spending about 10% of time learning what comes next. <span class="src">Turning AI Into Enterprise Value</span>

## Open Questions

- The source's productivity and EBITDA figures are presented as BCG estimates without a fully disclosed underlying methodology; the specific assumptions behind the 70-90% net-benefit-retention range are not detailed in the source excerpt available for this ingest.
- The value-creators report doesn't quantify what share of the 59 telcos studied are actually pursuing the "turn GenAI into impact" ingredient specifically (versus the other six), making it hard to assess how far the sector as a whole has progressed toward the AI-first model this concept's primary source recommends.
- The interview is a single executive's views and contains no Singtel results.
