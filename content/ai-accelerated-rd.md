## Summary

R&D productivity has been falling for decades, with sustaining Moore's Law costing 18 times more than in 1971 and drug approvals per R&D dollar down roughly 80-fold since 1950. McKinsey identifies three channels through which AI can reverse this decline, including faster design generation and surrogate models that replace slow simulations, together worth an estimated $360 to $560 billion in annual value.

## Highlights

- R&D productivity has been declining broadly across industries for decades, not just anecdotally: sustaining Moore's Law required 18 times more inflation-adjusted semiconductor R&D spending in 2014 than in 1971, and pharmaceutical "Eroom's Law" (Moore's Law reversed) found the number of new drugs approved per billion R&D dollars fell roughly 80-fold between 1950 and 2011 before stabilizing somewhat in the past decade.
- AI can accelerate innovation through three distinct channels, each corresponding to a different type of model: increasing the velocity, volume, and variety of design-candidate generation; accelerating candidate evaluation through AI "surrogate" models that predict physical-system behavior far faster than traditional simulation; and accelerating research operations more broadly (literature synthesis, internal knowledge management, documentation, human-AI ideation).
- Generative foundation models can produce design candidates other than text — chemical compounds, drug candidates, computer code, electrical and physical designs — letting organizations generate far more "shots on goal" than human researchers could alone, and often more varied ones, since AI-generated candidates aren't constrained by the same training and experiential biases as human designers (AlphaGo's famous "Move 37," a move no human strategist would have made, exemplifies this).
- AI surrogate ("digital twin") models replace slow, computationally intensive physics-based simulations (computational fluid dynamics, finite element analysis, computational electromagnetics) with neural networks trained to predict the same physical outcomes far faster — DeepMind's weather-forecasting model produces predictions in 8 minutes on a single AI-optimized processor that a supercomputer running tens of thousands of processors needs hours to produce via traditional physics simulation.
- AI's R&D acceleration potential varies sharply by industry: IP-product industries (software, gaming) could see roughly a doubling of R&D throughput since no physical prototyping constrains them; science-based product industries (pharmaceuticals, chemicals) could see 75% to 100%+ throughput acceleration; complex manufactured products requiring multidisciplinary engineering (aerospace, automotive, electronics) see more industry-specific ranges, from 25% (commercial aerospace) to nearly 100% (electronics).
- The total estimated annual economic potential from AI-accelerated R&D is $360-560 billion, concentrated in industries that are both highly R&D-intensive and already reputed for innovation — but this figure is explicitly conservative, excluding the value of AI enabling entirely new higher-value product categories and excluding genuinely breakthrough, market-transforming innovations.
- AI's throughput gains won't translate into organizational value without complementary changes beyond the technology itself — despite widely available generative code-writing tools (Google and Microsoft's CEOs both estimated roughly 30% of new code at their companies is AI-written), the actual software-development acceleration observed at most companies has so far been modest, a gap attributed to insufficient organizational "rewiring" alongside the technology.
- Four organizational levers determine whether companies actually capture AI's R&D acceleration potential: moving quickly and building scaling muscle rather than getting stuck in "pilot purgatory"; rewiring the organization broadly (strategy, talent, agile delivery, change management) rather than treating AI as a bolt-on; building a core competency in evaluating, integrating, and making build-versus-buy decisions about AI models; and being deliberate about where humans stay in the loop, both for safety/accountability reasons and because employee experience of AI assistance ("gives me superpowers" versus "I serve the machine") affects talent retention.

## Concept

<span class="src">The next innovation revolution—powered by AI</span> (McKinsey, Alex Singla, June 2025) opens from a historical observation largely invisible in day-to-day business discussion: R&D productivity has been declining across multiple industries for decades, even as the underlying pace of scientific advance continues. In semiconductors, Stanford economist Nicholas Bloom and collaborators found that sustaining Moore's Law's exponential transistor-density growth required roughly 18 times more inflation-adjusted R&D spending in 2014 than in 1971. In pharmaceuticals, researcher Jack Scannell coined "Eroom's Law" (Moore's Law spelled backward) to describe the inverse trend: the number of new drugs approved per billion R&D dollars spent fell by roughly half every nine years between 1950 and 2011, an approximately 80-fold decline in inflation-adjusted terms, though the decline has stabilized somewhat over the past decade. Bloom's broader company-level analysis across US sectors found declining R&D productivity was the norm (though not universal — 22% of organizations studied actually increased research productivity over the period), a pattern serious enough that sustaining the total-factor-productivity growth that powered roughly two centuries of accelerating human welfare (global GDP per capita grew more than 14-fold since the early 1800s after remaining roughly flat for most of recorded history) has required continuously increasing research investment just to keep pace. AI, in McKinsey's framing, has the potential to bend these declining-productivity curves back upward, not merely to improve efficiency at the margins. <span class="src">The next innovation revolution—powered by AI</span>

The article identifies three distinct channels through which AI can accelerate R&D, each mapped to a different type of underlying model. The first, increasing the velocity, volume, and variety of design-candidate generation, exploits generative foundation models' ability to produce outputs well beyond human language — chemical compounds, drug candidates, computer code, electrical and physical component designs — letting organizations generate far more candidate designs, far faster, than human researchers and engineers could produce unassisted, while also generating designs a human wouldn't have considered given their own training biases and on-the-job experience. A retailer using generative AI to create dozens of photorealistic 3D store configurations (versus the handful of lower-fidelity sketches a human designer would typically produce) illustrates the volume effect directly, and unexpectedly surfaced appealing aesthetic decor choices the AI inserted that fell outside the original design brief. AlphaGo's famous "Move 37" against world Go champion Lee Sedol in 2016 — a move so unconventional several commentators initially assumed it was a mistake, but which proved foundational to AlphaGo's win — exemplifies the same variety effect in a competitive-strategy context; researcher David Baker's team at the University of Washington, sharing the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, used deep-learning models to design entirely novel proteins (functional molecules that don't exist in nature) for applications from new medicines to environmental-pollutant-capturing agents, a feat considered beyond unassisted human design capability. The second channel, accelerating candidate evaluation through AI surrogate models, replaces or supplements slow, computationally intensive physics-based simulation — computational fluid dynamics for aerodynamics, finite element analysis for structural mechanics, computational electromagnetic modeling for antenna design — with neural networks trained to predict the same physical outcomes far faster once trained, functioning as a kind of "digital twin." DeepMind's weather-forecasting neural network predicts weather patterns in 8 minutes on a single AI-optimized processor, versus the hours a top physics-based forecasting system requires running on a supercomputer with tens of thousands of processors; British researchers Demis Hassabis and John Jumper shared the other half of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for AlphaFold, a model that predicts 3D protein structure from amino-acid sequence and has now been applied to roughly 200 million proteins, covering almost every known protein — a problem historically considered intractable given the quantum-level interactions involved. The third channel, accelerating research operations, covers a broader set of LLM-enabled activities: synthesizing customer/user needs from product reviews and support transcripts; exploring and synthesizing the rapidly growing body of published scientific literature and databases (Google, OpenAI, Perplexity, and Anthropic have all introduced "knowledge agent" products that perform multistep research tasks resembling what a human research assistant would do); codifying tacit organizational knowledge (transcribing and structuring recorded meetings, with participant permission); automating heavily regulated industries' documentation requirements (regulatory filings, engineering change orders) subject to human review; and direct human-AI collaborative ideation, where product managers, scientists, and engineers "converse" with LLMs to stimulate and stress-test ideas much as they would with a human colleague. <span class="src">The next innovation revolution—powered by AI</span>

McKinsey's economic analysis estimates AI could substantially accelerate R&D across industries representing 80% of large corporate R&D expenditure, with acceleration potential varying sharply by industry structure. IP-product industries like computer gaming and enterprise software — where no physical prototyping or manufacturing constrains the process — could see roughly a doubling of R&D throughput or more, since every acceleration lever applies directly and gen AI's most mature current capabilities (code generation, visual content creation) map precisely onto what these industries actually produce; even so, the article cautions that real-world acceleration observed at most companies remains modest despite widely available tools (Google's and Microsoft's CEOs have both estimated roughly 30% of new code at their companies is now AI-written), a gap attributed to insufficient complementary organizational change rather than any technical shortfall. Science-based product industries — pharmaceuticals, chemicals, alloys and composites, building materials — sit close to scientific discovery itself and could see the highest acceleration of any category (75% for chemicals R&D, potentially over 100% for pharmaceutical discovery), through applications spanning omics-based target identification, in silico molecule design, surrogate-model-based molecular optimization and property prediction, and literature/database mining, though the overall benefit for pharmaceuticals specifically remains constrained by the clinical trial process (cost, patient recruitment, regulatory capacity) sitting entirely outside AI's reach in this analysis. Complex manufactured products requiring multidisciplinary engineering (aerospace, automotive, electronics, medical technology, semiconductors) see acceleration potential varying from roughly 25% (commercial aerospace) to nearly 100% (electronics), driven substantially by verification and validation — confirming a design was built correctly and that the right system was built — which can account for as much as half of total R&D timeline in these industries and represents one of the largest single opportunities for the shift from physical prototyping toward in silico (traditional-simulation or AI-surrogate) testing, subject to regulatory gating. Consumer goods industries (food and beverage, personal care) see roughly three-quarters of their AI-driven acceleration potential from new-product-candidate generation specifically, using specialized foundation models to generate candidate recipes, cosmetic formulations, and product designs, complemented by AI-surrogate "digital twin" consumer models used in place of (or alongside) live consumer testing. Aggregating across these industries, McKinsey estimates $360-560 billion in total annual economic potential from AI-accelerated R&D, modeled conservatively as the EBIT impact of accelerated new-product development assuming unconstrained demand, no downstream bottlenecks (regulatory approval, production capacity), and current industry-standard margins on new products — explicitly excluding the additional value of AI enabling entirely new, higher-value product categories, lower R&D costs directly, or genuinely market-transforming breakthrough innovations (the article's own example: unlimited clean electricity from nuclear fusion). <span class="src">The next innovation revolution—powered by AI</span>

Realizing this potential, per the article, depends on organizational changes well beyond deploying the underlying AI technology. Moving quickly and building genuine scaling capability matters because speed itself functions as a competitive strategy, but successful pilots provide no guarantee of successful scaling — many companies get stuck in what the article calls "pilot purgatory," running promising proofs of concept that never convert into production-scale capability. Rewiring the organization broadly — aligning strategy, building appropriate talent and organizational models, establishing agile delivery and adoption practices, and building genuine change-management and governance capability — matters because meaningful R&D-throughput differences already exist between global competitors within the same industry even before AI enters the picture, meaning AI-driven rewiring is an opportunity for a genuine step-change in competitive position rather than simply catching up; one concrete structural shift the article recommends is unifying the traditionally separate groups responsible for physical prototyping/testing and for simulation into one team, since deciding what requires physical testing versus what can be handled in silico (via traditional numerical methods or AI surrogates) should be a single coherent decision rather than the product of two separately incentivized functions. Building a core competency around AI models — evaluating, integrating, training/adapting, and making build-versus-buy decisions across open-source, procured, and internally trained models, backed by deepened data sourcing and engineering capability — is identified as a genuinely new organizational muscle most R&D functions don't yet have. And being thoughtful about keeping humans in the loop matters both functionally (ensuring safety and clear accountability for consequential decisions, since fully automated R&D remains a future rather than current scenario across the industries studied) and for talent retention, since how employees experience AI assistance — feeling it "gives them superpowers" versus feeling they "serve the machine" — materially affects an organization's ability to recruit and keep the people needed to execute this transition. <span class="src">The next innovation revolution—powered by AI</span>

## Open Questions

- The article's economic estimates assume unconstrained demand and no downstream bottlenecks beyond the R&D process itself (regulatory approval, production capacity, supply chain), which it acknowledges are real constraints in practice — no source quantifies how much of the estimated $360-560 billion would actually be realized once those constraints are factored back in.
- Neither this source nor others in the vault address how the three acceleration channels interact with the physical AI-infrastructure supply constraints (chip, memory, energy scarcity) documented in [ai investment bubble](#/concept/ai-investment-bubble) — R&D-specific AI compute presumably competes with training and inference compute for the same constrained resources.
