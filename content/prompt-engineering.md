## Summary

Practical guidance on writing effective prompts for large language models, and how prompt-sharing can democratize AI-driven innovation. MIT SMR research on directing agents rather than prompting, and on using coding agents for knowledge work, extends the practice from conversation to configuration.

## Concept

Prompt engineering, in practical (non-research) terms, is the skill of shaping a large language model's output through the wording, context, and structure of the input given to it. One perspective, developed through direct experimentation with GPT-4-class models, argues against treating prompting as a discipline with secret techniques: because LLMs are improving rapidly at inferring intent, heavily engineered prompts full of superlatives ("act as the smartest person ever") tend not to outperform plain, direct requests, and the returns to prompt-crafting are expected to keep shrinking as models improve. <span class="src">A guide to prompting AI (for what it is worth)</span>

What does reliably help is supplying context and constraints. Because LLMs generate text by predicting likely continuations, unconstrained prompts tend to produce generic output that mirrors common patterns in training data. Telling the model what persona or perspective to adopt, what style or tone to use, what to avoid, and what source material to draw from breaks that default pattern and produces more specific, useful results — though assigning a specific named identity (e.g. "act as Bill Gates") does not itself confer that person's expertise. Pasting in reference text, having the model summarize or extend it, or asking it to search for information (in tools with retrieval access) further narrows the space of likely completions. <span class="src">A guide to prompting AI (for what it is worth)</span>

For more demanding tasks, prompts can be structured like a program written in prose: explicit, step-by-step instructions that the model works through in order, which both makes intermediate output checkable and tends to improve accuracy. Chain of Thought prompting — showing the model an example of the reasoning process before making the actual request — is one documented technique in this family. A small set of specific phrases (asking the model to be creative, to show its work or cite sources, to write and explain code, or to produce a draft rather than declining) have been observed to reliably shift output across different LLMs, functioning as near-universal levers even though most other "prompt hacks" do not generalize. Ultimately, skill in this area comes from sustained interactive practice — trying a request, refining it, and iterating — rather than from applying a fixed template. <span class="src">A guide to prompting AI (for what it is worth)</span>

Prompting also functions as a democratizing layer for innovation: because a well-written prompt is, in effect, a program that a non-programmer can write and share, it lowers the barrier to building useful AI-driven tools. A four-level progression describes how this plays out in practice. At Level 1, people use pre-made prompts written and tested by someone else, useful as a starting point even though a prompt built for one context often needs adaptation for another. Level 2 is customizing or authoring a prompt from scratch for one's own needs, which — like any prompt — requires testing and iterative revision rather than working correctly on the first attempt. Level 3 moves to building "blueprints": prompts that generate other prompts, letting an expert's prompt-crafting skill be packaged and reused by people who lack that skill themselves (for example, a prompt that generates a tailored AI tutor for a given topic). Level 4 is simply describing a goal to an autonomous agent (illustrated with an early GPT-4-based coding agent) and letting it plan and execute the steps itself, a mode expected to make the earlier, more manual levels progressively less necessary. Across all four levels, the innovation only compounds if practitioners share what they build — pre-made prompts, customized variants, and blueprints alike — with peers, which is the harder, cooperative half of the pattern. <span class="src">Innovation through prompting</span>

<span class="src">Stop Prompting AI. Start Directing It</span> (MIT SMR, August 2026) treats configuration as the successor skill to prompting. Its worked examples include a metal-parts manufacturer whose four framework agents jointly pointed to narrowing scope to aerospace, a professional-services practice whose agent found that about a third of interview discussions implicitly depended on one leader, a diversified industrial whose ROIC decline traced to a growth-weighted capital allocation formula, and a concrete company whose rejection categories hid a recurring "site not ready" cause. The guidance is to configure for discovery rather than answers, treat unexpected output as signal, evaluate proposals rather than conclusions, and track which proposals are advanced or rejected. <span class="src">Stop Prompting AI. Start Directing It</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses how prompting practices should adapt for models with very large context windows, native tool use, or reasoning modes that reduce the need for explicit step-by-step instruction — both pieces predate those developments becoming standard.
- The directing-intelligence examples are illustrative composites and the article gives no comparative evidence that agent systems find more insight than skilled prompting.
