## Summary

J.B. Wood, Todd Hewlin, and Thomas Lah's account of how cloud/SaaS pricing shifted risk from customer to supplier, making ongoing product consumption — not the initial sale — the primary driver of tech-industry revenue. Covers the Consumption Development discipline (Intelligent Listening, Consumption Innovation, In-Product Upsell) built to close the resulting "Consumption Gap."

## Highlights

- The "Consumption Gap" is the growing distance between the potential value a technology product could provide and the business value a customer actually realizes, because vendors can add features and complexity faster than customers can absorb and use them. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>
- Cloud/SaaS pricing inverts the traditional risk relationship: under "no usage, no money," the supplier bears the financial risk of a sale rather than the customer, since revenue only accrues as the customer actually consumes the product — turning ongoing consumption itself, not the initial purchase, into the thing a vendor must actively drive. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>
- Revenue in this model comes from a high volume of small "micro-transactions" (MTs) — new users, added features, extra capacity — rather than large upfront deals, meaning growth now depends on driving three specific behaviors: getting existing users to adopt more advanced features, getting new users onto an application, and getting existing accounts to adopt entirely new applications. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>
- Consumption Development is the proposed R&D discipline for closing the Consumption Gap, with three components: Intelligent Listening (using instrumented, real-time usage data rather than upfront market-requirements documents to decide what to build), Consumption Innovation (rebalancing development investment away from adding new features and toward simplifying and guiding users along a "Consumption Roadmap" to the value they already paid for), and In-Product Upsell (embedding recommendation engines and offer-management directly into the product to surface the next relevant feature or purchase in real time). <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>
- Feature overshoot has three recurring causes according to Don Norman, cited in the source: customers keep asking for more features across every market segment served, competitors keep innovating, and engineers want visible proof of what they can build — and unused features are explicitly negative, not neutral, because they degrade the interface and slow adoption of the features that actually matter. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>
- Customer support/service, not sales or marketing, is identified as the best-positioned existing organization to own driving consumption at scale, because it already has the lowest per-interaction cost, global call-center infrastructure, deep product knowledge, and ownership of self-service channels — reorganized into a proposed "Account Services Organization" that blends service and inside-sales capability. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>
- The broader shift from selling a product to selling an outcome is sometimes described as a move from B2B to "B4B" (business-for-business): rather than pricing for the transfer of a product or license, a B4B vendor prices for the customer's realized business outcome, which structurally requires the same ongoing, usage-driving vendor engagement this concept's Consumption Development discipline describes. <span class="src">B4B</span>

## Concept

Consumption Economics argues that three roughly simultaneous shifts — the 2008-09 global recession, the rise of cloud computing, and the arrival of the smartphone — permanently changed how technology companies must generate revenue, by shifting financial risk in a sale from the customer to the supplier. Under traditional on-premise licensing, a customer paid upfront and bore the risk that the product might go underused; under cloud/SaaS's "no usage, no money" model, the supplier only earns revenue as the customer actually consumes the product over time, which means driving ongoing usage — not just closing the initial sale — becomes existential to the vendor's own revenue. This dynamic is framed against the "Consumption Gap": technology vendors can add product features and complexity far faster than customers can realistically absorb and productively use them, creating a persistent and growing distance between the potential value a product could deliver and the actual business value a customer realizes from what they've already purchased. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>

Because subscription and usage-based revenue is built from a large volume of small "micro-transactions" (MTs) — an added user, an unlocked feature, extra capacity — rather than a small number of large upfront deals, growth now depends on systematically driving three distinct behaviors: getting existing users to adopt more of a product's advanced capability, getting entirely new users onto an application in the first place, and getting an existing corporate account to adopt additional applications beyond what it already uses. The book's proposed answer to the question "who owns the job of driving consumption?" is that it cannot be any single department's job, since a systematic, company-wide capability is required — but it identifies customer support and service organizations specifically as the best-positioned existing asset to build that capability on top of, because they already carry the lowest per-interaction cost of any customer-facing function, operate global infrastructure, hold deep product knowledge, and already own the self-service channels through which much of this ongoing consumption guidance would need to flow; the book's proposed restructuring, the Account Services Organization, blends this existing service capability with new inside-sales responsibilities specifically focused on recognizing and acting on consumption opportunities. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>

Consumption Development is presented as the R&D-side discipline needed to close the Consumption Gap directly, structured around three components. Intelligent Listening replaces traditional upfront market-requirements documents with real-time usage data gathered from products that are deliberately over-instrumented with "listening posts," on the logic that what customers actually do inside a product is a far more reliable signal of what to build next than what they say they want in a requirements-gathering exercise. Consumption Innovation rebalances development investment away from continuing to add new features (the traditional "speeds and feeds" competitive game) and toward simplifying the product and actively guiding individual users along a "Consumption Roadmap" — a tiered, unfolding presentation of features and complexity, similar in spirit to how games reveal new capability as a player demonstrates readiness for it, so that a user progressively earns access to more advanced functionality rather than being confronted with all of it at once. In-Product Upsell embeds recommendation engines and offer-management logic directly inside the product itself, tastefully surfacing the next relevant feature, piece of content, or purchase opportunity in real time as the user works, modeled explicitly on e-commerce patterns like Amazon's "customers who bought this also bought." The source's design guidance for these in-product offers is a 10:1 rule: for every one revenue-generating add-on offer made to a user, ten purely helpful, no-revenue feature offers should be made first, on the reasoning that consistent, altruistic-feeling guidance builds the end-user trust that makes a later commercial offer far more likely to be accepted, rather than dismissed as sales pressure. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>

A related diagnosis, attributed in the source to Don Norman (author of The Design of Everyday Things), explains why the feature overshoot driving the Consumption Gap keeps recurring even though it is well understood: customers across every market segment a vendor serves keep asking for incremental features specific to their own use case; competitors keep shipping new capabilities that create pressure to match them; and engineers themselves are motivated to build visibly impressive new functionality regardless of whether it serves the majority of users. Critically, the source argues unused features are not simply a wasted but harmless investment — they are actively negative, because they clutter the interface, add cost across the product's entire lifecycle (installation, customization, maintenance, support), and measurably slow adoption of the smaller set of features that actually drive most of a product's realized value. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>

This shift from selling products to selling outcomes is captured elsewhere in the vendor-side literature under the label "B4B" (business-for-business): pricing shifts from the transfer of a product or license to the customer's actual realized business outcome, which — structurally — requires exactly the kind of ongoing, active, usage-driving vendor engagement that Consumption Development is built to deliver, since a vendor being paid for outcomes rather than licenses has a direct financial stake in whether the customer actually consumes and benefits from the product. <span class="src">B4B</span>
