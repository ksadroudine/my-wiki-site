## Summary

A pattern documented across beach-safety, sepsis, welfare-fraud, child-welfare, hiring, and criminal-justice AI risk-prediction tools — vendors oversell automation, and evaluations get inflated by data leakage or context-blind accuracy claims. "Human in the loop" is often used as a liability shield, and a practical two-question test (does it work, and could it cause harm even if it does) helps separate genuine value from "AI snake oil."

## Concept

The argument opens with a concrete case: a Toronto AI tool deployed to predict when public beaches were safe for swimming claimed over 90% accuracy but, once actually checked, performed worse than the simple pre-existing method of daily bacteria testing — leaving beaches open on a majority of days when the water was actually unsafe. The specific failure mode was elementary: city officials never checked the deployed model's performance over the course of the summer it was in use, so the gap between claimed and actual accuracy went undetected until harm had already occurred. The author frames this not as an unusual or surprising failure but as the *default* state of affairs for AI risk prediction tools once deployed, and notes that far subtler failure modes are also possible — a model that's generally accurate but occasionally misses extreme cases, or one that works well across most sites but fails completely at one particular location — failures that are unrealistic to expect non-expert customers to catch on their own. This creates a structural problem: unless the buyer of a risk-prediction tool has genuine internal technical experts, they are effectively purchasing the tool on faith; and if they *do* have such experts, the article notes it's often easier and safer to just build the tool in-house rather than trust an outside vendor's unverifiable claims. <span class="src">The bait and switch behind AI risk prediction tools</span>

A specific, recurring evasion pattern is identified across multiple real-world deployments: a vendor bait-and-switch on the question of human oversight. Tools are marketed and sold on the promise of full automation and job elimination — the commercial case that justifies the purchase in the first place — but once concerns are raised about bias, catastrophic failure, or the tool's well-documented limitations, vendors retreat to fine print stating the tool was never meant to operate fully autonomously, that a human always makes the final call. Crucially, in every documented case, officials and vendors making this defense declined to answer the follow-up question of how often the human decision-maker actually overrides the tool's recommendation — leaving the "human in the loop" claim functioning as an unfalsifiable liability shield rather than a verified safety mechanism. The author calls this pattern "over-automation": tools get used for tasks well beyond their demonstrated capabilities, with the human-oversight defense invoked only reactively, after failure, rather than actively measured and reported as an ongoing safeguard. <span class="src">The bait and switch behind AI risk prediction tools</span>

Three further cases, spanning healthcare, welfare, and child protection, are used to show this is a systemic pattern rather than an isolated incident. Epic's sepsis prediction model, deployed across hundreds of U.S. hospitals after its 2017 release, was later found by University of Michigan researchers to vastly underperform its developer's claims — the root cause was data leakage: one of the model's inputs was whether a patient had already been given antibiotics, but a patient given antibiotics for sepsis had, in effect, typically already been diagnosed with sepsis by a clinician, so the model's own evaluation was silently counting circular reasoning as predictive success. Once independently measured in real deployment, 88% of the tool's alerts turned out to be false alarms, adding to clinical workload rather than reducing it; Epic stopped selling the one-size-fits-all version of the model roughly a year after the critical study, after initially defending it by asserting (in near-direct contradiction of the actual data) that its surrounding clinical workflow was what "gave the tool purpose." The Netherlands' childcare-benefits fraud detection algorithm, deployed from 2013, used statistical correlations — including whether a person held dual nationality, disproportionately implicating Turkish, Moroccan, and Eastern European parents — to make outright accusations of fraud without any additional corroborating evidence, wrongly accusing roughly 30,000 parents and demanding repayments of hundreds of thousands of euros in many individual cases; the case removed the human-review step that had existed before the algorithm's deployment, ran unchecked for over six years, and ultimately triggered a EUR 3.7 million government fine and the resignation of the entire Dutch cabinet once the scandal became public. The Allegheny Family Screening Tool (AFST), in use in Allegheny County, Pennsylvania since 2016 to decide which families get investigated for possible child neglect — investigations that can result in children being forcibly removed from a home even absent any abuse allegation, purely on poverty-related neglect grounds — was found two years after deployment to suffer from the same data-leakage problem that inflated its claimed performance, and was separately found to be systematically biased against Black families; when challenged, its creators offered the same "a human always makes the final decision" defense documented in the other cases, again without disclosing override rates. <span class="src">The bait and switch behind AI risk prediction tools</span>

A further obstacle compounding all of this is that when outside researchers or regulators ask to examine these models directly, companies frequently invoke "trade secret" protections to decline — a response documented for Epic, for Northpointe (maker of the COMPAS recidivism-prediction tool used in criminal sentencing), and for other vendors in this space, closing off exactly the kind of independent scrutiny that surfaced the data-leakage and bias problems in the cases above. The article's proposed remedy reverses the default assumption governing these tools: rather than treating an AI risk-prediction tool as trustworthy until proven otherwise, the burden should fall on the vendor selling it to proactively justify its validity with evidence before deployment — and absent such evidence, any risk-assessment tool, including (in the author's assessment) most currently on the market, should be treated as suspect by default rather than given the benefit of the doubt. <span class="src">The bait and switch behind AI risk prediction tools</span>

<span class="src">How to spot real value in AI — and avoid the snake oil</span> (MIT SMR, June 2025) is, in effect, the direct companion piece to this concept's original source: it reports on an MIT talk by Princeton's Arvind Narayanan, co-author with this concept's original source's author, Sayash Kapoor, of the book "AI Snake Oil: What Artificial Intelligence Can Do, What It Can't, and How to Tell the Difference." Narayanan's framing sharpens the underlying thesis into two failure categories: AI tools that "simply don't work" despite their marketing claims, and tools that work exactly as advertised but get used in the wrong context or for harmful ends — with both producing outcomes that are inaccurate, biased, or dangerous once deployed at scale. He supplies additional concrete examples of the first category well beyond the four risk-prediction tools already documented: video-based personality-scoring software for job candidates that proved to be "an elaborate random-number generator," changing scores radically when a background bookshelf was added or a candidate's glasses removed with no change to the underlying interview content; and pretrial-detention risk algorithms across the U.S. criminal justice system with predictive accuracy below 70 percent, "only slightly more accurate than the flip of a coin," despite determining whether someone is held in jail before trial. The article also draws a contrast this concept's original source doesn't make explicit: generative AI, in Narayanan's assessment, is not snake oil in the same way — it's "useful to basically every knowledge worker" — though it carries its own distinct risks (hallucinations that are inherent to how the technology works, not easily patched; AI-generated misinformation like inaccurate mushroom-foraging guides; nonconsensual deepfake generation; and AI agents attempting real-world tasks like autonomous web navigation that remain, in his words, "pretty much dead on arrival" in terms of reliability). Narayanan's practical contribution is a two-question test for evaluating any AI tool before deployment: does it actually work as claimed, and — a distinct, second question — even if it does work, could it still cause harm? Facial recognition technology illustrates why the second question can't be skipped even when the first is answered "yes": it has reached genuinely high technical accuracy, and that accuracy is itself part of what makes it harmful, since it enables effective mass surveillance when deployed without adequate guardrails. AI-based academic cheating detectors, which systematically misflag non-native English speakers, are offered as a tool failing the first question outright — Narayanan calls them straightforward "snake oil." The article's closing frame — treat AI as infrastructure to be evaluated soberly rather than magic to be marveled at, since "much of what we call AI today will fade into the background" as it matures — reinforces this concept's central call for skepticism as the default posture, while extending it explicitly to generative AI rather than leaving the original source's risk-prediction-tool focus as the only domain covered. <span class="src">How to spot real value in AI — and avoid the snake oil</span>
