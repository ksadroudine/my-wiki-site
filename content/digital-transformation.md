## Summary

Six legacy companies' HBR-documented digital transformations show technology is never the actual bottleneck — culture, process, and continuous adaptation are. A four-year Harvard Business School study of 8,300+ leaders finds the ones making real progress build a "digitally dexterous workforce" through four practices — reframing, engaging from the top, bridging people, and sustaining long-term commitment — rather than simply deploying new tools. The foundational "Digital Masters" research supplies the original two-capability framework (digital plus leadership) and five business-model-reinvention archetypes this later work builds on, and Pfizer's manufacturing-digitization case study shows the same technology-is-not-the-bottleneck lesson playing out over two decades in a single company.

## Highlights

- Six legacy companies' first-person accounts converge on the same lesson from different angles: Moody's went all-in on generative AI without a precise end goal, prioritizing 14,000-employee buy-in and a "yes, and" mindset over a fixed roadmap; Honeywell first radically simplified its own sprawl (150+ ERPs down to 10, 2,700 applications down to fewer than 1,000) before it could build anything new on top; and IKEA's then-CDO described digital transformation as an iceberg, where the customer-facing changes visible above the surface are dwarfed by the business-model and operating-model changes happening beneath it.
- Levi's found culture, not technology, was the harder constraint: its "perfectionist" mindset (six months to plan, six more to perfect before launch) had to give way to an agile, digital-first mindset, yielding four transferable lessons — failure is acceptable during transition, focus on leapfrogging rather than closing a gap, treat transformation as a data-collection opportunity, and allow real time for tech savviness to grow.
- A four-year Harvard Business School study — longitudinal case studies since 2017, roundtables with 240+ leaders since 2022, and annual cross-sectional surveys of 8,300+ leaders across 109 countries — found leaders making more progress on digital transformation define the goal not as deploying tools but as building a "digitally dexterous workforce," one both willing and able to use new technologies like generative AI.
- Five organizational characteristics recur every year since 2021 as what leaders identify as most important for digital agility: data-informed (not data-driven) decision-making, cross-functional collaboration, customer focus, continuous learning, and comfort with change — language matters here specifically, since employees resist when data is framed as replacing their judgment ("data-driven") rather than complementing it ("data-informed").
- Organizations where the CEO or C-suite personally led the transformation (35% of surveyed companies) reported more progress; 80% of leaders reporting greater progress described their C-suite as "very" or "extremely" tech-savvy, versus only 36% of leaders reporting less progress — generative AI specifically has made "data savviness" a top-three leadership quality (after adaptability and curiosity) in a way it wasn't in earlier survey years.
- "Bridgers" — people who build partnerships across organizational boundaries by translating between differing priorities, constraints, and working styles — are the mechanism through which leaders reconcile employee anxiety about job security with employees' genuine excitement about new tools; one airline hired a dedicated bridger team to connect every division with external research institutions, universities, startups, and VC firms.
- Digital dexterity takes far longer than leaders initially expect: one executive planned three years and needed almost eight, six of which went into building a single unified data source of truth before workforce capability could even be addressed — and the clearest sign of success is when digital stops being a named initiative and simply becomes assumed as part of how the business operates.
- Between 2024 and 2025, the same annual survey recorded a sharp swing in leader sentiment about generative AI's net effect on jobs: 80% called it complementary in 2024, versus only 53% in 2025 (43% now say substitutive), even as the same leaders continued investing in digital-dexterity programs.
- The foundational "Digital Masters" research (a global survey of 431 executives at 391 companies) found companies excelling at both digital capability and leadership capability were 26% more profitable than their average industry competitor and generated 9% more revenue from existing physical capacity — with the correlation, not proven causality, still yielding the same practical management advice either way.
- Digital Masters transform customer experience through four related interventions: understanding customer behavior and designing experience from the outside in; investing in new digital channels to increase reach and engagement; putting customer data at the center of the whole experience; and seamlessly blending physical and digital experience rather than replacing one with the other.
- 82% of surveyed Digital Masters agreed their senior leaders shared a common vision of digital transformation (71% said the vision was shared between senior and middle managers), versus under 30% and 17% respectively among non-Digital-Master firms — and two-thirds of Digital Masters described their vision as genuinely radical, versus 31% of respondents overall.
- Five archetypes of digital business-model reinvention recur across the companies studied: reinventing industries (multisided platforms like Hailo and Airbnb aggregating fragmented markets and building network-effect barriers to entry), substituting products or services (proactively cannibalizing your own revenue before a digital substitute displaces it involuntarily), creating new digital businesses (Nike layering new digital revenue streams onto its existing products), reconfiguring value delivery models (Volvo's "B2B2C" shift providing services directly to end customers without disintermediating its dealers), and rethinking value propositions (recombining existing products, services, and data for existing or new customers) — yet despite broad executive enthusiasm, only 7% of surveyed companies said their digital initiatives were actually launching new businesses.
- Strategic assets worth building a digital vision around should be assessed against the VRIN test (valuable, rare, inimitable, nonsubstitutable) before committing resources, and digital ambitions themselves fall into three tiers of increasing difficulty and payoff: substitution (a new-technology alternative to an existing function), extension, and transformation (fundamentally redefining a process or product through technology rather than merely accelerating it).
- Pfizer spent nearly two decades failing to digitize its paper-based pharmaceutical manufacturing batch records before breaking through in 2025, and the obstacle was never the electronic batch record (EBR) technology itself but organizational: unclear governance between central digital teams and manufacturing sites, a digital team that lacked manufacturing credibility, and a one-size-fits-all deployment model that treated plants as recipients rather than co-designers of the solution.
- Reframing the effort from "implementing MES" to "enabling MES capabilities" was Pfizer's single biggest turning point — the same reframing-as-workforce-problem-not-technology-problem move this concept's other sources make — paired with strategic site selection (targeting 80% of revenue at 50% of full-transformation cost) and up to three months of upfront diagnosis per site before any technical work began.
- After the reframe, Pfizer's go-live schedule accuracy rose from 60% to over 80%, yearly EBR deployment increased more than 50%, and the cost of implementing an EBR at a single site fell 80% — the foundation (trust, governance, repeatable process) that later enabled "golden batch" optimization and AI-driven process control, capabilities that could not have been layered onto the earlier paper-based or disconnected-"islands of digital" state at any cost.

## Concept

<span class="src">How 6 Companies Approached Digital Transformation</span> (HBR, June 2025) synthesizes first-person accounts from six legacy companies — Moody's, Honeywell, IKEA, Levi's, Adobe, and Intuit — to answer what digital transformation actually requires beyond adopting new tools. The recurring theme across all six is that technology adoption is necessary but never sufficient: transformation requires rethinking how a company creates value, its strategy, and the processes by which employees do their work, plus genuine cultural change. Moody's leadership, facing generative AI in late 2022 with "no proven use cases," decided inaction was riskier than uncertainty and committed to three guidelines — universal employee involvement (a 14,000-person buy-in effort), a "yes, and" posture that pushed legal, compliance, and risk staff to engage with new ideas rather than reflexively reject them, and an unwavering focus on measurable business value despite lacking a precise end goal, described by the CEO as "sprinting into the fog." Honeywell's transformation began by confronting sheer organizational sprawl — more than 150 ERP systems, 2,700 applications, and 1,700 websites with no overarching data plan — through a three-step sequence: radically simplify infrastructure first (150+ ERPs to 10, 2,700 applications to under 1,000, 1,700 websites to under 100), then build master databases for products, employees, and customers, and only then develop a strategy for putting that consolidated data to work; the resulting foundation let Honeywell add an entirely new cloud-based software-as-a-service data layer on top of its existing hardware business. IKEA's transformation, accelerated by pandemic store closures, required redesigning supply chains, inventory, logistics, and fulfillment alongside a more subtle reconception of customer experience that dissolved the line between digital and physical retail — its then-chief digital officer described the visible customer-facing changes as merely the tip of an iceberg sitting atop much larger, invisible shifts to the underlying business and operating model, and stressed that earning the internal buy-in to make those deeper changes required first being accepted into the company's existing culture rather than imposing change from outside it. <span class="src">How 6 Companies Approached Digital Transformation</span>

Levi's illustrates a case where cultural change was the harder problem, not technology: the company's ingrained "perfectionist" mindset (six months of planning, six more months of iteration before a "perfect" launch) had to give way to an agile, digital-first way of working before any specific tool could help. Levi's distilled four transferable lessons from the process: accepting that failure is a normal part of major transitions rather than something to avoid at all costs; reframing the goal from catching up to leapfrogging competitors entirely; treating the transformation itself as an opportunity to build organization-wide data literacy rather than just a technical migration; and allowing genuine time for tech savviness to develop rather than expecting it instantly. Adobe's contribution addresses a different dimension: managing the risk introduced alongside new AI tools requires evaluating AI's impact contextually (specific circumstances, environments, and real-world applications) rather than assessing it as a single undifferentiated technology by its inputs and outputs alone, operationalized through four principles — human involvement remains critical, hype does not equal quality, AI's continuous evolution can still be anticipated rather than merely reacted to, and a robust ethics framework is required rather than optional. Intuit, a digital-native company not automatically immune to transformation pressure, faces a distinct challenge: customer expectations reset continuously, since "today's breakthrough is tomorrow's baseline," a cycle AI has accelerated further — Intuit's response has been to treat transformation as a genuinely continuous process via three embedded principles: regular, rigorous, ego-free technical progress checks; embedding AI experts directly across teams rather than centralizing them; and building clear escalation processes that turn organizational conflict into competitive advantage rather than paralysis. <span class="src">How 6 Companies Approached Digital Transformation</span>

<span class="src">Why Digital Dexterity Is Key to Transformation</span> (MIT SMR, Linda A. Hill et al., February 2026) supplies the empirical research base and organizing framework the six company accounts above illustrate individually. Drawing on Harvard Business School's Leadership Initiative — longitudinal case studies since 2017, global roundtables with more than 240 leaders and digital natives since 2022, and annual cross-sectional surveys now covering more than 8,300 leaders across 109 countries and 11 GICS sectors — the researchers found that leaders reporting the most progress on digital transformation have moved well past the field's original definition (evolving processes, systems, and talent to exploit digital technologies) toward a more ambitious goal: building a "digitally dexterous workforce," one both willing and able to use new technologies like generative AI to deliver innovative solutions. Four practices consistently distinguish these leaders. Reframing the challenge means recognizing transformation as fundamentally a workforce and culture problem rather than a technology problem, centered on building five organizational characteristics that have topped the survey's rankings every year since 2021: data-informed decision-making, cross-functional collaboration, customer focus, continuous learning, and comfort with change — with the specific finding that framing decisions as "data-driven" (implying data replaces human judgment) triggers more employee resistance than framing them as "data-informed" (implying data complements expertise). Engaging from the top means the CEO or C-suite personally and visibly driving the effort rather than delegating it: companies where senior leadership led the transformation directly (35% of the 2025 sample) reported more progress, and "data savviness" has newly entered the top three leadership qualities associated with progress (after adaptability at 75% and curiosity at 52%), a shift the researchers attribute specifically to generative AI's arrival — importantly, this savviness means understanding "digital context, language, trends, and opportunities" well enough to model the same curiosity and tool experimentation leaders ask of their workforce, not deep technical expertise. Bridging people and perspectives means actively working across organizational boundaries — translating between differing priorities, constraints, and working styles — to build the psychological safety needed for open dialogue about change-related anxiety; one airline executive hired a dedicated team of "bridgers" specifically to connect every division of the company with external research institutions, universities, startups, and venture capital firms, introducing design-thinking workshops along the way. Sustaining long-term commitment means recognizing that culture change reliably takes far longer than initial estimates: one executive planned three years for a transformation that ultimately took almost eight, six of which were consumed simply building a single, unified "source of truth" data marketplace before workforce-capability work could meaningfully begin — the clearest sign of a transformation's success, per the research, is when "digital" and "AI" stop appearing as named initiatives in corporate strategy documents at all, because the capability has become fully assumed rather than aspirational. A striking one-year shift in the same survey data — the share of leaders calling generative AI's job effects "complementary" fell from 80% in 2024 to just 53% in 2025, with "substitutive" responses rising from 20% to 43% — shows sentiment about AI's ultimate impact on the workforce moving even as digital-dexterity investment continues. <span class="src">Why Digital Dexterity Is Key to Transformation</span>

<span class="src">Leading Digital</span> (George Westerman, Didier Bonnet, and Andrew McAfee) is the foundational research this concept's later sources build directly on, and the origin of the term this concept's whole subject matter takes its name from. Based on a global survey of 431 executives across 391 companies, the authors found that firms excelling at both of two distinct capabilities — genuine digital capability (rethinking business processes, customer engagement, and business models around technology) and genuine leadership capability (setting a clear vision for transformation and driving execution through it) — constitute a class the authors call "Digital Masters," and that these companies are 26% more profitable than their average industry competitor while generating 9% more revenue from their existing physical capacity. The book is explicit that this is a correlation rather than a proven causal finding, but notes the practical management guidance is identical either way. Digital Masters transform customer experience through four related interventions: designing the experience from the outside in by first understanding actual customer behavior; investing deliberately in new digital channels to expand reach and engagement; centering customer data at the heart of the experience rather than treating it as a byproduct; and blending physical and digital experience seamlessly, using digital technology to enhance rather than simply replace existing valuable assets — a distinction the book illustrates with Codelco's driverless mining trucks, which let the company design fundamentally different (cheaper, faster) mine layouts once human safety constraints no longer applied to certain areas. On vision specifically, the book's survey data anticipates this concept's later engage-from-the-top finding by roughly a decade: 82% of Digital Masters reported their senior leaders shared a common transformation vision (71% extending to middle management), against fewer than 30% and 17% respectively among other firms, and two-thirds of Digital Masters described their vision as genuinely radical rather than incremental, compared with just 31% of the full survey sample. Crafting that vision, per the book, requires identifying strategic assets that pass the VRIN test (valuable, rare, inimitable, nonsubstitutable) before committing to build around them, and calibrating ambition across three tiers — substitution (using new technology as a straightforward replacement for an existing function), extension, and transformation (fundamentally redefining a process or product rather than merely accelerating the old one). On business models specifically, the book identifies five archetypes of digital reinvention that still map cleanly onto contemporary examples: reinventing industries through multisided platforms that aggregate fragmented markets and build network-effect barriers to entry (Hailo for taxis, Airbnb for rooms, eBay's classic buyer-seller flywheel); substituting products or services proactively, since a company whose core offering is being displaced by a digital alternative must choose to cannibalize its own revenue on its own terms rather than have the choice made for it; creating new digital businesses layered onto an existing core (Nike building new digital revenue streams around its athletic products); reconfiguring value delivery models without disintermediating existing partners (Volvo's shift to a "B2B2C" model providing services directly to end customers in ways explicitly designed to strengthen, not bypass, its dealer network); and rethinking value propositions by recombining existing products, services, and data for existing or new customer segments. Despite broad executive enthusiasm for the concept, the book's own survey found the archetype rarely converts into practice at the time of the research: only 7% of respondents said their company's digital initiatives were actually helping launch new businesses, and just 15% said new business models were emerging from digital technology at all — a sobering baseline against which this concept's later sources (Moody's committing to generative AI without a fixed roadmap, Honeywell's multi-year infrastructure consolidation) can be read as documenting what it actually took, a decade later, to close that gap. <span class="src">Leading Digital</span>

<span class="src">Transforming Manufacturing at Pfizer The Hard Part Was Not the Technology</span> (MIT SMR, George Westerman and David Kiron, June 2026) supplies a single-company, two-decade natural experiment in exactly the thesis the other three sources argue in general terms: that technology is rarely the actual bottleneck. Pfizer's goal was unglamorous but consequential — replacing paper-based pharmaceutical manufacturing batch records (thousands of handwritten pages per production run, required for regulatory compliance) with an electronic Manufacturing Execution System (MES) capable of real-time analytics — yet for nearly 20 years the company, like the rest of its industry, produced only "paper on glass": electronic forms that replicated the old paper process on a screen while shop-floor equipment remained disconnected and data stayed siloed. The obstacles were entirely organizational rather than technical: Pfizer's more than 30 manufacturing sites, the product of decades of mergers, ran on incompatible automation layers from different vendors that "don't talk to each other"; regulatory approval requirements made a one-size-fits-all standardized rollout effectively impossible, since modifying an approved production line could trigger costly refilings; and the original transformation approach positioned manufacturing plants as passive recipients of centrally designed technology, with governance so unclear between central digital teams and site operations that a flagship site ultimately revolted and demanded the program restart with a different vendor, while overall schedule attainment sat at just 60%. The turnaround came from a leadership reframe strikingly similar to this concept's other sources' central finding: the team shifted its own mission from "implementing MES" to "enabling MES capabilities," repositioning itself as a partner helping sites achieve their own operational goals rather than an outside authority imposing a fixed solution. This was paired with concrete process changes — strategic site selection based on a value-to-complexity ratio (targeting sites covering 80% of revenue at 50% of full-transformation cost, rather than tackling every site's most complex production line first), up to three months of rigorous upfront diagnosis per site before any technical work began, cross-functional teams that combined manufacturing-operations experts with IT specialists rather than parachuting in technologists alone, and reusable templates (including an "MES-lite" option for simpler sites) that let each subsequent deployment go faster than the last. The results validate the reframe: go-live schedule accuracy rose from 60% to over 80%, yearly EBR deployment increased more than 50%, and per-site implementation cost fell 80% — with the resulting real-time data infrastructure becoming, only after the fact, the foundation for "golden batch" optimization and AI-driven process control that "could not have been layered onto paper records or disconnected islands of digital" at any price. As one Pfizer digital leader put it, quoting former IBM CEO Lou Gerstner: "the soft stuff is the hard stuff" — a direct, single-company echo of this concept's central cross-cutting lesson that culture, trust, and process design determine whether technology investment pays off at all. <span class="src">Transforming Manufacturing at Pfizer The Hard Part Was Not the Technology</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses how digital transformation leadership should differ for smaller or resource-constrained companies that lack the scale to run the kind of multi-year, multi-hundred-person research and rollout efforts described (Honeywell's ERP consolidation, the health-care system's BI/IT/organizational-effectiveness collaboration, the eight-year single executive's data marketplace build).
- The 2024-to-2025 sentiment swing on AI's complementary-versus-substitutive job effect is reported without explanation — neither source addresses whether this reflects genuinely worsening outcomes, more realistic leader expectations as deployments matured, or a change in which leaders were surveyed.
- The foundational Digital Masters research is roughly a decade old and predates generative AI entirely; none of the sources in this concept directly reconcile its five business-model archetypes or three-tier ambition framework against the AI-specific transformation patterns (Moody's "sprinting into the fog," Intuit's continuous-transformation principles) the later sources describe — it's left to the reader to judge how much the earlier framework still applies unchanged.
- Pfizer's transformation is described as "still ongoing" at time of writing, so the case study does not report whether the promised AI-driven process-control and predictive-manufacturing capabilities the new MES foundation was meant to enable have themselves materialized yet.
