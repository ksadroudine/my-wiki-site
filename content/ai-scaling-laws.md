## Summary

What scaling laws actually predict about larger AI models, and why several popular assumptions about scaling toward AGI are misconceptions. Also covers how the industry's scaling strategy has shifted toward smaller, longer-trained models with higher-quality data.

## Highlights

- Scaling laws are a well-established, highly predictable empirical relationship between model size, training compute, dataset size, and perplexity (next-word prediction accuracy) — but perplexity is largely irrelevant to end users, and "emergent abilities," the capabilities users actually care about, are not governed by any comparably reliable law. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>
- There is no empirical basis for assuming emergent capability gains will continue indefinitely as models scale; on benchmarks designed to test extrapolation to genuinely unseen tasks, LLMs tend to perform poorly, suggesting they may be closer to interpolating within their training distribution than generalizing beyond it. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>
- High-quality training data is a binding near-term constraint: even transcribing all of YouTube would add meaningfully less usable token volume than Llama 3's existing ~15 trillion-token training set, once non-speech content and quality filtering are accounted for, and acquiring further data is getting more expensive due to copyright and regulatory pushback. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>
- Exponential technology trends can plateau abruptly rather than gradually, as happened with CPU clock speeds in the 2000s and civil aircraft speeds after the 1970s; the trendline itself gives no advance warning of a plateau. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>
- Synthetic data is not a general substitute for more human training data — it is mainly useful for targeted gap-filling (math, code, low-resource languages, alignment) rather than for increasing overall training data volume, with self-play successes like AlphaGo being a special case limited to fully self-contained, simulatable environments. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>
- Frontier labs have shifted toward smaller, cheaper, more capable models trained for longer on higher-quality data rather than simply scaling up parameter count: GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Gemini 1.5 Pro are all cheaper (and inferred to be smaller) than their largest predecessors, while being more capable. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>
- Smaller models require proportionally more training compute to reach a given capability level, so the downward pressure on model size is producing upward pressure on training compute as labs trade training cost against inference cost. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>

## Concept

Scaling laws describe a highly predictable relationship: as model size, training compute, and dataset size increase together, language models reliably get "better" in the narrow sense of achieving lower perplexity — improved accuracy at predicting the next word. This predictability is the main basis for the popular expectation that scaling will continue for many more orders of magnitude and eventually produce artificial general intelligence (AGI). That expectation rests on a misreading of what scaling laws actually show, however: perplexity is largely irrelevant to end users, who care instead about "emergent abilities" — new capabilities that appear as scale increases. Emergence has in fact accompanied scaling so far, but it is not governed by any law-like regularity comparable to the perplexity scaling laws, so past emergence provides no guarantee that further scaling will keep producing new capabilities. On benchmarks specifically designed to test whether models can extrapolate to genuinely unseen task types, LLMs tend to perform poorly, which is evidence — though not proof — that their gains come more from interpolating within patterns already present in training data than from a general-purpose capacity to extrapolate beyond it. If that is broadly true, capability gains from further scaling should be expected to plateau once the space of tasks represented in available training data is effectively exhausted. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>

Data availability is a second, more immediate constraint. AI developers are already drawing on essentially all readily available high-quality text sources, and commonly proposed new sources are smaller than they first appear: transcribing all of YouTube's roughly 150 billion minutes of video would, after excluding music and non-speech content and applying deduplication and quality filtering, likely add substantially less usable data than Llama 3's existing training set of about 15 trillion tokens. Acquiring further data is also becoming more expensive, both because copyright holders increasingly expect compensation and because of reputational and regulatory pushback against large-scale data collection. Historical precedent — CPU clock speeds through the 2000s, and civil aircraft cruising speeds since the 1970s — shows that exponential technology trends can plateau suddenly rather than gradually, and that a trendline in isolation gives no warning that a plateau is imminent; the same could be true of LLM scaling, and it is not possible to know in advance how many further orders of magnitude of useful scaling remain. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>

Synthetic data is often proposed as a way around the data ceiling, but the available evidence suggests it functions mainly as a tool for closing specific capability gaps — math, code, low-resource languages, and alignment — rather than as a way to increase overall training data volume. The clearest synthetic-data success stories, such as AlphaGo's self-play training, depend on a fully self-contained, simulatable environment where the model can generate arbitrarily much high-quality training signal by playing against itself; this "System 2 → System 1 distillation" pattern is expected to generalize to other bounded domains like code generation, but not to open-ended tasks such as language translation, where no equivalent self-play loop exists. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>

Consistent with the possibility that scaling gains are slowing, industry behavior has shifted from maximizing model size toward maximizing capability per dollar. Historically, dataset size, model size, and training compute scaled together as the jointly optimal strategy; but with high-quality data increasingly a bottleneck, developers have instead pushed toward smaller models trained for longer. API pricing is used as a rough size proxy in the absence of published parameter counts: GPT-4o costs about 25% of GPT-4 while matching or exceeding its capability; Claude 3.5 Sonnet is roughly 5x cheaper than Claude 3 Opus while being more capable; Gemini 1.5 Pro is both cheaper and more capable than Gemini 1.0 Ultra. In each family, the largest model is no longer the most capable one. Because smaller models require proportionally more training compute to reach a given performance level, this shift trades training cost for reduced inference cost — a deliberate rebalancing, since inference cost accumulated over a model's operational lifetime is thought to dominate its total cost, and earlier models such as GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 are considered to have been comparatively under-trained on this metric. Llama 3's 8-billion-parameter model, for example, used roughly 20x the training compute of the original, similarly-sized Llama model. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>

Framed as a "ladder of generality" — a long-running trend of computers requiring less effort to be directed at a new task, running from special-purpose machines through Turing machines to today's instruction-tuned LLMs — the current step is unlikely to be the last, but the AI research community has historically been poor at predicting how many further steps exist, what triggers the next one, or when it will arrive; that difficulty in prediction is itself expected to persist regardless of how the scaling debate resolves. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>
