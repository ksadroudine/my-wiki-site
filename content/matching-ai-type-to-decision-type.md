## Summary

Generative AI and analytical AI (prediction, optimization) serve fundamentally different roles and should be matched to the decision or project at hand — analytical AI as the decision engine for narrow, measurable decisions, generative AI as an accelerator for analytics work, project ideation, and synthesis in wide, ambiguous decisions. A complementary technical framework extends the same logic to implementation choices — whether a specific task is a prediction or generation problem, and whether its data is tabular or unstructured, determines the pragmatic choice among machine learning, deep learning, and generative AI. A further technical addition, causal machine learning, supplies the missing category for genuine "what-if" counterfactual decisions that plain prediction cannot answer. Treating all AI as interchangeable is why most organizations struggle to convert AI adoption into measurable business impact.

## Highlights

- McKinsey's 2025 State of AI report found 88% of companies use AI in at least one function, but only around 40% see a positive bottom-line impact — a gap attributed to organizations applying generic AI enthusiasm to decisions without matching the specific AI capability to the decision's actual characteristics.
- Decisions can be diagnosed as narrow (clear objectives, usable data, fast feedback loops — e.g., where to open the next store, churn prediction) or wide (contested goals, incomplete information, political stakes — e.g., a brand repositioning, an organizational redesign) using a six-question scorecard: objective clarity, data readiness, causal stability, boundary transparency, feedback-loop speed, and reversibility.
- Wide, strategic decisions routinely contain narrow subdecisions that can still be modeled and optimized — a brand pivot (wide) may include message testing, media-mix optimization, and demand forecasting (all narrow) — so the diagnostic should be applied at the subdecision level, not just to the decision as a whole.
- For narrow decisions, generative AI's most reliable role is as an accelerator around an analytical core rather than a replacement for it: labeling unstructured data into structured inputs a predictive model can use, and translating technical model outputs (like SHAP variable-importance results) into plain-language explanations business stakeholders can act on.
- LLMs can also accelerate the development of prescriptive (optimization) models by conversationally interviewing a decision maker to identify decision variables, objective functions, and constraints, producing a first-draft mathematical formulation that a modeling expert still must review — and by explaining infeasibility or trade-off results (like shadow prices) back to non-technical stakeholders in plain language.
- A European retail bank's churn-prevention project illustrates the narrow-decision pattern working correctly: a predictive model drove the actual targeting decision, while generative AI translated unstructured complaint-center transcripts into structured model inputs and drafted context-aware outreach scripts — analytics led, generative AI supported.
- For wide decisions, generative and agentic AI's role shifts to synthesis and legibility: surfacing assumptions, framing scenarios, and articulating trade-offs to help people reason together, illustrated by a multinational insurer using generative tools to synthesize internal strategic history alongside external organizational-design research ahead of a product-to-customer-segment restructuring.
- The central risk in wide decisions is mistaking AI's fluency for understanding or judgment — a system can produce a persuasive, well-formatted narrative while missing context or embedding unexamined assumptions, so AI should be treated as an amplifier of human perspective rather than an authority that can substitute for organizational commitment and alignment.
- Even before deciding how to use AI on a specific decision, organizations must first identify which AI projects to pursue at all — a task complicated by generative AI's high profile overshadowing analytical AI, even though McKinsey research suggests generative AI use cases add only 15% to 40% on top of the economic benefits already available from analytical AI use cases.
- A customized generative AI "companion tool," fed a company's strategic reports, financial records, and data inventory, can improve AI-project ideation workshops across six steps split into a divergent stage (idea generation, idea structuring, data exploration) and a convergent stage (identifying AI solutions, pitch design, and impact quantification) — though the tool should not be trusted to do the actual financial quantification math itself.
- A holding company spanning telecommunications, retail, banking, and real estate used a generative AI companion tool across its ideation workshops and identified roughly 100 possible AI projects — twice what the company expected based on the number of participants — with the tool proving especially valuable for surfacing cross-subsidiary data assets (e.g., banking spending data informing a retail promotion strategy) that no single business unit knew existed elsewhere in the company.
- The same generative AI companion tool that surfaces AI project ideas also counteracts predictable human biases in ideation — rank-and-file workers tend to over-index on efficiency opportunities while senior leaders over-index on growth opportunities, and a well-customized LLM prompts groups toward the full range of options either group alone would tend to miss.
- A complementary technical-level framework classifies AI problems along two axes instead of narrow-vs-wide: is the problem a prediction problem or a generation problem, and is the input data tabular/structured or unstructured (text, images, audio)? Generation problems (any task where the desired output is itself unstructured — text, images, video, music) have only one real answer: generative AI. Prediction problems with tabular input data should favor traditional machine learning (regression, gradient boosting) over deep learning, since ML models are faster to build, easier to interpret, and often just as accurate for structured data.
- For prediction problems with unstructured input data — the case where "the right answer has changed most in recent years" — the recommendation is to try an LLM first when both the inputs and the output labels are "everyday" natural language (e.g., classifying product reviews as mentioning a defect), since LLMs can often solve this class of problem with zero specialized training; only fall back to deep learning (ideally a pretrained model fine-tuned on a modest amount of problem-specific data) if the LLM proves unacceptable on accuracy, cost, latency, or data-privacy grounds.
- LLMs can also reduce deep learning's traditional data hunger indirectly: instead of manually labeling thousands of training examples, an LLM can perform the labeling itself ("LLM-as-a-judge"), or — if even unlabeled examples are scarce — generate synthetic training examples seeded from the available real ones.
- Traditional ML answers "what will happen" using correlations; causal ML answers "what would happen if" — the genuinely distinct counterfactual question a manager needs answered before committing to an R&D budget, a discount, or a staffing level, since a strong correlation (e.g. R&D spend and revenue both rising in a growing economy) can mislead if acted on as if it were causal.
- A decision suits causal ML when it is repeated often enough to generate dozens to thousands of historical examples, can be expressed as a number or binary choice, and has enough data on the decision itself, its outcome, and likely confounders — one-off decisions or those requiring intuition and creativity remain outside causal ML's reach, just as they remain outside prediction and generation's reach in this concept's other frameworks.
- Building a causal ML model requires an interdisciplinary team (data scientists with causal-inference expertise plus domain experts who can sketch a causal graph distinguishing cause, effect, and confounder) and a deliberate validation step, since managers can only ever observe the outcome of the decision actually made, never the counterfactual — companies use either a "human in the loop" approach (a model recommends, a human still decides, e.g. a newsroom's promoted-article recommendations) or controlled A/B-style experiments (e.g. a semiconductor manufacturer testing its causal model's machine-selection prediction against a live production run) to build trust before relying on it.

## Concept

<span class="src">How Generative AI Can Support Advanced Analytics Practice</span> (MIT SMR, June 2024) establishes the technical foundation that the later, higher-level framework builds directly on. Concerned that generative AI hype was siphoning resources and attention away from advanced analytics — predictive and prescriptive modeling with a long track record of proven business value — the authors argue the two technology families are complementary rather than competing, and detail concrete experiments combining them. In predictive analytics, LLMs can dramatically reduce the time needed to incorporate previously unusable unstructured data sources (such as customer complaint transcripts) by labeling and structuring them for use in a predictive model, and can translate technical explanation techniques like SHAP analysis into plain-language narratives non-technical stakeholders can actually use — demonstrated in a telecommunications company's debt-collection "next best action" project where a previously unused complaint dataset, once structured by an LLM, meaningfully redirected the project's recommended actions. In prescriptive analytics (optimization), LLMs can conversationally interview a decision maker to identify the objective function, decision variables, and constraints of a business problem and produce a first-draft mathematical model — always subject to expert review — and can explain a completed model's results, trade-offs, and even sources of infeasibility (such as insufficient total warehouse supply relative to combined store demand) in plain language, reducing the time technical teams spend manually explaining outputs to business stakeholders.

<span class="src">Analytical AI: A Better Way to Identify the Right AI Projects</span> (MIT SMR, January 2025), by an overlapping author team, addresses a prior question the 2024 article leaves implicit: before an organization can apply the right AI type to a decision, it first has to identify which AI projects are worth pursuing at all — and generative AI's high profile risks crowding out analytical AI project opportunities before they're even considered, despite McKinsey research suggesting generative AI use cases add only 15 to 40 percent on top of the economic benefits analytical AI use cases already deliver. The article's practical contribution is a customized generative AI "companion tool" for AI-project ideation workshops, fed a company's strategic reports, financial records, business-process documentation, and data inventory ahead of time. The tool supports a divergent stage (generating ideas while counteracting predictable human biases — rank-and-file workers tend to favor efficiency opportunities, senior leaders growth opportunities; structuring submitted ideas into topic clusters in real time; and helping participants discover what data the company actually has, which is often unknown even to people within the same organization) and a convergent stage (proposing realistic analytical-AI or generative-AI solutions for each opportunity, drafting a compelling pitch, and helping estimate the opportunity's financial impact, though the tool should not be trusted to do that quantification math itself). A holding company spanning telecommunications, retail, banking, and real estate used this approach across a series of workshops and surfaced roughly 100 possible AI projects — double what the company expected — with the tool proving particularly valuable for identifying cross-subsidiary data assets, such as suggesting the retail business use the telecom subsidiary's real-time location data to improve supply-chain visibility, an opportunity no single business unit would have spotted alone.

<span class="src">Calibrate AI Use to the Decision at Hand</span> (MIT SMR, May 2026), by an overlapping author team, generalizes this narrower analytics-focused argument into a full framework for deciding when to use analytical AI versus generative AI at all, citing the 2024 article directly as evidence for how generative AI functions as "an accelerator around the analytical core" in narrow decision contexts. The article's central move is classifying decisions as narrow (clear objectives, usable data, fast feedback loops — demand forecasting, fraud detection, store-location selection) or wide (contested, multicriteria, politically charged — market entry, brand repositioning, organizational redesign), using a six-question diagnostic scorecard (objective clarity, data readiness, causal stability, boundary transparency, feedback-loop speed, reversibility). Narrow decisions are analytical AI's home territory, where AI functions as a precise decision engine while humans set objectives, supply quality inputs, and define guardrails; generative AI's role there, echoing the 2024 findings directly, is accelerating around that analytical core — clarifying problem framing, documenting data logic, translating outputs into business language. Wide decisions call for a different calibration entirely: generative AI (and agentic AI, when designed with explicit checkpoints and traceability) helps synthesize diverse inputs, frame scenarios, and surface assumptions, making an inherently ambiguous decision space more legible without pretending to resolve the judgment and alignment work the decision actually requires. Two real-world cases illustrate the split: a European retail bank's churn-prevention effort (narrow — a predictive model drove targeting decisions, generative AI structured unstructured complaint data and drafted outreach scripts) and a multinational insurer's shift from product-centric to customer-segment organizational structure (wide — generative tools synthesized internal strategic history and external change-management research to help leadership articulate coherent scenarios and anticipate second-order effects, without generating the decision itself). The article closes with six practical steps for organizations — inventorying critical decisions and classifying each as narrow or wide, funding narrow AI bets that show fast measurable impact while halting "automation" pilots that are secretly wide decisions in disguise, and building separate playbooks and instrumentation for each decision type.

<span class="src">When to Use GenAI Versus Predictive AI</span> (MIT SMR, March 2025), by an unrelated author (MIT's Rama Ramakrishnan) working from a purely technical rather than organizational-decision angle, supplies a complementary answer to the same underlying question this concept's other sources address at the level of business decisions. Rather than classifying by decision breadth (narrow vs. wide), Ramakrishnan classifies by problem type and data structure: is the task a prediction problem (classification or regression, with predefined possible outputs) or a generation problem (the desired output is itself unstructured content), and is the input data tabular or unstructured? Generation problems have a single answer — generative AI is the only real option, since traditional machine learning and deep learning aren't built to produce novel unstructured output. Prediction problems split further: with tabular input data, traditional machine learning (regression, gradient boosting methods like XGBoost) is favored over deep learning, since it's faster to build, easier to interpret, and frequently just as accurate; but with unstructured input data (text or images), the framework identifies where "the right answer has changed most in recent years" — when both the input and the desired output labels are everyday, non-technical language (classifying whether a product review mentions a defect, for instance), current LLMs can often solve the problem directly with zero specialized training, a capability that didn't exist before generative AI and previously would have required collecting and manually labeling a large training set for a deep learning model. Only when an LLM proves unacceptable — on accuracy, cost, latency, or data-privacy grounds — does the framework recommend falling back to deep learning, ideally starting from a pretrained model fine-tuned on a comparatively small amount of problem-specific data rather than training from scratch; and even there, LLMs remain useful indirectly, since they can perform the labeling of training data themselves ("LLM-as-a-judge") or generate synthetic training examples when even unlabeled real examples are scarce. Ramakrishnan's framework operates one level "below" the other sources' narrow/wide decision diagnostic: where that framework tells an organization which broad category of AI capability (analytical vs. generative) a given business decision calls for, this framework tells a builder, once analytical AI has been selected, whether to actually implement it as classical machine learning or deep learning — and identifies the specific technical circumstances (everyday-language classification tasks) where generative AI has quietly displaced deep learning as the pragmatic first choice even for prediction problems, not just generation ones. <span class="src">When to Use GenAI Versus Predictive AI</span>

Read together, the four articles trace a single line of research spanning nearly two years, from technical mechanism to project selection to organizational framework to implementation-level technology choice. The 2024 piece demonstrates specific, tested ways generative AI can support (not replace) analytical modeling in real projects. The January 2025 piece steps back one level, to how organizations should identify AI project opportunities in the first place without generative AI's visibility advantage crowding out analytical AI opportunities. The March 2025 piece, from an independent author, supplies the technical decision rule for choosing among machine learning, deep learning, and generative AI once a specific prediction or generation task is in hand. And the May 2026 piece elevates the whole picture into a general diagnostic for matching any AI capability to any business decision — directly addressing why McKinsey's 2025 State of AI research found such a wide gap between AI adoption (88% of companies) and measurable bottom-line impact (only about 40%): most organizations are applying AI uniformly rather than calibrating the specific technology, at both the decision level and the implementation level, to the problem's actual characteristics.

<span class="src">A New Machine Learning Approach Answers What-If Questions</span> (MIT SMR, Stefan Feuerriegel, February 2025) supplies a technical category Ramakrishnan's prediction-vs-generation framework doesn't fully capture: causal machine learning, drawn from a five-year applied research program with companies including Booking.com, EthonAI, Hitachi ABB Power Grids, Neue Zürcher Zeitung, Novartis, and UBS. Traditional ML predicts "what will happen" from correlations — useful when only the probability of an outcome matters, such as whether a customer will repay a loan — but managers deciding between two courses of action (raise the R&D budget or not, offer a discount or not) need the answer to a genuinely different, counterfactual question: what would happen if a specific decision were made, holding confounding factors like economic climate constant. Causal ML, better understood as "counterfactual prediction" than as a source of definitive answers, fills this gap using causal inference rather than pure correlation. In practice this requires three categories of data — decisions (what managers have actually done in the past), outcomes (the measurable results), and confounders (factors like economic conditions or workforce composition that influence both the decision and the outcome, and must be identified and controlled for or the model risks reversing cause and effect entirely). Concrete deployments illustrate the pattern: Booking.com uses causal ML to determine not just whether to offer a discount but which specific customers should receive one; Hitachi ABB cut semiconductor yield loss by roughly half by identifying which machine combination in its etching and implantation steps causally produced the best-quality chips, validated afterward through a controlled A/B-style production experiment; and Neue Zürcher Zeitung replaced editors' instinct-driven article-promotion decisions with a causal model recommending which articles to feature, while keeping editors as the final human-in-the-loop decision-makers, building trust over time as the model's recommendations proved reliable even in cases where they diverged from editorial gut feeling. A decision suits causal ML specifically when it recurs often enough to generate dozens to thousands of historical examples, can be expressed as a number or a binary choice, and has adequate data across all three categories — the same recurring, well-bounded, data-rich profile this concept's narrow/wide diagnostic already identifies as favoring analytical AI generally, with causal ML representing a further refinement within that narrow-decision territory specifically for questions of counterfactual impact rather than simple forecasting. Building and trusting a causal ML model requires the same interdisciplinary collaboration (data scientists, causal-inference specialists, and domain experts sketching a shared causal graph) this concept's other technical source identifies as necessary for prescriptive-analytics work, reinforcing that generative AI's accessibility has not eliminated the need for genuine cross-functional modeling expertise on an organization's highest-value decisions. <span class="src">A New Machine Learning Approach Answers What-If Questions</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither article addresses how the narrow/wide diagnostic should handle decisions that shift category over time — for example, a decision that starts wide (a novel market entry) but becomes progressively narrower as data accumulates and uncertainty resolves.
- The 2026 article's "wide decision" case study (the insurer's organizational redesign) does not report a concrete outcome or measurable result, unlike the narrow-decision churn-prevention case, leaving the practical evidence for generative AI's value in wide decisions comparatively less substantiated than for narrow ones.
- The causal ML source doesn't explain how a builder should choose between causal ML and Ramakrishnan's plain prediction framework when a decision could plausibly be framed either way — for instance, whether "predicting churn risk" (prediction) and "predicting the effect of a retention offer on churn" (causal) are genuinely different modeling problems in practice or two framings of the same underlying task.
