## Summary

Analytics models change decisions only when they are built for how executives decide, and a four-layer framework of data, analytics, decision and narrative addresses this. Google's small-business analytics team developed it after a technically sound staffing model failed to gain traction, and each layer targets a specific failure mode, from inconsistent metric definitions to poor storytelling.

## Highlights

- A Google SMB analytics team built a sophisticated model forecasting staffing needs across more than 100 countries, complete with seasonality, geography, and customer-prioritization rules — but senior stakeholders showed little enthusiasm and the resulting business decision stalled, because the presentation lacked practical business framing.
- The core lesson is that analytics must be built for how decisions are made, not just how data is analyzed — sophisticated, technically validated models routinely fail to gain traction with executives if they lack a clear narrative and business context.
- The four-layer framework — data, analytics, decision, narrative — is a pyramid where each layer builds on the one below it, and each targets a distinct, specific source of executive disengagement rather than treating "storytelling" as a single post-hoc communication problem.
- At the data layer, inconsistent metric definitions silently corrupt downstream analysis — different teams counted "customer meeting" so differently (from a brief email to an all-day conference) that reported meeting counts varied by more than 2x across regions, producing wildly different staffing recommendations until a single cross-functional definition was agreed and enforced.
- At the analytics layer, executives prioritize model explainability and practical relevance over technical sophistication, so the team built an interactive model with transparent, adjustable key drivers (meeting frequency, meeting length, available rep time) that recalculated staffing impact in real time and highlighted which specific drivers caused a result to shift.
- At the decision layer, outputs must be grounded in real-world constraints (e.g. country-specific labor laws requiring native-speaker representatives) — rather than building every legal and operational constraint into the model itself, the team instead labeled which levers were actually feasible, which shifted discussion from questioning the model's legitimacy to optimizing within known boundaries.
- At the narrative layer, storytelling was embedded into the structure of the analysis from the start rather than added as dashboard polish at the end — presentations led with business context and the decision at stake (e.g. the effect of a 10% efficiency increase) before descending into supporting technical detail, the reverse of a typical technical-logic-first walkthrough.
- The framework has since been applied beyond its original staffing use case to sales strategy optimization, business planning, and executive decision enablement at Google SMB, and has been adopted by cross-functional teams in Google's finance and marketing organizations.

## Concept

<span class="src">How One Google Team Built Storytelling Into Analytics</span> (MIT SMR, Jiaxi Zhu, 2025-09-02) documents a four-layer analytics framework developed within Google's Small and Medium Business (SMB) division after a technically sound model repeatedly failed to move executives to action. The team had built a model forecasting volatile customer-support staffing demand across more than 100 countries, simulating thousands of scenarios and accounting for seasonality, geography, and complex customer-prioritization rules; the model was validated, its assumptions vetted, its logic pressure-tested. Yet when presented to senior stakeholders, it generated little enthusiasm — executives focused on practical questions ("What does this mean for next quarter's staffing in Latin America?") the presentation hadn't addressed, and weeks of technical work stalled without producing a decision. This was not an isolated failure: the team observed repeatedly that technically sound models failed to generate executive movement, because data professionals believed better models alone would improve business outcomes while executives were overwhelmed by complexity and skeptical of black-box insights they couldn't contextualize. The resulting framework, built starting in 2023 and refined through mid-2024, integrates storytelling directly into the design of the analytics stack from the outset rather than treating it as a presentation-layer afterthought applied just before delivery. <span class="src">How One Google Team Built Storytelling Into Analytics</span>

The framework's four layers form a pyramid, each addressing a distinct, specific failure mode and each building on the one beneath it. The data layer targets inconsistent metric definitions as the most basic blocker to actionable insight: in a project analyzing ideal sales-rep meeting loads, the team discovered different teams counted "customer meeting" so inconsistently — ranging from a brief email exchange to an all-day conference — that some regions reported more than double the meeting count of others using the same underlying activity, corrupting the resulting customer-to-representative ratio recommendations. The fix was not a technical one but a governance one: partnering across functions to agree a single business-relevant definition (only scheduled phone or in-person conversations counted; emails were recategorized as "light-touch engagement") and building a unified, consistently tagged data source around it. The analytics layer targets model opacity: rather than a static model, the team built an interactive tool exposing key drivers (meeting frequency by segment, meeting length, available representative time net of internal responsibilities like training) as adjustable inputs, recalculating staffing and revenue impact in real time and explicitly surfacing which specific drivers were responsible when a result shifted — converting stakeholder debates from arguing over hidden assumptions to discussing trade-offs and resourcing priorities directly. The decision layer targets the gap between what a model recommends and what an organization can actually do: some of the model's optimal staffing moves were infeasible under country-specific labor law (native-speaker requirements) or role-reassignment restrictions, and rather than encoding every such constraint into the model itself (which would have made it rigid and opaque), the team instead labeled which levers were genuinely actionable versus regulation-constrained, which shifted planning conversations from disputing the model's legitimacy ("why can't we shift more reps into that market?") to optimizing within already-agreed boundaries. The narrative layer, finally, targets delivery itself: rather than walking executives through technical logic from the bottom up, presentations were restructured to lead with business context and the specific decision at stake — such as the downstream effect of a 10% increase in sales-team efficiency — using visualization to surface trade-offs, before descending into supporting detail only as needed to build confidence in the recommendation. <span class="src">How One Google Team Built Storytelling Into Analytics</span>

Since its initial application to global sales capacity planning, the framework has been extended to sales strategy optimization, business planning, and broader executive decision enablement within Google's SMB division, and has also been adopted by cross-functional teams within Google's finance and marketing organizations. The article's central claim is that accuracy and technical sophistication alone are insufficient for analytics to earn real influence at the executive level — analytics must instead be built to reflect how decisions actually get made, including the ambiguity, practical limits, and trade-offs decision-makers operate under, with each of the four layers (clean shared data, transparent models, decision framing bounded by real constraints, and narrative delivery) contributing a necessary but individually insufficient piece of that goal. <span class="src">How One Google Team Built Storytelling Into Analytics</span>

## Open Questions

- The article is a single first-person account from within one team at one company; it doesn't address whether the four-layer framework has been validated or replicated outside Google SMB, or whether its lessons hold in organizations with less mature internal data infrastructure to begin with.
- It's not established how much of the framework's success depended on the author's own seniority and direct relationships with stakeholders (as head of analytics) versus the framework's structure itself — a less senior team applying the same four layers might not achieve the same executive buy-in.
