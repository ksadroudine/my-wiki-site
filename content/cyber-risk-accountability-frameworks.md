## Summary

What the July 2024 CrowdStrike outage — which crashed 8.5 million systems and triggered a lawsuit between Delta and CrowdStrike — reveals about gaps in how organizations assess, mitigate, and assign accountability for cyber risk. Frames four open questions the industry still lacks systematic methods to answer, and argues for a new "explainable cyber risk management" approach.

## Concept

On July 19, 2024, a single faulty content update from cybersecurity vendor CrowdStrike crashed more than 8.5 million systems worldwide, disrupting operations for days across thousands of organizations, including hundreds of Fortune 1000 companies, with total losses estimated above $5 billion and roughly $1.5 billion in insurer payouts across business-interruption, cyber, and system-failure coverage — making it one of the clearest large-scale demonstrations yet of aggregated cyber risk accumulation, where a single point of failure propagates losses across an entire dependent ecosystem. Delta Air Lines, one of the most visibly affected companies, subsequently sued CrowdStrike, claiming the outage was "catastrophic," disrupted 7,000 flights and 1.3 million customers over five days, and cost more than $500 million — attributing the damage to what it called CrowdStrike's "forced untested updates to its customers," including onto systems like Delta's own where automatic updates had been deliberately disabled. CrowdStrike, while acknowledging the root cause was a fault in its own software update, responded that Delta's claims reflected "a lack of understanding of how modern cybersecurity works" and argued Delta's unusually slow recovery stemmed from its own failure to modernize an "antiquated IT infrastructure," rather than from anything CrowdStrike could reasonably be held liable for beyond its contractually capped liability. This dispute unfolded against a broader backdrop of worsening cyber risk despite growing cybersecurity investment: the average cost of a data breach reached $4.88 million in 2024 (up 10 percent year over year), confirmed data breaches hit a record high in Verizon's annual analysis, and a Milliman market survey identified structural gaps across the industry — a highly fragmented cybersecurity solutions market, cybersecurity decisions built on disjointed data points rather than end-to-end risk perspectives, weak capability to assess cyber risk concentration and systemic dependency, a soft cyber insurance market hampered by insufficient historical data, limited supply-chain risk transparency, and new uncertainty introduced by emerging technologies like AI, quantum computing, and cloud computing. <span class="src">What the 2024 CrowdStrike Glitch Can Teach Us About Cyber Risk</span>

The CrowdStrike incident illustrates all of these gaps at once, and the analysis frames it around four open questions that neither CrowdStrike nor the broader industry currently has systematic, quantifiable methods to answer. First is the level of risk any given update actually carries: CrowdStrike's Falcon software issues content updates — sometimes multiple per day — specifically to improve protection against emerging threats, and had done so thousands of times since 2013 with almost no reported problems, but the risk of any content update is never truly zero, so the risk introduced by deploying it needs to be weighed against the risk eliminated by the improved protection it provides — an assessment the industry currently lacks effective tools to perform systematically. Second is how that update risk should be optimally mitigated — applied immediately or deliberately delayed — which requires balancing the risk of downstream business disruption from a possible faulty update (a risk that falls as the delay increases) against the risk of remaining exposed to attack without the update's protection in the meantime (a risk that grows as the delay increases); Delta's lawsuit noted it had deliberately disabled automatic updates specifically to maintain its own change-management controls, yet CrowdStrike, treating updates as zero-risk, forced the update through regardless — a mistake CrowdStrike acknowledged afterward by changing its deployment process, though the industry still lacks agreement on what risk information vendors should disclose and how customers should act on it. Third is how an organization ensures adequate business resiliency for when — not if — an update turns out to be faulty despite high-quality development, testing, and certification processes; both Delta and CrowdStrike made vague, largely unsubstantiated claims about the adequacy of Delta's own IT resiliency compared to peer airlines, illustrating that the industry lacks a systematic, rigorous, comparable methodology for assessing organizational cyber resiliency in the first place. Fourth is how accountability for resulting losses should be assigned across the parties involved — CrowdStrike pointed to a contractual liability cap in the single-digit millions, implying limited responsibility for Delta's much larger claimed losses, while Microsoft was also drawn into the dispute over its role certifying Windows kernel-access software, illustrating how cyber dependencies across multiple third parties introduce accountability questions that current contractual and legal frameworks handle ambiguously at best. <span class="src">What the 2024 CrowdStrike Glitch Can Teach Us About Cyber Risk</span>

The proposed response is a shift toward what the analysis calls "explainable cyber risk management" — a new paradigm addressing the fact that most current cyber solutions focus on specific local targets rather than providing end-to-end risk insight in a systematic, credible, and justifiable manner. This requires organizations to develop five specific capabilities: modeling the "what-if" downstream business impact, direct and escalated, of a cyber event at any supply-chain partner, since many organizations (Delta apparently included) hadn't considered scenarios like a faulty vendor update in advance; establishing explicit cyber-resiliency objectives and processes for impactful events at supply-chain partners, given how widely recovery times and losses varied across organizations affected by the same CrowdStrike incident; continuously assessing the likelihood and interdependence of cyber events, internal and external, rather than relying on static or infrequent assessments, since CrowdStrike itself allegedly failed to adequately assess the likelihood of faults in its own update process; ensuring transparent internal and external communication as the basis for risk decisions, building on CrowdStrike's post-incident move to give customers more control over update timing but going further to share the specific risk attributes of each update; and specifying well-defined accountability boundaries between relevant parties in advance of an incident, rather than leaving them ambiguous until litigation forces a resolution, as the CrowdStrike-Delta-Microsoft dispute illustrates. The broader conclusion is that occasional unintentional technology failures are inevitable and malicious cyberattacks will keep growing in scale and impact as reliance on emerging digital technologies increases, so more effective, transparent, structured, and agile cyber risk management approaches are urgently needed — not just better individual security tools. <span class="src">What the 2024 CrowdStrike Glitch Can Teach Us About Cyber Risk</span>
