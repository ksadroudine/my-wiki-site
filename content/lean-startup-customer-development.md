## Summary

Customer Development and the Lean Startup reframe a startup as a temporary organization searching for a repeatable, scalable business model, not as a smaller version of an established company executing a known plan. The method proceeds through customer discovery, customer validation, customer creation and company building, where the first two are the search for a model and the last two the execution of it. The concept covers the business model canvas, the pivot as a substantive change to one of its nine boxes, early adopters called earlyvangelists as the target of a minimum viable product, and the build-measure-learn loop, in which each product version is an experiment that turns a hypothesis into validated fact. It also describes startup metrics that track hypotheses turning into facts and growth hacking as a cross-functional responsibility.

## Highlights

- A startup is defined not as a smaller version of a company but as a temporary organization designed to search for a repeatable and scalable business model; this "search" mode requires a different organizational structure (a founder-led Customer Development team) than the functional structure needed once a validated model moves into "execution" mode. <span class="src">The Startup Owner's Manual</span>
- Customer Development runs through four stop-and-assess steps — customer discovery, customer validation, customer creation, and company-building — where the first two constitute the "search" for a business model and the last two constitute "execution" of a model once validated. <span class="src">The Startup Owner's Manual</span>
- A pivot is a substantive change to one of the nine boxes of the business model canvas driven by customer feedback, distinct from a minor iteration (e.g. changing a price point); pivots are treated as a normal, expected part of the search process rather than a failure. <span class="src">The Startup Owner's Manual</span>
- "Earlyvangelists" — customers who already recognize they have a painful problem, have cobbled together an interim workaround, and have or can acquire budget — are the correct target for a minimum viable product, not mainstream customers who expect a finished, fully-featured product. <span class="src">The Startup Owner's Manual</span>
- Startup-appropriate metrics track progress converting hypotheses into facts (e.g. whether the problem and product features have been validated) rather than execution against a static plan; the only financial metric that always matters at this stage is cash-burn rate and months of runway remaining. <span class="src">The Startup Owner's Manual</span>
- The Build-Measure-Learn loop treats a minimum viable product as an experiment designed to generate validated learning as fast as possible, not as a shrunken version of the final product; the goal of each loop is to test a specific hypothesis and decide, with evidence, whether to persevere or pivot. <span class="src">The Lean Startup</span>

- "Growth hacking" reframes growth as an organization-wide, cross-functional responsibility spanning product, engineering, and support as well as marketing, driven by rigorous analytics and a deep understanding of customer behavior rather than treated as marketing's job alone — a shift the source frames as an evolution of marketing rather than an alternative to it. <span class="src">Startup Growth Engines</span>

## Concept

Both Customer Development (Steve Blank and Bob Dorf) and the Lean Startup (Eric Ries, building directly on Blank's work) begin from the same reframing: a startup is not a smaller version of an established company but a temporary organization whose sole purpose is to search for a repeatable, scalable, and profitable business model, as distinct from an established company's purpose of executing a known model. On day one, a startup's understanding of its customer, problem, and business model is treated as a set of unproven hypotheses rather than facts, and the entire discipline exists to convert those hypotheses into evidence as quickly and cheaply as possible before committing to scale. <span class="src">The Startup Owner's Manual</span>

The Customer Development process organizes this search into four sequential steps, each with an explicit stop-and-assess checkpoint before proceeding. Customer discovery translates the founder's vision into a set of business-model hypotheses (recorded on a nine-box business model canvas covering value proposition, customer segments, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partners, and cost structure) and then runs face-to-face tests, first of the customer's perceived problem and then of the proposed solution, targeting "earlyvangelist" customers rather than the mainstream market — earlyvangelists are recognizable by already having an acute, recognized problem, an improvised interim workaround, a search already under way for a solution, and either existing or readily acquirable budget. Customer validation repeats these tests at greater scale and rigor to confirm the model is genuinely repeatable and scalable, not just anecdotally promising. Only once discovery and validation succeed — described as reaching "escape velocity" — does the company move into customer creation (aggressively spending to build end-user demand) and company-building (transitioning from a founder-led search organization into a functionally-structured execution organization). <span class="src">The Startup Owner's Manual</span>

A central working distinction is between a pivot and an iteration: an iteration is a minor adjustment to a business-model component (e.g. changing a price from $99 to $79), while a pivot is a substantive change to one or more of the nine business-model canvas boxes, driven directly by evidence from customer testing. Far from signaling failure, frequent pivoting is presented as the expected mechanism by which a startup converges on a working model — the canvas is redrawn after each pivot, and the accumulated stack of past canvases documents the company's actual learning trajectory. Because startups face a hard cash constraint that established companies don't, the methodology insists on startup-specific metrics rather than the metrics used to run an existing business: rather than tracking progress against a revenue plan or launch date, teams should track whether the customer problem and minimum feature set have actually been validated through direct customer contact, alongside the one financial number that always matters regardless of stage — cash-burn rate and the number of months of runway remaining. <span class="src">The Startup Owner's Manual</span>

The Lean Startup formalizes the same underlying loop — most explicitly credited by Ries to Blank's Customer Development work — as Build-Measure-Learn: a minimum viable product is built not as a stripped-down version of the eventual finished product but as a deliberately minimal experiment designed to generate the maximum validated learning for the minimum effort, after which the results are measured against a specific hypothesis and used to decide, with evidence rather than opinion, whether to persevere with the current approach or pivot to a different one. This closes the loop back to Customer Development's core discipline of treating every product decision as a testable hypothesis rather than an assumption to build around. <span class="src">The Lean Startup</span>

A complementary account of what happens once a startup has validated its business model and moved into scaling is offered under the "growth hacking" label: sustained growth is described as coming from a shared organizational commitment that treats growth as everyone's responsibility — product, engineering, and support, not marketing alone — built on a genuinely must-have product and a rigorous, data-driven, deeply customer-informed approach to acquisition, retention, and monetization, reframing "growth hacking" as an evolution of marketing practice rather than a replacement for or opponent of it. <span class="src">Startup Growth Engines</span>
