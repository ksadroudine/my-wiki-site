## Summary

Brands face AI-driven trust risk from three directions at once — a responsible-AI expert panel says undisclosed AI use erodes customer trust before anything even goes wrong, seven years of consumer research shows five specific psychological pitfalls that turn an inevitable AI failure into disproportionate brand damage, and the rise of agentic commerce forces brands to compete for both human attention and AI agents' own recommendation logic through a genuinely new discipline — "share of model."

## Concept

MIT Sloan Management Review and Boston Consulting Group's third consecutive annual responsible-AI expert panel — an international group of academics and practitioners assembled to gauge how organizations worldwide are implementing responsible AI — put a direct provocation to its panel of experts: should companies be required to disclose AI use in their products and offerings to customers? The response was close to consensus: 84% of experts agreed or strongly agreed. The panel's reasoning clustered around several reinforcing arguments. Transparency, several experts argued, is the foundational precondition for any effective responsible-AI framework and the most basic form of transparency required across virtually every domain of life — with several panelists explicitly comparing AI disclosure to existing product-labeling norms for sustainability, climate impact, fair trade, or nutritional content, framing it as a natural extension of an established practice rather than a novel regulatory burden. Beyond precedent, panelists argued companies have a genuine ethical obligation to disclose AI use specifically because it enables informed decision-making and risk assessment — one panelist drew a direct analogy to disclosing a drug's side effects, arguing AI users have an equivalent right to know the risks (hallucinations, fabricated sources) associated with a tool before relying on it. Beyond the ethical case, the panel noted concrete business reasons for disclosure even where not legally mandated: transparency helps build and sustain trust among customers, investors specifically screening for credible responsible-AI governance, and employees who value working for a company with genuine RAI commitments — meaning voluntary disclosure functions as a competitive differentiator, not merely a compliance cost. The panel was equally direct about disclosure's practical difficulties, however. Several experts noted no clear, stable definition exists that reliably differentiates "AI" from ordinary software or decision-making systems, making it genuinely hard to specify when disclosure is legally required in the first place; others warned that mandating disclosure without clearly specifying the particular harms it's meant to address risks producing vague, box-checking compliance rather than genuine transparency, and that disclosures can inadvertently expose competitively sensitive information about a company's underlying models or training data. Perhaps the panel's most consistent caution was that poor-quality disclosure can actively undermine the transparency and accountability it's meant to serve: disclosures buried in dense terms-of-service language, or written in language ordinary customers can't parse, fail to accomplish their purpose regardless of whether they technically satisfy a legal requirement — several panelists specifically called for disclosures written in plain language and designed to be visually accessible at the actual point of interaction, not merely present somewhere in a legal document. The panel converged on a workable middle ground for when disclosure is actually necessary: most agreed disclosure should be required whenever a customer is directly interacting with an AI system rather than a human, and whenever AI is being used to make decisions of real consequence to a person's life (health care, finance, hiring decisions were cited repeatedly), including disclosing how affected individuals can reach a human to challenge an AI-driven decision or action. Many panelists extended this to data-specific disclosures as well — customers, several argued, have a right to know how their personal data is being used to train or operate the AI systems processing it, not just that AI is involved at all. At the same time, the panel was explicit that universal, blanket disclosure requirements would quickly become impractical as AI becomes genuinely pervasive across ordinary business operations — using AI to prioritize internal emails, point employees to open parking spaces, or draft slide decks was repeatedly cited as an example of AI use that doesn't warrant customer-facing disclosure, in the same way companies don't disclose their use of a search engine or a mobile phone; the operative distinction several panelists drew was whether the AI use touches or profiles the customer directly (which warrants disclosure) versus purely internal operational use (which generally doesn't). The panel's synthesized recommendations for organizations: ground disclosure decisions explicitly in core responsible-AI principles like transparency and accountability rather than treating disclosure as a standalone legal checkbox; make disclosures genuinely easy to understand by engaging cross-functional teams including UX/UI designers, not just legal drafters, so disclosures appear in the right place at the right moment rather than only in buried legal text; voluntarily go beyond whatever the applicable legal minimum happens to be, particularly wherever AI is making decisions of real consequence to individuals, since ethical obligation and legal requirement aren't the same threshold; and publish broader details of an organization's responsible-AI program and code of conduct beyond narrow product-level disclosures, since this builds trust with customers, investors, and employees simultaneously rather than only satisfying a narrow disclosure requirement for one specific product. <span class="src">Artificial Intelligence Disclosures Are Key to Customer Trust</span>

Where the MIT SMR/BCG panel addresses trust erosion before anything goes wrong, HBR's Julian De Freitas turns to the harder problem of managing brand damage once an AI system inevitably does fail — grounding the analysis in a stark real-world case study. In October 2023, a General Motors Cruise robotaxi was involved in an accident in San Francisco in which a human-driven Nissan struck a pedestrian who was then thrown into the autonomous vehicle's path; an independent engineering investigation found no human driver could plausibly have avoided the resulting collision, meaning Cruise's vehicle genuinely wasn't at fault for the initial impact. But Cruise's report to regulators omitted that the vehicle then dragged the severely injured pedestrian 20 feet, and when that omission surfaced, the fallout was severe and disproportionate to the underlying fault: a $1.5 million federal fine, a Department of Justice criminal investigation that ultimately produced a $500,000 settlement, revocation of Cruise's San Francisco operating permit, half the company's workforce laid off, the CEO's resignation, more than 50% of the company's valuation erased, a driverless Waymo vehicle set on fire by an angry crowd in an unrelated incident, and regulatory investigations opened into multiple other AV developers — culminating in GM ending its robotaxi business entirely by the end of 2024. Drawing on seven years and seven separate research studies into how consumers perceive and react to AI failures, De Freitas identifies five specific, empirically documented psychological pitfalls that together explain why AI failures generate this kind of disproportionate brand damage, and that marketers can actively manage rather than simply absorb as an unavoidable cost of deploying AI. First, people blame AI first: in a 5,000-participant study, respondents assigned significantly more liability to the maker of a not-at-fault autonomous vehicle than to an equivalent not-at-fault human driver in an identical accident scenario, a bias that has been independently replicated by researchers in China, suggesting it persists across cultural contexts. The mechanism, De Freitas's research suggests, is that a novel technology like an AV prompts people to imagine unrealistic counterfactuals — what a hypothetically perfect AI driver could have done — holding AI to a standard no human would be held to; deliberately shifting attention toward the actually-at-fault party measurably reduces this bias, but that tactic must not be confused with concealing genuinely relevant facts, since Cruise's leadership emphasized the human driver's fault while omitting the dragging incident, and once that omission was discovered, the company lost control of the media narrative entirely and permanently damaged regulator trust in the process. Second, one company's AI failure contaminates public perception of other companies' AI: in a study informing 3,724 participants about a benefit-allocation error and randomly attributing it to either a human employee or an algorithm, participants were significantly more likely to predict that an algorithm (rather than a human employee) in an entirely unrelated state's system would make the same kind of error — a pattern the researchers replicated across multiple other AI failure scenarios, attributing it to people treating "AI" as a single homogeneous category rather than recognizing that different AI systems have genuinely different capabilities and failure modes (this ripple effect showed up concretely after the Cruise incident, when a Waymo vehicle was attacked in an unrelated incident and regulators opened investigations into multiple other AV companies that had nothing to do with the original accident). The available countermeasure is explicit differentiation: Anthropic's marketing of Claude specifically emphasizes its distinct "constitutional" training methodology precisely to dissociate the product from competitors and pre-establish grounds for limiting contamination if a competitor fails, and companies that clearly communicate when a human supervises an AI system and retains final decision authority ("human in the loop") also measurably reduce the odds that a given failure gets read as an indictment of AI in general. Third, people place disproportionately more blame on companies that overstate their AI's capabilities: in a 9,492-participant study using driving simulations, participants using a vehicle labeled "Autopilot" (implying high capability) took manual control significantly later in a simulated near-collision than participants using an identically-capable vehicle labeled "Copilot" (implying more modest, assistive capability) — meaning the label itself induced complacency — and separately, participants consistently judged companies more liable for accidents when the company's own marketing had emphasized the AI's superior capability, directly implicating campaigns like GM's "Humans are terrible drivers" advertising for Cruise as a factor that may have increased, rather than decreased, the public's assignment of blame once an accident occurred. The practical corrective De Freitas offers: companies using an inherently capability-inflating label (like "Autopilot") should compensate by clearly and accurately explaining the system's true capabilities and limitations elsewhere — a website, fine print — since his research finds companies that pair a misleading label with clear supplementary disclosure face measurably fewer penalties after a failure than companies relying on the misleading label alone; the cleaner, if less marketing-friendly, alternative is simply choosing an honestly calibrated label from the outset. Fourth, people judge humanized AI systems more harshly for identical failures: in a study describing an investment-allocation error as caused either by a plain "algorithm program" or by a named, humanized AI persona ("Charles"), brand attitudes were measurably more negative in the humanized condition — because humanized AI gets ascribed genuine mental capacities (understanding, planning, remembering) that in turn increase the perceived responsibility assigned to it when something goes wrong, an effect that compounds when both verbal cues (first-person language, a human name) and visual cues (a humanlike avatar) are present together, and that a roughly 500,000-session analysis of a telecommunications company's chatbot interactions found gets specifically amplified when customers are already angry going into the interaction. The practical guidance is to reserve humanized bot personas for lower-stakes, generally positive contexts (product search, general assistance) rather than deploying them in domains prone to angry customer interactions (customer service complaints, billing disputes), and to proactively temper user expectations at the outset of an interaction the way Slack's chatbot does ("I try to be helpful. But I'm still just a bot. Sorry!"), which research finds measurably reduces user frustration when the bot subsequently underperforms. Fifth and finally, people are specifically outraged by AI systems with programmed group-based preferences, distinct from ordinary AI error: after a Mercedes-Benz executive stated the company's self-driving cars would prioritize passenger safety over pedestrian safety if forced to choose, the resulting media firestorm forced a swift public reversal; De Freitas's own controlled study, presenting 826 participants with autonomous-vehicle dilemmas that were resolved either randomly or via a programmed group-based preference (favoring, say, a child over an elderly pedestrian), found participants were significantly more outraged by any programmed group-based preference than by an equivalent random outcome — while a related study by other researchers found people are considerably more supportive of AI systems using structural preferences (e.g., prioritizing saving a greater number of lives, regardless of who they are) than group-based ones, since a structural rule offers a clear, broadly agreeable utilitarian justification that a demographic-based one does not. De Freitas's overarching lesson is that AI failure is genuinely inevitable at scale, which makes proactive management of these five specific, empirically-grounded risk factors — rather than simply hoping failure never occurs — the actual work of responsible AI marketing. <span class="src">Don’t Let an AI Failure Harm Your Brand</span>

A third HBR analysis, by Oguz Acar and David Schweidel, addresses a distinct but closely related challenge: how brands must adapt not just to prevent AI-driven trust damage, but to proactively compete for relevance as AI agents increasingly mediate the entire customer relationship. The starting example is instructive: Pernod Ricard's head of digital and design discovered, after partnering with a marketing agency to formally audit how leading AI models represented his liquor brands, that the models were often wrong or incomplete — one popular model, for instance, miscategorized the affordably-priced Ballantine's Scotch as a "prestige" product. In response, Pernod Ricard launched an ongoing program to monitor and improve what the researchers call "share of model" — the measure of how often and how favorably a brand appears in AI-generated results relative to its competitors — regularly prompting popular AI models with questions about its products, cataloging the responses, and iteratively adjusting website and advertising copy specifically to correct how the models describe its brands (Danone runs a comparable ongoing monitoring program). This shift, the authors argue, is the direct successor to the decades-long discipline of search-engine keyword optimization, now applied to an environment where a growing share of consumers research and increasingly complete purchases through AI intermediaries rather than traditional search: a July 2025 survey of 750 US consumers found 60% expect to use agentic AI to complete purchases within the next 12 months, and major platforms (OpenAI partnering with Stripe, PayPal, Walmart, and Shopify to enable in-chat purchasing) are actively building the infrastructure to make that shift concrete. The authors identify three coexisting, non-mutually-exclusive modes of AI-mediated brand-consumer interaction that companies must navigate simultaneously: brand agents, in which a company's own AI engages directly with customers (Capital One's Auto Navigator Chat Concierge can check dealership inventory, schedule test drives, and estimate trade-in values, letting customers complete most of a car-buying journey before ever visiting a dealership); consumer agents, in which an independent AI acts on an individual's behalf across multiple brands (Claude's "computer use" capability navigating screens and completing purchases functions as something closer to a personal digital representative than a brand tool); and full AI intermediation, in which agents interact autonomously on both sides of a transaction with minimal human involvement at all (ChatGPT's agent autonomously searching OpenTable, selecting a restaurant, and completing a reservation). To navigate this landscape, the authors propose a three-stage adoption framework. Stage one is determining whether deploying an AI agent makes sense at all for a given context, since research spanning 119,000 participants found consumer receptiveness to AI agents is high in low-stakes, routine, predictable-outcome domains (illustrated by Amazon's decade-long evolution from the physical Dash Button through Subscribe & Save to the fully autonomous Alexa+ AI, which can interpret an intent like "restock my groceries" and execute the full multistep task chain unassisted) but notably lower in personally meaningful purchases, domains where visible human effort itself signals care (gift-giving, personal messages), high-stakes decisions where people want to retain direct control (healthcare, per Boston University and Salesforce survey research), subjective rather than objective judgment calls, and luxury or premium experiences where customers are explicitly paying for human guidance as part of the product itself (Lamborghini's CEO has deliberately resisted autonomous-driving features specifically because the brand's value proposition is the visceral experience of driving, not being driven) — the authors note this choice needn't be binary, illustrated by AG1's hybrid strategy of training its AI agent on brand voice and real-time customer data for routine support interactions while keeping community-building interactions (like responding personally to every customer review) strictly human, a program that achieved 99% AI-agent interaction quality scores while measurably freeing human staff for complex, empathy-requiring cases. Stage two, once customers are receptive to AI agents generally, is persuading them to use a brand's own agent rather than an independent, third-party alternative — a genuinely uphill battle, since consumer agents hold structural advantages in both perceived trust (people naturally trust agents they personally control, viewing them as unbiased advocates analogous to a fiduciary financial adviser, whereas brand-controlled agents are reasonably suspected of serving the company's interests over the customer's) and data breadth (an independent agent like ChatGPT can draw on a user's accumulated history across every brand and domain, not just one company's data). The authors argue brands can offset this disadvantage by leveraging what independent agents structurally cannot replicate — deep, proprietary, real-time product data (Sephora's AI draws on Color IQ technology differentiating 140,000 skin tones and first-party profiles from more than 34 million Beauty Insider loyalty members, driving triple the purchase-completion rate and a 30% reduction in returns) and human-in-the-loop escalation paths unavailable to a purely independent agent (ServiceNow's agent autonomously resolves 80% of incoming queries and automatically escalates the remaining 20% to human experts, cutting complex-case resolution time by 52%) — while noting that responsible-AI signaling itself measurably drives adoption: a Salesforce survey found 72% of consumers demand transparency about whether they're interacting with AI or a human, and the authors' own three large-scale discrete-choice experiments with 3,268 UK participants found responsible-AI features like privacy and auditability were consistently among the most influential factors in AI-product adoption decisions — in one pension-planning-app study, embedding responsible-AI features into the product design lifted predicted adoption from 2.4% to 63.2%. Stage three addresses the reality that many consumers will nonetheless choose to rely on independent agents regardless of how well a brand's own agent performs, creating a distinct strategic imperative: ensuring a brand is visible to, and ultimately recommended by, those independent consumer agents. Instacart's rapid, repeated adaptation illustrates the discipline required — building both an in-app ChatGPT-powered search tool and a separate ChatGPT plug-in enabling users to add ingredients to a cart directly from a recipe conversation inside ChatGPT itself, then launching a custom GPT when OpenAI introduced that capability, maintaining presence across each new integration surface as the underlying platform evolved. Harvard Business School research the authors cite documents specific technical levers for shaping how AI agents perceive and recommend a brand, including "strategic text sequences" — algorithmically generated text, often nonsensical to human readers, added to a product page that measurably increased one fictional test brand's likelihood of being surfaced as an LLM's top recommendation — and the emergence of next-generation reasoning models that expose their own decision criteria, letting brands understand and align with the actual factors (price, compatibility, reviews) driving a given recommendation; separately, Carnegie Mellon research found that even simple prompt-wording changes (substituting synonyms in an otherwise identical query) shifted brand recommendation likelihood by as much as 78.3%, underscoring that understanding how consumers actually phrase their AI queries — not just what products they're searching for — is now foundational to brand marketing content strategy. The authors also point to emerging technical standards brands are beginning to adopt proactively, particularly llms.txt, a machine-readable format (already used by Cloudflare, HubSpot, and Stripe) that structures product information specifically for AI-agent parsing, with early adopters reporting measurable increases in both AI-generated and organic traffic, and flag the likely future emergence of pay-to-play monetization models within AI-mediated marketplaces analogous to search-engine advertising, which will require brands to maintain visibility while navigating evolving transparency regulations around paid AI placement. <span class="src">Preparing Your Brand for Agentic AI</span>

## Open Questions

- None of the three sources reconciles a direct tension between the disclosure panel's finding (that AI use should be disclosed, especially for consequential decisions) and the agentic-brand-strategy research's finding (that brands should build "human-in-the-loop" and proprietary-data advantages into their agents to compete with independent consumer agents) — it's unclear whether aggressive AI-agent deployment for competitive reasons is compatible with the disclosure panel's ethical recommendation to go beyond minimum legal disclosure requirements.
- De Freitas's five pitfalls are grounded in controlled experimental studies (simulations, hypothetical scenarios, survey vignettes); none of the three sources addresses how reliably these lab-measured effect sizes translate to real, high-stakes brand crises in the wild, beyond the single Cruise case study used to motivate the research.
