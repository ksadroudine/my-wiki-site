## Summary

PwC's survey finding that people, not technology, hold back enterprise AI agent adoption, paired with Walmart's real-world response — consolidating dozens of disconnected agents into four unified "super agents" via the Model Context Protocol, once too much agent proliferation made its own tools confusing to use.

## Concept

PwC's May 2025 survey of 300 senior US executives paints a picture of enterprise AI agent adoption that is genuinely accelerating on the surface but still shallow underneath. Investment momentum is unmistakable: 88 percent of respondents say their team or business function plans to increase AI-related budgets specifically due to agentic AI, with more than a quarter planning increases of 26 percent or more; 79 percent report AI agents are already being adopted within their companies; and of those adopting, two-thirds report measurable value through increased productivity, with more than half also reporting cost savings, faster decision-making, or improved customer experience. But PwC is explicit that broad adoption doesn't equal deep impact — much of what's happening today is employees using agentic features built into existing enterprise applications to speed up routine tasks (surfacing insights, updating records, answering questions), a genuine and meaningful productivity boost that nonetheless stops well short of actual transformation. The gap between activity and impact shows up starkly in the numbers: 68 percent of companies report that half or fewer of their employees interact with agents in everyday work, and while 35 percent describe their agent adoption as "broad," only 17 percent report near-full adoption spanning almost all workflows and functions — a level of integration PwC contrasts with a leading hospitality company (Wyndham) already running coordinated teams of agents working across functions simultaneously to enhance both customer and employee experience, illustrating what genuine multi-agent integration actually looks like in practice rather than as an aspiration. Concern about falling behind is real and widespread — 46 percent of respondents worry their company may be losing ground to competitors on agent adoption — yet PwC finds few companies actually moving early to build the operating models needed to integrate and orchestrate multiple agents across vendors, functions, and business lines that would resolve that worry. <span class="src">PwC’s AI Agent Survey</span>

The survey's central and most consequential finding is that the barrier to deeper agentic AI adoption is overwhelmingly human, not technical. When PwC examined the top-ranked challenges executives cited — cybersecurity concerns and cost, each named by 34 percent of respondents — its own assessment is that these function mostly as safe, deflecting excuses: agents can genuinely be secured, and a well-designed implementation typically pays for itself within months. The challenges executives ranked lower on their own lists are, in PwC's view, the real obstacles: the ability to connect agents across applications and workflows (19 percent), organizational change readiness to keep pace with the technology (17 percent), and employee adoption itself (14 percent) — all rooted in organizational change rather than technology capability, pointing to a deeper truth that mindset, not the tools themselves, is where agentic AI's real opportunity and real barrier both live simultaneously. Workforce dynamics compound this: 67 percent of executives agree AI agents will drastically transform existing roles within the next 12 months, and 48 percent expect the shift to actually increase headcount rather than simply displace workers — meaning the human dimension of adoption isn't merely a resistance problem to overcome but an active redesign challenge leaders need to lead directly, rethinking strategy, redesigning workflows, and deliberately bringing employees into the process rather than treating adoption as something that happens to them. Trust compounds this challenge further, and drops sharply as the stakes of a given use case rise: executives report the highest trust in agents for data analysis (38 percent), performance improvement (35 percent), and daily collaboration alongside human team members (31 percent), but trust falls to just 20 percent for financial transactions and 22 percent for autonomous employee interactions — a gap PwC treats as decisive evidence that a responsible AI approach specifically addressing agent-specific risk isn't optional if organizations actually want to extend agentic deployment into genuinely high-stakes territory. Even among companies already using agents, the deeper structural changes that unlock the largest value remain rare: only 45 percent are fundamentally rethinking their operating model and only 42 percent are redesigning processes around agents, even though half agree their operating model will be effectively unrecognizable within two years, and just 44 percent are actively developing new agentic products or services — an area PwC explicitly flags as ripe for far more activity than it's currently seeing. Among the 18 percent of surveyed companies not using AI agents at all, the top cited reason is a lack of clear use cases or business value, which PwC characterizes bluntly as a failure of vision rather than a genuine technical or resource constraint, especially given real examples of success compounding quickly once started — one major retail company began by using agents to cut software-development cycle times and halve production errors, then rapidly expanded the same approach across HR, finance, supply chain, and marketing. PwC's resulting five-point playbook for leading with agents: get off the bench, since there's no remaining excuse for staying purely in experimentation or wait-and-see mode when near-term agentic value can directly fund the next stage of investment; rethink offense and defense together, since AI agents are actively eroding longstanding competitive advantages for incumbents even as they open genuine new-market entry opportunities for those who move decisively; focus on people specifically, since the technology itself is largely ready — what differentiates companies is deliberately reimagining work, the workforce, and individual worker roles rather than simply layering agents onto unchanged jobs; orchestrate and integrate, since deploying a few agents in isolation won't move the needle at all — genuinely agentic organizations require something like an operating system to coordinate multiple agents from multiple vendors across complex, cross-functional business processes; and design trust in from the very beginning, building agent strategy on a responsible AI foundation specifically tailored to the added risks autonomous, decision-making systems introduce, rather than retrofitting trust and governance after deployment. <span class="src">PwC’s AI Agent Survey</span>

Walmart's own experience illustrates almost exactly the orchestration challenge PwC's survey identifies as the quieter, harder-to-solve barrier beneath the more commonly cited excuses. Over recent months, Walmart built dozens of separate AI agents — enough that, in the words of chief technology and development officer Suresh Kumar, things started to get genuinely confusing for users, since each agent was typically accessed through its own distinct interface embedded in a different underlying system. Kumar frames the core usability problem directly: "if I have an agent that helps you with your payroll and I have a different agent that helps you with identifying merchandising trends, you shouldn't have to remember that and switch between those two." Rather than continuing to add agents piecemeal, Walmart stepped back and consolidated its entire agent portfolio into four unified "super agents," each surfaced through a single interface that internally taps the capabilities of numerous behind-the-scenes agents on the user's behalf: Sparky, the customer-facing super agent, is already live and continuing to expand its capabilities; Marty, the supplier-facing super agent, is expected to launch within a few months and will eventually handle functions like checking purchase analytics and initiating advertising campaigns; and separate employee-facing and engineering super agents are expected within the coming year, with the employee agent eventually able to take concrete actions like verifying an employee's discount-card eligibility and issuing a replacement directly. Kumar describes the shift as a natural evolution of a genuinely successful problem: Walmart found so many legitimate use cases for AI agents across the business, with buy-in from leadership at every level including CEO Doug McMillon, that proliferation itself became the thing that needed managing. Technically, the four super agents are connected using the Model Context Protocol (MCP), the open-source standard Anthropic introduced in November of the prior year, which lets each super agent's unified interface call out to the many smaller agents, internal applications, and data sources sitting behind it; because MCP wasn't yet widely established when Walmart first began building agents, part of the current consolidation effort involves retroactively bringing older, pre-MCP agents into conformance with the standard so they can participate in the unified experience. Walmart's own read on the payoff is significant: a simplified, unified interface is expected to meaningfully drive up adoption of its AI tools overall, precisely because having too many agents scattered across too many disparate locations was never intuitive for users in the first place — Kumar frames avoiding exactly that fragmentation as the explicit goal of the whole overhaul. The company is backing this architectural shift with substantial new leadership investment as well, hiring Instacart executive Daniel Danker as head of global AI acceleration, product, and design (reporting directly to CEO McMillon) and separately recruiting a dedicated AI platforms leader reporting to Kumar — with US division CEO John Furner framing the broader stakes in terms of both top-line growth (through more personalized, enticing shopping experiences) and bottom-line savings (through more efficient supply-chain and inventory management), in an industry where, he notes, the composition of the top ten retailers can change dramatically from one decade to the next. <span class="src">Exclusive Why Walmart Is Overhauling Its Approach to AI Agents</span>

## Open Questions

- The PwC survey doesn't report whether the 18% of companies not using AI agents at all differ systematically by industry or company size, which would help clarify whether "lack of clear use cases" reflects genuine industry variation or a broader failure of imagination as PwC suggests.
- Neither source addresses how Walmart is handling the transition period where some agents have been migrated to MCP and others haven't — what the user experience looks like for functionality still served by a pre-consolidation agent.
