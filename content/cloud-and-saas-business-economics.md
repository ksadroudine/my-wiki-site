## Summary

The cloud changed the economics of the technology industry in three ways. It shifted growth to a few leading providers, moved financial risk from customers to suppliers and made ongoing consumption, not the initial sale, the driver of revenue. The concept explains how established technology companies that were not built for the cloud can still capture growth by harvesting their older products, acquiring new capability and changing their sales channels. It describes the five customer segments that adopt cloud at different speeds and for different reasons, and a discipline called Consumption Development that closes the gap between the value a product could deliver and the value customers actually realize. It also explains how software-as-a-service performance is judged, through the Rule of 40, five operating benchmarks that distinguish companies that beat it and a growing set of alternative metrics that investors must read critically.

## Concept

The economics of the cloud market can be read from three vantage points: how the market grew and who captured the growth, how vendors should target customers who move at different speeds, and how the move to consumption-based pricing changes what a technology company must do to earn revenue. Bain's January 2019 sizing of the shift projected that cloud would represent 60 percent of all global IT industry growth through 2021, split across public cloud infrastructure and enabling services (9 percent annual growth, reaching $127 billion), infrastructure and platform as a service (29 percent, $117 billion) and SaaS (16 percent, $159 billion). The tailwind was captured overwhelmingly by the leading providers, with AWS growing more than 40 percent a quarter and delivering over half of Amazon's operating income by the third quarter of 2018 while Azure grew 76 percent in the same quarter. <span class="src">How Technology Incumbents Can Realize Their Cloud Growth Ambitions</span>

The flip side was a difficult period for incumbents outside the leading cloud vendors. From 2013 to 2017 every legacy technology category except three underperformed growth expectations, with high-end enterprise servers declining 11 percent against a forecast decline of 3 percent, so the shift was substantially cannibalizing incumbent categories instead of merely supplementing them. The one bright spot, midrange and volume servers, buoyed partly by white-box manufacturers and partly by a resurgence in branded gear from Dell, HP and Lenovo, offered a transferable playbook. Incumbents should harvest cash flow from legacy franchises specifically to fund new hybrid and multicloud offerings rather than just defending the old business, use acquisitions to obtain capability in fast-growing cloud segments where they have little organic exposure, and evolve the sales and channel model to match how customers now buy and consume cloud services, rather than selling cloud-adjacent products through a motion built for on-premises deals. Bain's closing warning is that the shift is structural and not cyclical, so incumbents that read short-term stability as safety are setting up a later reckoning. <span class="src">How Technology Incumbents Can Realize Their Cloud Growth Ambitions</span>

Bain's second installment narrows from market sizing to customer targeting with a five-segment taxonomy first developed in 2011 and revisited with 2019 data. Transformational customers, the early adopters that set the market's pace, prioritize performance and scalability over cost, but some are finding a ceiling, since a predictable workload running at 80 percent utilization increasingly favors on-premises economics over pay-as-you-go. Heterogeneous customers, typically mature businesses with forward-looking but risk-aware IT leadership, deliberately split workloads across SaaS, infrastructure and platform services and retained on-premises systems based on fit instead of pursuing wholesale migration. Safety Conscious customers have real demand but have been held back by private-cloud offerings too costly or complex to meet their need for a dedicated, non-shared environment, a segment vendors could unlock by improving private and public cloud interoperability. Price Conscious customers have been most sophisticated at capturing savings, because they treat migration as a chance to audit and rightsize computing and storage, uncovering fragmentation and duplication, and Bain's client work found rightsizing can cut costs by 30 to 60 percent, a gain lift-and-shift migrations reliably miss. Slow and Steady customers waited out the early-adopter phase and commit over three to five years, and were projected to become the largest segment by 2022, accounting for half of spending growth, which makes them the most important segment for a vendor seeking growth. Together with the sizing analysis, the segmentation gives incumbents the discipline for executing their playbook: a rising tide does not lift all boats evenly, so success depends on choosing which face of the cloud to serve rather than riding overall growth. <span class="src">Revisiting the Five Faces of the Cloud</span>

The vendor's problem runs deeper than targeting, because the move to cloud changed who bears risk. J.B. Wood, Todd Hewlin and Thomas Lah argue that three roughly simultaneous shifts, the 2008 to 2009 recession, cloud computing and the smartphone, permanently changed how technology companies must generate revenue by moving financial risk in a sale from customer to supplier. Under on-premises licensing the customer paid upfront and bore the risk that the product would go underused, whereas under the cloud and SaaS model of no usage, no money, the supplier earns only as the customer actually consumes the product, so driving ongoing usage becomes existential. This is framed against the Consumption Gap, the growing distance between the value a product could deliver and the business value customers realize, which arises because vendors can add features and complexity faster than customers can absorb them. Revenue is built from many small micro-transactions, such as an added user, an unlocked feature or extra capacity, so growth depends on three behaviors, namely getting existing users to adopt more advanced capability, getting new users onto an application and getting existing accounts to adopt additional applications. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>

No single department can own consumption, since a company-wide capability is required, but the authors identify customer support and service as the best-positioned existing asset, because it already has the lowest per-interaction cost of any customer-facing function, global infrastructure, deep product knowledge and ownership of the self-service channels through which consumption guidance would flow. Their proposed Account Services Organization blends that service capability with inside-sales responsibilities focused on recognizing and acting on consumption opportunities. On the development side, Consumption Development has three components. Intelligent Listening replaces upfront market-requirements documents with real-time usage data from products deliberately over-instrumented with listening posts, on the logic that what customers do inside a product is a more reliable signal of what to build than what they say they want. Consumption Innovation shifts investment away from the traditional speeds-and-feeds race of adding features and toward simplifying the product and guiding individual users along a Consumption Roadmap, a tiered presentation of features similar to how games reveal capability as a player shows readiness. In-Product Upsell embeds recommendation engines and offer management inside the product to surface the next relevant feature, content or purchase in real time, modeled on e-commerce patterns such as customers who bought this also bought. The design guidance is a 10:1 rule, ten purely helpful no-revenue offers for every revenue-generating one, because consistently helpful guidance builds the trust that makes a later commercial offer more likely to be accepted. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>

The same source cites Don Norman to explain why feature overshoot persists even though it is understood: customers in every segment keep requesting features specific to their own use, competitors keep shipping capabilities that create pressure to match, and engineers want to build visibly impressive functionality regardless of whether it serves most users. Unused features are not harmless, because they clutter the interface, add cost across the product's life through installation, customization, maintenance and support, and slow adoption of the smaller set of features that drive most of the realized value. The wider shift from selling products to selling outcomes is described elsewhere as a move from business-to-business to business-for-business pricing, in which vendors price for the customer's realized outcome rather than for the transfer of a product or license, which structurally requires the same ongoing, usage-driving engagement that Consumption Development provides, because a vendor paid for outcomes has a direct stake in whether the customer consumes and benefits from the product. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span> <span class="src">B4B</span>

Investors judge this new model with the Rule of 40, the principle that a software company's growth rate plus profit margin should exceed 40 percent, and Bain finds the market rewards it decisively, since companies beating the threshold are on average three times more valuable in enterprise value to revenue. Bain and OPEXEngine analyzed 2019 financial data for nearly 80 SaaS companies and found that those beating the rule scored consistently higher on five operating benchmarks. The most important was employee retention, because customer satisfaction correlates with employee satisfaction and leaders protect both, a point Bain noted needed to account for pandemic-era changes in staffing, workspace, travel and safety. Second was efficient sales and marketing spend, tracked through the magic number, the ratio of revenue growth to sales and marketing spend, which measures whether the sales organization is geared to growth and not merely to maintenance. Third was a better ratio of customer lifetime value to acquisition cost, reflecting good targeting and products that sustain value, with digital investment stripping acquisition cost and digital-native SaaS companies holding a structural edge over incumbents. Fourth was disciplined operating expense, with efficient product development, ruthless culling of weak pipeline items to concentrate on the most promising ones, productive sales teams, contained administrative costs and continued investment in customer success. Fifth was efficient growth from the existing base, meaning more new revenue from upgrades and cross-selling and better limits on churn than peers. In the context of the early pandemic, when most enterprise IT budgets were being cut but many buyers planned to raise SaaS spending, particularly in security and collaboration, Bain judged that companies already strong on these benchmarks were positioned to sustain growth. <span class="src">The Five Habits of Highly Successful Software Companies</span>

Barron's widens the lens from the Rule of 40 to the vocabulary of alternative metrics cloud companies use about themselves, which investors must read critically. The article opens with venture investor Bill Gurley's 1998 warning that alternative performance measures let companies produce impressive-looking revenue by effectively selling dollar bills for 85 cents, aimed even at the now-standard price-to-sales ratio, which proved prescient before the dot-com crash, yet complex metrics never disappeared and multiplied as cloud companies grew more sophisticated. It defines the terms investors most need to decode. Annual recurring revenue is useful for companies that blend subscription and term contracts because it smooths up-front recognition on term deals. Remaining performance obligations are contracted work not yet recognized, a forward-looking signal distinct from recurring revenue. Net retention rate is a same-store-sales-style measure of revenue growth per existing customer, with a high figure, such as the 168 percent of one profiled company, signaling that existing customers want more. Contribution is transaction-level profitability before fixed costs, sometimes paired with a take rate for marketplaces. Adjusted EBITDA strips out stock-based compensation and one-time items, and total addressable market is the most marketing-oriented and the easiest to inflate. Recurring revenue and net retention are also the inputs to the growth-rate calculations behind the Rule of 40, so this vocabulary is needed before the five benchmarks can be applied. <span class="src">The Cloud Is Already Confusing—the Metrics Make It Worse</span>

## Open Questions

- The Bain vendor-strategy and segmentation pieces date from 2019 and the Rule of 40 research from 2020, so they predate the acceleration of cloud adoption during the pandemic, and the sources do not show how the five-segment taxonomy or the growth sizing held up through it.
- The sources recommend that vendors focus on one customer segment but do not discuss the trade-offs of serving several segments at once.
- The five operating benchmarks, especially the magic number and the ratio of lifetime value to acquisition cost, are built around per-seat subscription economics, and it is unclear whether they remain as predictive once consumption-based, AI-driven pricing becomes widespread or whether a revised set will be needed.
- The underlying 2019 dataset of nearly 80 SaaS companies is not broken out by company, so the findings cannot be independently verified from the article, and the 2021 Barron's glossary does not rank the metrics by how easily each can be presented misleadingly.
