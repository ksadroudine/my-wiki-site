## Summary

BCG's "Five Promises of Personalization" framework — Empower Me, Know Me, Reach Me, Show Me, Delight Me — for building competitive advantage through 1:1 customer experiences at scale. Covers the data, technology, organizational, and risk-management foundations it requires, an earlier 2022 articulation of the same authors' thinking as "intelligent experience engines," a companion "privileged insights" strategic rationale for why proprietary customer data matters, the scored Personalization Index and named company examples (Spotify, Cisco, DoorDash) from the authors' HBR companion article, and a further companion piece distilling three ways AI-driven companies operate differently.

## Highlights

- Competitive advantage from personalization is modeled as P = n × v², where n is the volume of customer interactions a company can learn from and v is the speed of learning — doubling the number of experiments is said to more than double the quality of the resulting learning, giving speed an exponential rather than linear payoff. <span class="src">Personalized</span>
- The framework rests on five sequential "Promises of Personalization": Empower Me (put the customer in control of achieving their goal), Know Me (build a real, individual-level understanding of the customer), Reach Me (contact the right customer, in the right channel, at the right moment — and know when not to), Show Me (deliver tailored content that matches what the customer expects the company to know), and Delight Me (continuously test and refine so the experience keeps improving). <span class="src">Personalized</span>
- Customer data is layered by provenance: zero-party data (explicitly given by the customer), first-party data (inferred from the customer's own behavior, owned directly by the company), second-party data (shared by a trusted partner under the customer's permission, e.g. a co-branded credit card), and third-party data (purchased or licensed from outside sources, e.g. ad platforms or data marketplaces) — with first-party and loyalty-program data singled out as the highest-value sources because loyalty members are typically 2-3x more engaged than average customers. <span class="src">Personalized</span>
- Reaching customers effectively depends on three "intelligence" components working together: targeting intelligence (propensity, affinity, and headroom models plus trigger events that decide *when* to act), experiment design and activation (rules-based, automated matching of experiences to customers, ideally tested via parallel multivariate experiments rather than sequential A/B tests), and next best action orchestration (cross-channel "air traffic control" that prioritizes and sequences messages, and suppresses conflicting ones, e.g. pausing marketing messages during an active service issue). <span class="src">Personalized</span>
- Content strategy has evolved from producing one finished asset at a time toward modular, template-based content creation — reusable blocks assembled and tagged as data, increasingly generated and varied at scale by generative AI, up to "atomic" content that updates itself in real time even after a message has already been sent. <span class="src">Personalized</span>
- Organizationally, the most successful AI transformations follow a "70/20/10 rule": roughly 70% of the effort goes into people (processes, incentives, ways of working), 20% into getting the data right, and only 10% into the technology itself — and reorganizing the company's structure is explicitly warned against as a first step, since it's better to start with small, cross-functional, self-governing agile "pods" running two-week sprints against a clear use case. <span class="src">Personalized</span>
- Success is measured through three core metrics — customer interaction engagement, net incremental sales lift (measured against a universal control group of customers who receive only mass, non-personalized communications, to avoid double-counting overlapping campaign effects), and long-term customer lifetime value — all of which need near-real-time measurement, since waiting weeks for data breaks the learning cycle. <span class="src">Personalized</span>
- Personalization carries specific, named risks mapped back to each of the Five Promises — including inappropriate/mismanaged data sharing, AI targeting or content bias, copyright infringement in gen-AI-produced content, and unmonitored automated outreach — with mitigations spanning clear-language consent design, chief privacy/data officer roles, embedded rules engines, red-team testing for edge cases, and content-source audit trails (e.g. tying gen AI output to a company's own content via embeddings for source verification). <span class="src">Personalized</span>

- The Personalization Index turns the Five Promises into a scored 0-100 self-assessment (Empower Me worth 50 points as the most heavily weighted promise, Know Me/Reach Me/Show Me/Delight Me worth 10 points each, plus a final 10 points for having a senior leader with P&L accountability for personalization), built from 200 marketer interviews, mystery-shopping across companies, and a financial analysis of personalization's impact on growth and shareholder returns. <span class="src">Personalization Done Right</span>
- Companies score, on average, only 49 out of 100 across 12 industry segments studied (with the top decile averaging 72), and two-thirds of consumers in a BCG survey of 5,000 report a recent personalized recommendation that felt inappropriate, inaccurate, or invasive — even though more than 80% say they want and expect personalized experiences, and 85% of C-suite executives plan to increase 2024 AI spending, mostly on cost-saving rather than personalization. <span class="src">Personalization Done Right</span>
- Personalization Index leaders grow 10 percentage points faster than laggards and 6 points faster than the average, with 30% higher customer spend and 3x the pre- and post-purchase engagement of competitors' customers; every 10-point rise in Index score correlates with roughly a 7-point rise in Net Promoter Score, and $1 invested in a personalization leader five years ago would be worth $3 versus $0.50 for the same investment in a laggard — implying a $2 trillion cross-industry prize for personalization leaders. <span class="src">Personalization Done Right</span>
- Named examples illustrate each promise in practice: SonderMind's mental-wellness app personalizes patient treatment journeys from a 12-brain-function assessment (Empower Me); Spotify (600M+ users) and newcomer Sweetgreen (60% of 2023 sales through digital channels) both built deep first-party digital relationships despite very different scale (Know Me); Cisco combines product-use, service, marketing, and external financial-news data to tell its B2B sales team whom to contact and when (Reach Me); jewelry brand Pandora used AI-generated, per-customer content to cut certain content-creation cycle times from 12-14 months to 10 days (Show Me); and DoorDash runs hundreds of app micro-experiments continuously (Delight Me) — while Voya Financial's My Voyage app shows the promises working together, unifying customers' full financial picture and using an experimentation "test engine" to drive double-digit revenue growth. <span class="src">Personalization Done Right</span>
- Three cross-cutting lessons separate personalization leaders from laggards: rethinking leadership roles so the mandate comes explicitly from the CEO with every C-suite function (CFO, CDO, CMO, CDAO, COO, general counsel, CIO) holding a specific accountability; securing the foundation by using the Index to prioritize gap-closing investment and favoring smart integration of existing tools over building everything internally (personalization can often be largely self-funding once early wins prove the business case); and competing on speed, since leaders learn roughly 10 times faster than laggards, compressing 8-12-week campaign cycles down to three to five days. <span class="src">Personalization Done Right</span>

## Concept

The book's central thesis is that personalization functions less like a single marketing tactic and more like a compounding source of competitive advantage, on the model of BCG's long-standing "time-based competition" concept: value grows as a function of the volume of customer interactions a company can learn from (n) multiplied by the square of the speed at which it learns from them (v²), because faster experimentation compounds rather than simply accumulates. This "personalization flywheel" is what makes a mature personalization capability hard for competitors to replicate, since it is built from years of accumulated interaction data and learning velocity rather than a copyable point solution. <span class="src">Personalized</span>

The framework organizes personalization into five sequential promises. Empower Me is described as the most important: using everything known about a customer to help them achieve a specific goal — educating them, helping them find or fix something, or rewarding them — in whichever channel and moment they choose, which requires the company to first identify which specific parts of the customer journey are worth personalizing at all rather than attempting to personalize everything at once. Know Me is foundational to the rest: it depends on collecting and structuring customer data across a hierarchy of sources by provenance — zero-party data volunteered directly by the customer, first-party data inferred from the customer's own recorded behavior and owned outright by the company, second-party data obtained from a trusted partner under the customer's original permission (a co-branded credit card is the given example), and third-party data purchased or licensed from outside platforms and data marketplaces. Loyalty program data is highlighted as unusually valuable first-party data because loyalty members tend to be 2-3x more engaged than the average customer and generate ongoing engagement signals simply through checking their status. Managing this data well requires three disciplines working together: a data strategy that starts from target use cases rather than available technology, a customer data platform (CDP) that stitches disparate data sources into one view of each customer (increasingly via AI-driven probabilistic identity matching), and data governance — typically owned by a chief data or privacy officer — that defines shared data terminology, audits data quality and provenance, and enforces "red line" limits on what data may never be used for. <span class="src">Personalized</span>

Reach Me converts that customer knowledge into timely, relevant contact, built on three "intelligence" components: targeting intelligence (trigger events like an abandoned cart, combined with propensity, affinity, and headroom models, decide *when* an experience should be delivered), experiment design and activation (a rules layer — exclusion rules, frequency caps, business rules — automates which customers become eligible for which experiences, and personalization leaders run many multivariate experiments in parallel rather than sequential simple A/B tests), and next best action orchestration (a cross-channel "air traffic control" layer, sometimes implemented as a "state machine" that tracks each customer's progress through a multi-step journey, that prioritizes and sequences messages across channels and actively suppresses conflicting ones — e.g. pausing a scheduled promotional message during an active customer service incident). Show Me is about the content layer itself, which the book argues has shifted from producing one finished asset per interaction (an approach too slow and limited in variation to power personalization at scale) toward modular content: reusable templated components, tagged as searchable metadata and increasingly generated and varied by generative AI tools (Adobe Firefly, Jasper, and similar are cited), up to "atomic content" that can update itself using real-time data even after an email or message has already been sent to the customer. Delight Me is the compounding layer that makes the other four promises feel effortless over time, powered by a measurement feedback loop that gives agile teams the data to make weekly or faster improvements — success here is explicitly said to hinge on people and process more than on technology, formalized as the book's "70/20/10 rule": roughly 70% of effort in a successful AI/personalization transformation goes into people (processes, ways of working, incentives), 20% into getting the underlying data right, and only 10% into the technology stack itself. A specific organizational warning follows from this: companies should not start a personalization effort by redesigning their org chart, since a reorganization is a slow, complex undertaking that makes little sense before the operational requirements of personalization are even understood; instead, the recommended starting point is small, cross-functional, self-governing "pods" with clear goals and decision rights, running agile-style two-week sprints against a narrow, high-visibility use case, only evolving toward centralized functional structures as the program scales. <span class="src">Personalized</span>

Measuring success rests on three core metrics: customer interaction engagement (open rates, conversion, browse behavior, and similar, ideally broken out by customer type and channel rather than averaged), net incremental sales lift (the harder metric to calculate, because simply summing the incremental revenue attributed to multiple concurrent personalization campaigns double-counts overlapping effects — best-in-class companies instead maintain a single universal control group that receives only non-personalized mass communications, withheld from personalization entirely, as a clean baseline), and long-term customer lifetime value. All three need to be measured close to real time, since the "learning cycle" that drives competitive advantage is a function of the number of experiments run, multiplied by how quickly they can be measured, plus how quickly the resulting insight can be acted on — teams that wait weeks for data effectively break their own learning loop. <span class="src">Personalized</span>

Finally, the book maps specific personalization risks back onto each of the five promises, alongside concrete mitigations. Under Know Me: lack of clear, plain-language consent, and mishandled or "stranded" data spread inconsistently across the enterprise, addressed through elevated chief privacy/data officer roles, clear metadata tagging of what data may be used for what purpose, and giving customers visibility into and control over their own data. Under Reach Me: inappropriate targeting and message bombardment across teams, addressed through embedded rules engines with auditable dashboards and cross-team orchestration, alongside human review of automated targeting to catch edge cases. Under Show Me: outdated or hard-to-update content, copyright exposure from generative AI outputs, and content bias, addressed through templated/modular content with defined expiration and refresh rules, tying gen-AI-produced content back to a company's own licensed content library via embeddings for source verification and audit trails, and pre-publication human review of AI-generated content batches. Under Delight Me: unintended or harmful edge-case outputs, addressed through dedicated "red team" testing that deliberately surfaces unusual scenarios (e.g. customers with atypical service-contact patterns) to find and patch guardrails before they cause harm in production. <span class="src">Personalized</span>

A companion HBR article by the same authors, adapted from the same book, operationalizes the Five Promises into a scored diagnostic — the Personalization Index — built from 200 marketer interviews, mystery-shopping observation of how companies actually deliver experiences across channels, and a financial analysis linking personalization maturity to growth and shareholder returns. The Index runs 0 to 100 and weights the five promises unevenly: Empower Me alone is worth 50 of the 100 points, reflecting its status as the most important promise, while Know Me, Reach Me, Show Me, and Delight Me are worth 10 points each, and a final 10 points go to whether the organization has a senior leader with real P&L accountability and a cross-functional mandate for personalization — a factor the authors found, across the large companies they studied, often makes the difference between success and failure on its own. The gap between aspiration and delivery is stark: across 12 industry segments, companies score only 49 out of 100 on average (with the top decile averaging 72), and in a BCG survey of 5,000 global consumers, two-thirds report a recent personalized recommendation that felt inappropriate, inaccurate, or invasive, even though more than 80 percent say they want and expect personalization — a gap the authors attribute to companies lacking a clear standard for what "good" personalization actually looks like, not to any lack of consumer appetite for it. This gap represents a missed opportunity at scale: 85 percent of C-suite executives surveyed planned to increase 2024 AI spending, but mostly on cost-saving initiatives rather than personalization, which the authors argue is the mistake, since Personalization Index leaders grow 10 percentage points faster than laggards and 6 points faster than the average, driven by 30 percent higher customer spend and triple the pre- and post-purchase engagement — with every 10-point rise in Index score correlating with roughly a 7-point rise in Net Promoter Score, and $1 invested in a personalization leader five years ago worth $3 today versus $0.50 for the same investment in a laggard, implying a $2 trillion prize waiting to be captured across industries. Concrete examples illustrate leadership on each promise: SonderMind's mental-wellness app personalizes patient treatment journeys from an interactive 12-brain-function assessment (Empower Me); Spotify's 600 million-plus users and newcomer Sweetgreen's 60 percent digital-channel sales share (as of 2023) both demonstrate deep first-party digital relationships despite vastly different company scale and age (Know Me); Cisco combines product-use, service-request, marketing-interaction, and even external financial-news data to tell its B2B sales team exactly whom to contact, when, and about what (Reach Me); jewelry brand Pandora used AI-generated, individually tailored imagery to cut certain content-creation cycle times from 12-14 months down to 10 days (Show Me); and DoorDash runs hundreds of continuous in-app micro-experiments, such as testing add-to-cart and product-substitution recommendations (Delight Me). Voya Financial's My Voyage app shows all five promises working in concert: by unifying a customer's full financial picture (retirement, personal savings, healthcare benefits) into one app and building a "test engine" to relentlessly experiment on which nudges actually help members (like increasing retirement contributions), Voya grew accounts and drove double-digit revenue growth while earning recognition as one of Newsweek's "Most Trusted Financial Services" brands and one of Ethisphere's "World's Most Ethical Companies." The article distills three cross-cutting lessons that separate leaders from laggards, echoing but sharpening the book's own organizational guidance: rethinking leadership roles so the mandate for personalization comes explicitly from the CEO, with every relevant C-suite function (CFO understanding the business case, CDO owning the digital experience, CMO instilling agile marketing practices, CDAO building the data platforms, COO redesigning delivery processes, general counsel championing responsible AI principles, CIO prioritizing the right technology investments) holding specific, named accountability rather than treating personalization as a side project of the marketing department alone; securing the foundation by using the Index itself to identify capability gaps, tying the business case and required investment to each gap directly, and favoring smart integration of existing tools over building everything internally, since personalization programs can often become largely self-funding once early wins prove out; and competing on speed, since Index leaders learn roughly ten times faster than laggards, compressing traditional 8-to-12-week campaign-development cycles down to three to five days — with the authors citing a large U.S. bank that failed at cross-functional agility once (by creating yet another organizational silo) before succeeding on a second attempt with an empowered, budget-holding cross-functional pod that cut its campaign cycle from 12 weeks to 3 days and, in doing so, cut credit-card churn in half. <span class="src">Personalization Done Right</span>

David Edelman's earlier 2022 HBR article, written years before the book and "Personalization Done Right" formalized the Five Promises, shows the same underlying thinking already in place under a different name: "intelligent experience engines." The article identifies five pivotal practices — connecting data signals into a 360-degree customer view, reimagining the end-to-end journey as a seamless flow, activating experiences across channels, fulfilling according to customer context, and testing relentlessly — that map closely onto Know Me, Empower Me, Reach Me, Show Me, and Delight Me respectively, and it already states the 70/20/10 people/data/technology ratio that reappears unchanged in the later book. Brinks Home, a struggling smart-home brand competing against ADT, Google Nest, and Ring with only 2% market share, illustrates the framework's payoff: working with AI start-up OfferFit, it scaled A/B testing from two or three tests a day to roughly 50,000, increasing average direct-to-consumer package size from $489 to $968 and overall revenue by 9.5% within a year. Further named examples deepen each practice: lululemon builds its 360-degree customer view from email capture at purchase, in-store yoga sign-ups, clickstream browsing data, and (after acquiring Mirror) in-home workout behavior; Qantas reimagines the end-to-end journey by extending personalization beyond flights into a cross-category loyalty ecosystem with partners like Woolworths, Hilton, and Avis, plus a health-insurance app that rewards healthy habits with loyalty points; Starbucks and the much smaller sweetgreen (140 stores versus Starbucks's 33,000-plus) both activate experiences across channels, with sweetgreen's app-driven model actually surpassing Starbucks's percentage of digital sales; Kroger's and Tesco's in-house analytics arms (84.51° and dunnhumby) fulfill according to customer context by running hundreds of propensity models to target promotions; and Stitch Fix tests relentlessly, running hundreds of experiments a month while expecting a third to fail, feeding the results directly back into its recommendation engine. <span class="src">Customer Experience in the Age of AI</span>

A companion piece by a different set of PwC "Beyond Digital" researchers supplies a strategic rationale for why the Know Me layer's emphasis on proprietary, trust-based customer data matters so much competitively. <span class="src">How to Gain a Competitive Advantage on Customer Insights</span> argues that most of the customer data companies spend billions of dollars a year acquiring — market research, purchased data, generic big-data analysis — is also available to competitors and therefore does not actually differentiate a company from its rivals. The alternative is a "system of privileged insights": unique, trust-built information about customer needs, desires, and experiences that only one company has access to, built through three linked practices — establishing a genuine foundation of trust and value (Adobe's explicit refusal to use customer data for "tactical advantage" over customer benefit), integrating insight collection into everyday customer touchpoints rather than running it as a separate research process (Zara/Inditex trains retail employees as a frontline sensing network, feeding real-time style and inventory signals from more than 700 designers' worth of store-level observation directly to headquarters, driving more than 60,000 new garment designs a year), and wiring the resulting insights into day-to-day operations, incentives, and metrics rather than treating them as inputs to innovation alone (Salesforce's customer-success-driven product development). The system is explicitly framed as a self-reinforcing flywheel — more trust drives more customer engagement, which yields more insight, which improves the experience, which builds further trust — directly reinforcing this concept's claim that a mature personalization capability compounds over time (n × v²) rather than being a copyable point solution. <span class="src">How to Gain a Competitive Advantage on Customer Insights</span>

<span class="src">What Sets AI-Driven Companies Apart</span> (HBR, November 2024), drawn from the same "Personalized" book as this concept's foundational sources, addresses a gap the Five Promises framework leaves implicit: given that only 10% of companies have mastered scaling generative AI (per BCG research) despite 70% of workers reporting genuine enthusiasm for it, what organizationally distinguishes the minority that succeeds? The article's answer is three specific operating differences rather than a talent or technology gap. AI-driven companies embed AI directly into strategic priorities, taking a portfolio approach that prioritizes real customer problems worth solving rather than pursuing efficiency gains alone — one large consumer products company deliberately used AI-driven efficiency savings to fund customer-value investments rather than dropping them straight to the bottom line, explicitly to signal to employees that AI was a growth lever rather than a pretext for headcount cuts. They break down functional silos and involve employees directly in shaping solutions, organizing around small, nimble "two-pizza" cross-functional pods (business, analytics, tech, and operations) that iterate quickly against well-defined KPIs — the same 10% of companies leading on generative AI, per the authors' own CMO survey and personalization-index research, are also furthest ahead in scaling predictive AI, because they had already built the operational and data foundation this concept's Know Me layer describes. And they use AI to improve experience rather than chase efficiency alone: a large convenience retailer's AI scheduling tool boosted employee satisfaction and lowered turnover (not just productivity) by matching store associates' shift preferences, and was consequently rolled out nationally in six months versus the two-plus years earlier scheduling-tool rollouts had typically required, precisely because front-line teams could see they personally benefited rather than merely absorbing a cost-cutting mandate. <span class="src">What Sets AI-Driven Companies Apart</span>

## Open Questions

- The book's examples and case studies (H&M, Spotify, various retailers and telcos) are not independently verified in this source; figures like specific incremental-revenue outcomes are presented as the authors' consulting-engagement experience rather than externally audited results.
- Neither the 2022 nor 2024/2025 sources explain why the 70/20/10 ratio and the underlying five-practice structure persisted essentially unchanged across roughly three years of the same authors' work, despite the intervening acceleration in generative AI capability — it's unclear whether this reflects genuine framework stability or insufficient updating.
- "What Sets AI-Driven Companies Apart" doesn't specify how the 10% figure for gen AI scaling success was measured or what threshold separates a company that has "mastered scaling" from one that hasn't, making it hard to independently verify how the featured companies were selected.
