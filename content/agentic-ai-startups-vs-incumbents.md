## Summary

Agentic AI is compressing the cost and time of building a company, which lets small AI-native startups attack incumbents whose organizations, data and workflows were designed for stability and control. Incumbents keep real advantages in trust, compliance infrastructure, specialized talent and ownership of the system of record, but a16z argues the customer's job is bigger than the record, leaving room for vertical startups that learn a profession through manufactured learning loops, and consulting shows the disruption already reaching billing and staffing models. A six-type taxonomy of AI startups, from originators and explorers to enhancers, optimizers and experimenters, helps founders, investors and corporate buyers judge which kind of company they are dealing with and where its defensibility lies.

## Concept

HBR's research across dozens of AI-native startups describes a second great compression of entrepreneurship, comparable to the internet-era compression and driven by five mutually reinforcing forces. Zero-latency iteration means a product can change instantly on customer feedback, and where a SaaS startup once needed 12-18 months and USD 1 million or more to find product-market fit, AI-assisted vibe coding now yields working prototypes in days, with agents also critiquing architecture and running security and performance tests, so that at one company UX designers modify interface code through an AI assistant without engineers and ship updates every two weeks. Automated go-to-market lets startups run marketing and acquisition once available only to well-funded enterprises, with agents testing thousands of image and copy combinations, shifting spend to the best channels and handling onboarding tasks that took months, so that one startup gives presale demos on a prospect's own data within hours and completes implementations in about a quarter of the usual time. <span class="src">How Agentic AI Supercharges Startups and Threatens Incumbents</span>

Autonomous business functions shrink the headcount needed, since a conventional product team of six to eight people takes six to twelve months to build a minimum viable product while an AI-native startup can start with a domain expert and an AI engineer who, with AI coding tools, performs the work of ten. A restaurant-decision-intelligence startup built an agentic system from one engineer and one domain expert in months, and a health-insurance prior-authorization startup cut legal expenses about 90% with contract-review agents, freeing 30 of its 40 employees to work on the product. Capital efficiency follows, as one startup condensed a typical two-year Series A path to ten months and a venture studio's twelve AI-native startups reached Series A on 80% less capital and 20-40% less time, letting investors make more bets per dollar. The AI-driven flywheel compounds the rest, since solving unsolvable manual tasks at the center of a customer's business yields proprietary workflow knowledge that generic agent platforms cannot replicate, as when a startup built what its founder called the best medical-record ingestion engine by structuring 600-page patient files, and deep integration then creates the switching costs that once protected SaaS incumbents. <span class="src">How Agentic AI Supercharges Startups and Threatens Incumbents</span>

HBR treats the resulting threat as structural. Startups form around clean workflows, tight learning loops and data models built for continuous learning, while most incumbents evolved for stability, specialization and control, and middle managers whose authority rests on controlling information often resist threats to their span of control, because the roles were designed for a different operating logic, in line with Christensen's warning that the processes serving a core market can blind a company to other ways of operating. Technical debt makes it worse, since enterprise workflows are poorly documented, data sits in silos, integrations are brittle and exceptions need manual intervention, which are conditions agents need resolved rather than resolve. HBR therefore warns, echoing Hammer's 1990 advice to obliterate rather than automate, that the most common incumbent mistake is automating broken workflows before re-architecting them. <span class="src">How Agentic AI Supercharges Startups and Threatens Incumbents</span>

HBR is equally clear that AI-native startups are vulnerable. Autonomous systems introduce quality, reliability and compliance risk, since a misread signal or faulty configuration can have real-world consequences and agents, unlike deterministic software, do not reliably produce the same output twice, and startups must build the testing, monitoring, audit-trail and incident-response infrastructure that incumbents spent decades refining before customers will extend equal trust. Talent is a further constraint because agentic AI increases the need for top employees with deep industry knowledge, who are concentrated at large established firms. The recommended incumbent response is to audit vulnerability against the five forces and identify which high-volume, high-handoff workflows a rival would attack first, to start with a bounded, high-friction process rather than an easy pilot, as when an IT-services firm cut an RFP response requiring hundreds of personnel-hours to a 20-minute first draft and raised both volume and win rate, to re-architect before automating, to consider partnering with AI-native ventures to see a legacy-free workflow, to improve data quality and make human handoffs explicit, and to prepare people for roles centered on judgment, edge cases and oversight of agents. <span class="src">How Agentic AI Supercharges Startups and Threatens Incumbents</span>

A16z maps where in this contest incumbents or startups hold the advantage through its agent hierarchy of four application-level agents distinguished by the autonomy and judgment each needs. Retrieval assistants find information, summarize documents and draft responses, process agents do rule-based work such as updating records or routing approvals, policy agents apply an organization's playbooks, precedents and thresholds to ambiguous cases, as Docusign's Iris applies a legal team's review playbook, and principal agents make open-ended judgment calls on strategic tradeoffs that no organization has yet handed to AI. Most incumbent AI products, little more than chatbots and retrieval tools a year earlier, are moving toward narrow policy application, but their judgment is bounded by the record their system owns, which is where the central argument turns, that the job is bigger than the record. Real work crosses applications, teams and companies, and a signed contract is not the legal matter, a closed ticket is not the customer's resolution and a CRM opportunity is not the sale. <span class="src">The Incumbents Are Coming</span>

Salesforce's Claudeforce, which lets users work with Salesforce CRM data entirely from inside Claude, illustrates the unbundling, since Claude becomes the front door to the job while Salesforce still owns the data and the actions on it, which opens a chance for AI-native challengers to rebundle around the full cross-system job. A16z notes limits to access-based solutions, because even MCP-based access across applications adds latency, applications describe the same customer, contract or transaction with inconsistent definitions, and a general agent cannot see information held outside a company's own systems, such as a supplier's records. The way vertical AI closes the gap is a genuine learning loop rather than memory alone, since frontier labs are building general memory that lets agents recall preferences or last week's decision but memory does not say whether past work was good, why an expert corrected it or what to change. The loop combines the model, a domain-specific harness of context, tools, workflows and evaluation criteria, and iterative feedback from expert corrections and outcomes, and it compounds two kinds of learning, the general standards of a profession, which benefit every customer, and one firm's templates, precedents, risk thresholds and escalation rules, which benefit mainly that customer. <span class="src">The Incumbents Are Coming</span>

A startup does not need a large stock of historical customer data to build this loop, because it can manufacture a professional-level curriculum first and learn institution-specific practice from live deployment. Harvey post-trained an open-weight model for Tenet with synthetic, public and expert-created data and no customer data, building about 1,750 legal-task environments, each simulating a partner-assigned matter with documents, tools and an expert rubric of about 50 quality criteria. A16z's four-question test for a strong vertical market asks whether an expert can quickly judge what the AI got right or wrong and explain how to improve it, whether the work is hard enough that judgment matters rather than simple rules, whether it recurs often enough for the product to learn, and whether a startup can begin with one assignment and grow into owning the entire job. Legal, tax and accounting repeatedly clear this bar because the work repeats, needs judgment and is routinely reviewed by experts, and less obvious markets such as industrial-defect investigation, where information is scattered across supplier documents, test results, equipment logs and plant rules that no single system owns, can too. The synthesis is not zero-sum, since an incumbent's record plus a general agent will be enough for some jobs, while for others a focused vertical company can win by becoming the best at doing the job itself. <span class="src">The Incumbents Are Coming</span>

Management consulting shows the dynamics already at work. AI has lowered the barrier to entry that scale once protected, and Queen's Tower Advisory, founded by a former Deloitte partner who found a small platform could suddenly operate like 100 to 150 people, aims for a team of roughly 20% humans and 80% AI agents, an instance of autonomous business functions. The Management Consultancy Association estimates that AI-enabled smaller firms are growing up to 50%, private capital that once waited for IPO-scale firms is backing entrants directly, with one European group putting more than EUR 500 million into tax-advisory challenger WTS, and incumbent stress shows in the numbers, with Big Four cuts to UK graduate recruitment, a 5,600 fall in PwC's global headcount in one year and a fall of more than 50% in Accenture's share price since late 2021, cutting its market value from over USD 260 billion to about USD 108 billion. <span class="src">How AI threatens the giants of consulting</span>

Three pillars of the consulting model are attacked together. The generalist-consultant model weakens as clients arrive with an AI-generated headline view and engage a human specialist only for the layer beneath, pushing incumbents toward deeper vertical specialization. Billable-hour pricing weakens because AI reviewing thousands of contracts in minutes severs hours from value, and McKinsey spent more than two years overhauling partner pay, with roughly a third of its work now tied to performance-based fees, while independence rules make outcome-based billing structurally harder for audit-dependent Big Four firms. The junior-heavy pyramid loses its logic without billable hours, pushing some firms toward a flatter obelisk and others toward an hourglass pinched in the middle as AI automates routine mid-level work. Incumbents retain money, global multidisciplinary reach, decades of client loyalty and air-gapped programs such as KPMG's Project Watts that let teams bypass approval bureaucracy to build client-facing AI tools in weeks, but the same tension that a16z describes appears at the top, since OpenAI has launched a USD 4 billion-backed consulting and services business while allying with major consultancies, which one critic calls letting the fox into the hen house. <span class="src">How AI threatens the giants of consulting</span>

Because startups differ greatly, MIT Sloan Management Review authors Davenport and Shay argue that existing categorizations, McKinsey's takers, shapers and makers, BCG's four-stage maturity continuum in which only 4% of surveyed companies reach the value-engine stage, the enablers-versus-adopters split and the investor shorthand of picks and shovels versus gold miners, were built for large incumbents or adoption strategy rather than for startups. Their typology instead asks how a company creates, delivers and extracts value from AI. Originators such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Perplexity, Cohere and Mistral build foundation models, with the highest capital and talent needs, extreme entry barriers and converging, increasingly commoditized model quality even as OpenAI keeps a usage lead. Explorers such as Cognition Labs, SandboxAQ, Safe Superintelligence and Thinking Machines Lab pursue scientific or architectural breakthroughs five to ten years out in agentic, quantum and general AI, are funded by mission-driven VCs or large incumbents, are often secretive and are measured by research output rather than revenue. <span class="src">Six Types of AI Startups, Explained</span> <span class="src">Putting AI to work</span>

Infrastructure builders such as Scale AI, Pinecone, Weights & Biases and chip startups Tenstorrent, Mythic and Groq supply the data, APIs and frameworks others build on, and succeed through ecosystem adoption and workflow integration depth more than model quality. Enhancers apply general-purpose models to vertical or functional problems, as Descript does for video and podcast editing and Jasper for marketing content, and this is where most AI startup activity concentrates, with 46% of Y Combinator's spring 2025 batch explicitly AI-agent startups, so differentiation must come from proprietary data, domain focus and workflow design because a thin wrapper on ChatGPT is not a product. Optimizers do not sell AI but use it to run their business better, such as logistics startups optimizing routes, direct-to-consumer retailers personalizing marketing and fintechs automating fraud detection, succeeding through operational excellence, in a world where McKinsey found 71% of organizations regularly using generative AI, up from 65% in early 2024. Experimenters, by far the largest cohort, use AI tools without budget, roadmap or integration plan, and BCG puts 74% of companies in the not-doing-much or pure-experimentation zone. The types are neither exclusive nor permanent, since a startup can move from experimenter to optimizer to enhancer, but transitions carry risk and capital needs, and the leap from enhancer to originator demands capital and technical depth few possess. <span class="src">Six Types of AI Startups, Explained</span>

The typology yields guidance for three groups. Founders are urged to ask what kind of AI company they are and how fast they can get there, with explorers competing on talent, compute and proprietary model quality, infrastructure builders on reduced developer friction and ecosystem depth, enhancers on workflow design and proprietary data rather than interface polish, optimizers on deploying AI only where it improves margin, and experimenters avoiding what Gartner calls the pilot-to-production chasm. Investors can use it as a diligence lens, asking whether the startup owns the model, data or distribution behind its advantage, whether anything proprietary lies beneath the interface and whether the exit is a product, a platform or a feature, since an experimenter, an enhancer with sticky vertical usage and an optimizer with internal leverage are very different opportunities even when they use the same buzzwords. Corporate buyers should match partner type to their maturity, partnering with infrastructure builders only when in-house capability exists and needs acceleration, such as an internal AI factory, engaging enhancers for fast function-specific wins in sales, content, HR or logistics, benchmarking against optimizers in their sector to judge realistic return, and treating originators and explorers as inspiration rather than models to imitate unless they will fund AI research at that level. <span class="src">Six Types of AI Startups, Explained</span> <span class="src">Putting AI to work</span>
