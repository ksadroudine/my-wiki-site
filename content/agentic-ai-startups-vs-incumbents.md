## Summary

Agentic AI has compressed the cost, time, and headcount needed to launch a startup — HBR documents five compounding forces behind this "second great compression of entrepreneurship" — while incumbents' systems of record remain valuable precisely because a general-purpose agent still can't reconstruct the full cross-system job on its own. A16z's "agent hierarchy" (retrieval, process, policy, principal) maps exactly where each side currently has the advantage, and where vertical AI startups can still win through focused, manufactured learning loops. A concrete industry case study — management consulting — shows the same compression already reshaping a real professional-services market — AI-native challenger firms are attacking the Big Four's billable-hour and pyramid-staffing model directly. MIT SMR also classifies AI startups into six types to help buyers choose partners.

## Highlights

- HBR's five compounding forces behind the "second great compression of entrepreneurship": zero-latency iteration (AI-assisted "vibe coding" collapses SaaS product-market-fit timelines from 12-18 months and $1M+ to days/weeks); automated go-to-market (AI agents generate and optimize marketing content and onboard customers in hours instead of months); autonomous business functions (AI-native startups need as few as two people — a domain expert and an AI engineer — versus six to eight for a traditional MVP team); radical capital efficiency (one venture studio's AI-native startups reached Series A on 80% less capital, 20-40% faster); and an AI-driven flywheel (deep workflow integration creates proprietary data and high switching costs that compound over time).
- Incumbents' structural mismatch isn't a shortage of AI tools but organizational architecture: siloed data, brittle integrations, and undocumented workflows mean agentic AI reproduces existing flaws faster rather than fixing them — echoing Michael Hammer's 1990 dictum "stop paving the cow paths... obliterate, don't automate" — while incumbents retain real counter-advantages in trust, compliance infrastructure, and top-tier specialized talent that AI-native challengers still lack.
- A16z's "agent hierarchy" ranks four types of application-level agents by required autonomy and judgment: retrieval assistants (find/summarize information), process agents (rule-based record updates and routing), policy agents (apply an organization's playbooks and thresholds to ambiguous cases), and principal agents (make open-ended judgment calls on strategy, risk, and resources) — with most incumbent AI products still climbing from retrieval toward policy, bounded by the record they own.
- "The job is bigger than the record": Salesforce's Claudeforce lets Claude become the front door to CRM work while Salesforce still owns the underlying data and actions — unbundling the interface from the system of record and creating an opening for AI-native startups to "rebundle" around the full cross-system job, since even MCP-based access across applications suffers latency and inconsistent entity definitions between systems.
- Vertical AI startups win through a manufactured learning loop rather than accumulated customer history: Harvey post-trained its Tenet model on ~1,750 synthetic legal-task environments (each with documents, tools, and an expert rubric of ~50 criteria) without using customer data to start, then used production deployment to learn firm-specific practice on top of the profession-level curriculum it had already built.
- The four-question test for a strong vertical AI market: can an expert quickly judge what the AI got right or wrong; is the work hard enough that judgment matters, not simple rules; does the work happen often enough for the product to actually learn; and can the startup start with one assignment and grow into owning the entire job — legal, tax, and accounting repeatedly clear this bar, and less obvious markets like industrial defect investigation can too.
- AI-native consulting challengers are attacking the Big Four's core economics directly: one UK startup (Queen's Tower Advisory) aims for a 20%-human/80%-AI-agent team composition, arguing "the amplification factor of AI" lets a 20-person platform operate like 150; the Management Consultancy Association estimates smaller firms are growing up to 50% as AI lets them compete on projects that used to require incumbent scale, while PwC's global headcount fell 5,600 in one year and Accenture's share price has fallen more than 50% since late 2021.
- The consulting disruption attacks three specific economic pillars simultaneously: the generalist-consultant model (clients increasingly get an AI-generated "cursory diagnosis" before engaging a human specialist, pushing incumbents toward deeper vertical specialization); the billable-hour pricing model (McKinsey spent two-plus years overhauling partner compensation, with roughly a third of its work now tied to performance-based fees, since AI reviewing thousands of contracts in minutes breaks the link between hours worked and value delivered); and the junior-heavy pyramid staffing structure (without billable hours, "that entire resourcing model loses its logic," pushing firms toward flatter "obelisk" or AI-automated-middle "hourglass" structures).

## Concept

HBR's research across dozens of AI-native startups, plus interviews with founders, investors, and corporate executives, documents what the authors call a "second great compression of entrepreneurship" — a shift as profound as the original internet-era compression, driven by five interconnected, mutually reinforcing forces. Zero-latency iteration means each version of a digital product can change instantly on customer feedback: where a SaaS startup traditionally needed 12 to 18 months and $1 million or more to establish product-market fit, AI-assisted "vibe coding" (natural-language-prompted development) now produces functional prototypes in days, with AI agents also critiquing technical architecture, proposing cleaner file structures, and running real-time security and performance testing — at one company, UX designers now modify UI code directly via an AI coding assistant without involving engineers at all, releasing new updates every two weeks. Automated go-to-market capabilities let AI-native startups execute marketing and customer-acquisition strategies once available only to well-funded enterprises: agents generate and test thousands of image/copy combinations, dynamically shift spend to the highest-performing channels, and handle onboarding tasks (data access, workflow mapping) that traditionally required months of manual configuration — one startup delivers presale live demos using a prospect's own data within hours and completes full implementations in roughly a quarter of the time traditional SaaS onboarding takes. Autonomous business functions dramatically shrink the headcount required to build and scale: where a conventional product team needs six to eight people (engineers, a product manager, UX researcher and designer, a business lead) to build an MVP in six to twelve months, today's AI-native startups can start with as few as two — a domain expert and an AI engineer who, using AI coding tools, "performs the work of ten." At one restaurant-decision-intelligence startup, a single AI engineer paired with an industry domain expert built an agentic system ingesting and validating restaurant data, correlating external factors like weather, and generating pricing and staffing recommendations within months; at a health-insurance prior-authorization startup, AI agents handling contract review cut legal expenses roughly 90%, freeing 30 of 40 total employees to work directly on the product itself. Radical capital efficiency follows directly: one AI-application-framework startup condensed a typical two-year-plus Series A fundraising journey to ten months, and one venture studio's twelve AI-native startups launched over four years reached Series A on 80% less capital and 20-40% less time than the studio's earlier, non-AI companies — meaning venture firms can make more bets per dollar of committed capital, potentially improving overall portfolio returns. And the AI-driven flywheel compounds all four of the above: solving genuinely "unsolvable" manual tasks at the center of a customer's business gives an AI-native startup deep, proprietary operational and workflow knowledge that off-the-shelf agent platforms can't replicate — a health-insurance startup built what its founder called "the best medical-record ingestion engine" by reliably structuring 600-page unstructured patient files that both humans and generic AI systems struggle with, and as health plans build increasing trust in that structured data, deep workflow integration creates the same high switching costs that made SaaS incumbents valuable in the first place, now working in the startup's favor instead. <span class="src">How Agentic AI Supercharges Startups and Threatens Incumbents</span>

HBR frames the resulting threat to incumbents as structural rather than a simple shortage of AI tools: startups form around clean workflows, tight learning loops, and data models designed for continuous learning, while most incumbents evolved for stability, specialization, and control, with middle managers whose authority derives from controlling information and resources often resisting anything that threatens their span of control — not from a lack of capability, but because the roles themselves were designed for a different operating logic, echoing Clayton Christensen's original warning that the processes serving an incumbent's core market can "blind" it to fundamentally different ways of operating. Technical debt compounds this: enterprise workflows are rarely documented cleanly, data lives in silos, integrations are brittle, and exceptions require manual intervention — conditions agentic AI depends on being resolved, not conditions it resolves on its own — which is why HBR's central prescriptive warning, echoing Michael Hammer's 1990 "Reengineering Work: Don't Automate, Obliterate," is that incumbents' most common mistake is trying to automate broken workflows before re-architecting them, simply reproducing existing flaws at machine speed instead. Yet HBR is explicit that AI-native startups carry real vulnerabilities incumbents can exploit: autonomous systems introduce genuine quality, reliability, and compliance risk (a misinterpreted signal or faulty configuration can trigger real-world consequences, and unlike deterministic software, agents don't reliably produce the same output twice, making behavior harder to standardize and verify), and startups must build the testing, monitoring, audit-trail, and incident-response infrastructure incumbents have spent decades refining before customers will extend them the same trust — talent is a further constraint, since agentic AI increases (rather than decreases) the need for top-tier employees with deep specialized industry knowledge, precisely the people already concentrated at large established companies. HBR's recommended incumbent response sequence is: run a vulnerability audit against the five disruptive forces above, identifying which high-volume, high-handoff workflows an AI-native rival would attack first; start with one bounded, high-friction process rather than an easy, low-stakes pilot, since a pilot that clearly raises the bar organization-wide resets both customer and internal expectations (one IT-services firm's AI-assisted RFP-response system cut a task requiring hundreds of personnel-hours down to a 20-minute first draft, increasing both response volume and win rate); re-architect the process before automating it, removing unnecessary steps and clarifying decision ownership first; consider partnering with AI-native ventures specifically to see firsthand how a legacy-free workflow can run; strengthen data quality and make human-handoff points explicit rather than implicit; and prepare people for roles shifting toward judgment, edge cases, and oversight of agents rather than routine execution. <span class="src">How Agentic AI Supercharges Startups and Threatens Incumbents</span>

A16z's companion analysis, published two months later, supplies the structural map for exactly where in this fight incumbents versus AI-native startups currently hold the advantage — organized around what it calls the "agent hierarchy," four types of application-level agents distinguished by how much autonomy and judgment each requires. Retrieval assistants find information, summarize documents, and draft responses; process agents carry out rule-based work like updating records or routing approvals using limited interpretive judgment; policy agents apply an organization's existing playbooks, precedents, and thresholds to genuinely ambiguous cases (Docusign's Iris applying a legal team's own review playbook is the example given); and principal agents make open-ended judgment calls involving strategic tradeoffs around risk and resources that no organization has yet handed to AI. A16z's read is that most incumbent AI products, only chatbots and analytical retrieval tools a year earlier, are now moving up this hierarchy toward narrow policy application — but that judgment remains bounded by the specific record the incumbent's system of record actually owns, which is where the article's central argument turns: "the job is bigger than the record." A customer's actual work gets done through a combination of people, process, and software that crosses applications, teams, and even separate companies — a signed contract isn't the underlying legal matter, a closed support ticket isn't the customer's actual resolution, a CRM opportunity record isn't the sale itself — and different parties along that chain hold different pieces of information and want different outcomes. This is precisely the space Salesforce's Claudeforce (a partnership letting users work with Salesforce CRM data entirely from inside Claude, without opening the Salesforce interface at all) illustrates concretely: Claude becomes the front door to the job while Salesforce continues to own the underlying CRM data and the actions performed on it, unbundling the interface layer from the system-of-record layer and opening a genuine rebundling opportunity for AI-native challengers — though a16z notes even MCP-based cross-application access introduces real latency, and different applications frequently describe the identical customer, contract, or transaction using inconsistent internal definitions, meaning access alone doesn't guarantee a general-purpose agent can perform a cross-system job well, and a general agent still cannot see information held entirely outside a company's own systems (a supplier's internal records, for instance). A16z's account of how vertical AI actually learns to close this gap distinguishes remembering from learning directly: frontier labs are already building general-purpose memory that lets any agent recall a customer's stated preferences or a decision from last week, but memory alone doesn't tell an agent whether a piece of past work was actually good, why an expert corrected it, or what to change next time — that requires a genuine learning loop combining the underlying model, a domain-specific "harness" (context, tools, workflows, evaluation criteria), and iterative feedback from expert corrections and real outcomes. Two distinct kinds of learning compound within that loop: learning the general standards of a profession (teachable from expert-created assignments, realistic examples, and quality rubrics, benefiting every customer of the product) and learning one particular firm's or customer's specific practice (its templates, precedents, risk thresholds, escalation rules — beneficial mainly to that one customer) — and a startup doesn't need to start with the largest possible stock of historical customer data to build this; it can manufacture a professional-level curriculum first and then use live production deployment to learn the institution-specific layer on top of it. Legal AI company Harvey's work on its Tenet model is a16z's concrete illustration: Harvey post-trained an open-weight model using synthetic data, public legal data, and human-expert-created data, without starting from any customer data at all, constructing roughly 1,750 distinct legal-task environments (each simulating a partner-assigned matter, complete with documents, tools, and an expert-defined rubric averaging about 50 specific quality criteria) — manufacturing a training-and-evaluation curriculum rather than waiting years to accumulate enough customer history to learn from organically. A16z's proposed four-question test for identifying a strong vertical AI market is: can an expert quickly judge what the AI got right or wrong and explain how to improve it; is the underlying work hard enough that genuine judgment matters, rather than reducible to simple deterministic rules; does the work recur often enough for the product to actually accumulate learning; and can the startup plausibly begin with one narrow assignment and expand into owning the entire end-to-end job over time — markets meeting all four (legal, tax, and accounting repeatedly clear this bar because the work repeats, requires judgment, and is already reviewed by experts as a matter of course; less obvious markets like industrial-defect investigation, where information about a manufacturing problem is scattered across supplier documents, test results, equipment logs, and plant rules that no single system owns end to end, can clear it too) offer the strongest learning-loop dynamics. A16z's closing synthesis is explicitly not zero-sum: there are real jobs where an incumbent's system of record plus a general-purpose agent will simply be enough, and other jobs where a focused vertical AI-native company can still win by becoming demonstrably the best at doing the specific job itself — "the incumbent may own the record and the lab may control the front door, but the vertical AI company can still win by becoming the best at doing the job itself." <span class="src">The Incumbents Are Coming</span>

<span class="src">How AI threatens the giants of consulting</span> (The Economist/FT, May 2026) grounds this concept's abstract startup-vs-incumbent dynamics in a single, already-underway industry disruption: management consulting, where AI has "brought down the barrier to entry" scale used to protect. Queen's Tower Advisory, founded by a former Deloitte partner after AI made a small platform "suddenly at 100 people, 150 people, very quickly," aims for a team composition of roughly 20% humans and 80% AI agents — a concrete instance of HBR's "autonomous business functions" force, where a domain expert paired with AI tooling replaces what used to require a much larger team. The Management Consultancy Association estimates such AI-enabled smaller firms are growing up to 50%, private-equity capital that once waited for firms to reach IPO-scale before investing is now backing new entrants directly (one European private-capital group put more than €500 million into tax-advisory challenger WTS), and incumbent stress is already visible in the numbers: the Big Four cut UK graduate recruitment and PwC's global headcount fell 5,600 in a single year, while Accenture's share price has fallen more than 50% since late 2021, cutting its market value from over $260 billion to about $108 billion. The disruption attacks three specific pillars of the traditional consulting model simultaneously, mapping directly onto this concept's incumbent-vulnerability framework: the generalist-consultant model breaks down as clients increasingly arrive with an AI-generated "cursory or headline view" of a problem before engaging a human specialist for "that layer down," pushing incumbents toward deeper vertical specialization and away from the broad generalist staffing AI now handles; the billable-hour pricing model breaks down because AI reviewing thousands of contracts in minutes severs the link between hours worked and value delivered — McKinsey spent more than two years overhauling how it pays partners in response, with roughly a third of its work now tied to performance-based fees, while independence rules make outcome-based billing structurally harder for audit-dependent Big Four firms than for pure-play consultancies; and the junior-heavy pyramid staffing structure breaks down since, without billable hours to justify large cohorts of junior staff, "that entire resourcing model loses its logic," pushing some firms toward a flatter "obelisk" structure and others toward an "hourglass" pinched in the middle as AI automates routine mid-level work — directly extending the same pyramid-disruption pattern documented for AI-native product-development teams and agentic-native software companies elsewhere in this concept. Incumbents retain real counter-advantages this concept's other sources already identify in the abstract: money (billions already invested in AI infrastructure), global multidisciplinary reach for complex multinational engagements, deep client loyalty built over decades, and internal "air-gapped" experimentation programs like KPMG's Project Watts that let teams bypass normal approval bureaucracy to build client-facing AI tools in weeks rather than months. But the article's closing tension is squarely the "job is bigger than the record" dynamic a16z describes: OpenAI has launched its own $4-billion-backed consulting and services business and forged alliances with major consultancies even as it builds tools that could bypass consultants entirely, prompting one critic to describe the incumbents' own AI partnerships as "letting the fox into the hen house" — the frontier labs that supply incumbents' AI tools are simultaneously positioned to become their most direct competitors. <span class="src">How AI threatens the giants of consulting</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses how quickly incumbents' own compliance, audit-trail, and testing infrastructure — the advantage HBR identifies as incumbents' strongest defense — can itself be commoditized or replicated by well-funded AI-native startups, several of which (per HBR's own examples) already operate in highly regulated industries like health insurance.
- A16z's "agent hierarchy" describes where incumbent products currently sit but doesn't address how quickly incumbents could climb from policy toward principal-level agents if they chose to invest aggressively — leaving unclear whether the current incumbent-startup balance of power is durable or simply an artifact of incumbents' present under-investment.
- The consulting-disruption source doesn't resolve whether mid-tier firms (too small for Big-Four-scale AI investment, too slow-moving for boutique agility) face a genuinely different fate than either incumbents or startups, beyond one expert's warning that they risk being "stranded in the middle" — none of this concept's frameworks (HBR's five forces, a16z's agent hierarchy) explicitly models a three-way rather than two-way competitive outcome.
