## Summary

Enterprise cyber resilience now has to address attackers who log in rather than break in, AI systems that find and exploit vulnerabilities at machine speed, and dependencies on vendors whose failures cascade across the economy. PwC's outlook sets six priorities for 2026, from securing AI and cloud to unifying operational technology and IT and modernizing the security operations center, and pairs them with practical steps such as agentic remediation within an autonomy envelope and a board briefing within 90 days. Interviews with CEOs who survived attacks show that resilience, not prevention alone, requires leaders to be responsible before an incident, and the CrowdStrike outage shows the need for explainable cyber risk management that models update risk, supply-chain impact and accountability in advance.

## Concept

PwC frames enterprise cybersecurity for 2026 around a threat landscape in which attackers increasingly log in rather than break in, exploiting legitimate accounts and authentication instead of breaching perimeters, in stealthier, persistent, identity-centric operations that are often tied to geopolitical and ideological conflict, while AI and increasingly accessible attack tools lower the barrier to covert, widespread campaigns. Six priorities follow. Securing AI systems themselves is the number one investment priority in PwC's 2026 Global Digital Trust Insights survey, even as AI-powered threat hunting is the leading defensive AI capability being adopted, and it requires extending controls to AI systems, adding AI-specific safeguards and embedding governance early under secure-by-design principles. Cloud protection is the second priority and the threat organizations feel least prepared to manage, since perimeter defenses cannot keep pace with cloud-native malware, insider threats, misconfigurations and supply-chain vulnerabilities, and PwC recommends AI-driven real-time detection, strong data governance, continuous asset monitoring and zero-trust principles. <span class="src">What’s ahead for cybersecurity in 2026</span>

The third priority is the convergence of operational technology and IT, since systems built for efficiency rather than security are being connected to internet-facing environments, expanding the attack surface into physically consequential domains such as utilities and requiring asset visibility, network segmentation and OT security within broader risk management. The fourth is supply-chain visibility, because third parties have become common entry points, particularly in sectors such as healthcare where supplier failures threaten patient safety, so periodic assessments are giving way to continuous real-time monitoring. The fifth is a SOC 2.0, in which security operations centers powered by automation, AI, machine learning and intelligent agents across endpoints and networks move analysts from reactive alert triage to proactive hunting. The sixth is emerging technology, with satellite communications exposed to interception and jamming, quantum computing threatening current encryption while nearly half of organizations have not begun migrating to post-quantum cryptography even though migration takes years, and 6G, expected around 2030, widening the attack surface through unproven protocols. Success underlying all six depends on a skilled, empowered workforce, and 53% of organizations are prioritizing AI and machine-learning tools to close talent gaps. <span class="src">What’s ahead for cybersecurity in 2026</span>

PwC grounds the cloud and legacy priorities in its own experience. Its survey ranks cloud-related threats as the single threat organizations feel least prepared to address, with legacy systems and supply chain the top two vulnerabilities and more than half of respondents only somewhat capable of withstanding attacks there, a gap PwC attributes less to a shortage of signals than to signals scattered across disconnected tools, times and systems that make it hard to see what is related and to prioritize. To address its own legacy exposure, PwC piloted Microsoft's newly released Baseline Security Mode, a single-dashboard configuration framework covering 20 focus areas that Microsoft identified as the most commonly exploited legacy and misconfigured settings in Microsoft 365, such as applications still connecting through outdated exchange web services or legacy file formats. The pilot gave PwC centralized visibility into settings it had managed across multiple locations and global shutoff switches for risky features, and over half of the recommendations were higher-impact fixes it had not prioritized, which it ran through change management and then turned into client offerings, namely automation tooling to consume and prioritize recommendations, a center of excellence and tailored playbooks. The broader argument is that foundational security is a collective imperative, since one organization's weak legacy posture can affect many others in a hyperconnected ecosystem, so strengthening the baseline everywhere raises collective resilience. <span class="src">Security at the speed of business</span>

A driver behind the outlook's urgency is that frontier AI models can discover and exploit software vulnerabilities at a speed, scope and complexity beyond what manual research or traditional patch cycles can absorb, producing what PwC calls a vulnerability onslaught that will overwhelm even mature teams constrained by headcount and specialized skills. Working with Anthropic, PwC does not ask organizations to replace their security investments but integrates Claude Opus 4.7 into the policy enforcement points, security controls and test infrastructure they already own, through the Model Context Protocol and API integration, since most enterprises run mixed environments of legacy and modern systems, incompletely integrated acquisitions and mainframes where wholesale code changes are rarely practical. The approach deploys agentic remediation, an AI harness that determines which defensive levers to pull across the existing control stack and activates them, agentic test-case generation, which turns probabilistic model output into deterministic test cases so patches and rule changes can be validated automatically, and pre-production validation orchestration, which produces structured proof that an action achieved its security objective without unintended business impact. <span class="src">Preparing for the vulnerability onslaught Build on what you can trust</span>

Validation is what unlocks genuine autonomy in security operations, because once an organization can prove that an agent's action did not break the business it can delegate real decision rights within defined boundaries. PwC formalizes this as an autonomy envelope of decision boundaries, standing authority frameworks and an audit trail, which lets defensive agents act at machine speed on routine actions while humans keep oversight of scope, escalation thresholds and policy. <span class="src">Preparing for the vulnerability onslaught Build on what you can trust</span>

The leadership case for why this matters at board level centers on Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, described as the first model to show at scale a capability that upends a core assumption of cyber risk, that the most dangerous weaknesses in critical networks are hard and slow to find, by autonomously chaining multiple vulnerabilities into a working exploit, a task that once required elite human operators. PwC stresses that this is structural rather than a one-vendor story, since similar capabilities are emerging across frontier labs and comparable models will arrive faster than most companies can prepare, so every organization should accept that vulnerabilities it is not yet aware of already exist in its environment and some may become exploitable before they can be fixed. Collaborative responses such as Anthropic's Project Glasswing consortium, with roughly 50 participating organizations, are a useful start but cover only a fraction of the enterprise landscape, and as widely used platforms are hardened PwC expects adversaries to redirect toward enterprise-specific targets that only the organization can secure, which is why it calls security posture a leadership obligation. Adopting AI-assisted defensive scanning, which PwC calls the most significant capability defenders have gained in a decade, is the credible response, since every week of operating at human speed while attackers use AI widens the gap and declining to use such tools will become harder to defend as a standard of care. <span class="src">Leadership in the age of AI-exposed risk</span>

PwC translates this into six steps leaders can direct immediately. First, raise the baseline of basic security with risk-based patching, universal multi-factor authentication, segmentation around crown-jewel assets and behavioral detection beyond known signatures, since its survey shows AI mainly magnifies existing execution gaps. Second, turn AI inward before adversaries do by pointing agents at the organization's own code, configurations and dependencies, requiring agent-driven security review before every production merge and building toward a standing vulnerability-operations capability, while governing the defensive agents like any privileged access, because autonomous tools without audit trails create a new attack surface. Third, treat visibility as non-negotiable, starting with a consolidated, continuously updated inventory of the applications, data stores, identities and external dependencies where compromise would do most damage. Fourth, match machine-speed attacks with machine-speed response, using deception such as canaries, honey tokens and decoy credentials that produce high-confidence signals when an attacker moves laterally and pre-authorized containment actions, such as isolating a host, disabling a credential or rotating a secret, that run automatically when trigger conditions are met so that the first critical minutes are not lost to approval chains built for a slower era. <span class="src">Leadership in the age of AI-exposed risk</span>

Fifth, brief the board within 90 days on five dimensions, namely the pace of adaptation to an adversary that scales without fatigue, visibility and exposure, such as the share of critical assets fully inventoried and monitored and unmitigated third-party exposure, resilience and containment, such as time to patch against AI-driven exploitation timelines, maximum blast radius and detection speed for lateral movement, remediation velocity, the ratio of discovered to remediated vulnerabilities, and governance and disclosure readiness, including whether SEC materiality assessment and incident reporting can be done within four business days and whether SOX IT general controls are designed for this pace. Sixth, engage the wider ecosystem through sector information-sharing groups and by holding critical third parties accountable for their own remediation pace. PwC's reassurance is that most cyber risk still comes from longstanding, known weaknesses and misaligned incentives rather than novel threats, so the tools now available, AI-assisted scanning above all, can close the gap at scale if leaders act on the exposure they already have rather than waiting for regulators or a breach to set the standard of care. <span class="src">Leadership in the age of AI-exposed risk</span>

Leadership practice matters as much as technology, and MIT Sloan Management Review's research draws on 37 in-depth interviews with CEOs of large enterprises, averaging USD 12 billion in revenue, across the US, Europe and Asia, nine of whom had led their company through a serious cyberattack, which allows a comparison of battle-tested and untested views. The clearest regret among attack-tested CEOs was overemphasizing prevention at the expense of resilience, focusing on whether an attack would occur rather than when and how the organization would respond, and pouring resources into technology while lacking basic resilience practices across departments. They came to understand cyber resilience as the capacity to anticipate, withstand, respond to and adapt to an attack, minimizing impact, recovering quickly and emerging stronger, since even the most advanced organizations will eventually be breached. <span class="src">The CEO’s Cyber Resilience Playbook</span>

Four further regrets completed the pattern. CEOs regretted feeling merely accountable for cybersecurity, taking ownership after something went wrong, instead of being proactively responsible beforehand, a distinction that matters because 72% of all CEOs interviewed, attacked or not, admitted discomfort making cyber decisions, and several wanted to become co-responsible with the CISO, a shift CISOs welcomed because they had generally found their CEOs insufficiently engaged. They regretted blindly trusting cybersecurity and technology teams without building enough understanding to ask informed questions or challenge advice, a gap that widened when an attack left the company's fate in the hands of experts whose reasoning they could not evaluate, and the research found that the more CEOs understood resilience the more concerned and engaged they became. Nearly all CEOs without attack experience thought their organizations well prepared, a confidence that only the attack-tested recognized as dangerous complacency, because even careful crisis playbooks cannot capture how unpredictably an attack unfolds and an incident may compromise the very communication channels a playbook assumes. Finally, they regretted reacting to competing stakeholder demands during a crisis, with shareholders asking about financial impact, the board about evidence of recovery, regulators about answers and customers about their data, instead of reassuring stakeholders proactively, and a CEO can take the posture of an amplifier who reinforces external pressure to maintain urgency, a filter who judges which pressures to pass to the response team, or an absorber who takes the pressure directly so that it does not distract the people running recovery. <span class="src">The CEO’s Cyber Resilience Playbook</span>

From these regrets the research distills a seven-practice playbook for building resilience before an attack teaches the lesson. Bringing in an outsider CEO with direct attack experience to brief leadership and the board gives a relatable wake-up call that technologists cannot, and a CEO-chaired cyber resilience learning forum, held at least annually and ideally quarterly and kept separate from formal risk governance, builds shared understanding and normalizes naive questions. An independent resilience audit each year, treated as seriously as a financial audit, builds informed trust between the CEO and the technical team and exposes blind spots, and although CISOs sometimes hesitate to propose it for fear of being put on the spot, it tends to build trust and shared responsibility. Identifying the two to five business processes most critical to keeping the organization running, and mapping them to supporting applications and servers, must happen before an attack, because otherwise every department insists its own processes be restored first. Lining up legal, communications, forensic and, in the worst case, ransom-negotiation help in advance and preparing a proactive crisis communications plan spare the CEO from building relationships and messages under pressure, since markets and regulators are less forgiving of organizations that look caught flat-footed or seem to be hiding something. A full postmortem covering communication and business continuity as well as technical root cause often exposes outdated processes, and surviving an attack can create value, as one CEO redirected savings from post-attack technology consolidation into further resilience investment and others described new appreciation for previously invisible critical processes and the chance to share lessons with peers, even competitors, since resilience is a noncompetitive domain. <span class="src">The CEO’s Cyber Resilience Playbook</span>

The CrowdStrike outage shows accountability gaps in a dependent ecosystem. On July 19, 2024, a single faulty content update from CrowdStrike crashed more than 8.5 million systems worldwide and disrupted thousands of organizations, including hundreds of Fortune 1000 companies, for days, with losses estimated above USD 5 billion and roughly USD 1.5 billion in insurer payouts across business-interruption, cyber and system-failure coverage, a clear demonstration of aggregated cyber risk in which one point of failure propagates losses across a dependent ecosystem. Delta Air Lines sued, calling the outage catastrophic, claiming it disrupted 7,000 flights and 1.3 million customers over five days at a cost above USD 500 million and blaming forced untested updates, including onto systems such as its own where automatic updates had been disabled. CrowdStrike, while acknowledging a fault in its own update, said Delta's claims showed a lack of understanding of how modern cybersecurity works and attributed Delta's slow recovery to an antiquated IT infrastructure, and pointed to a contractually capped liability. The dispute arose as risk worsened despite more investment, with the average cost of a data breach reaching USD 4.88 million in 2024, up 10%, confirmed breaches at a record high in Verizon's analysis, and a Milliman survey finding a fragmented solutions market, decisions built on disjointed data, weak ability to assess concentration risk, a soft cyber insurance market lacking historical data, limited supply-chain transparency and new uncertainty from AI, quantum and cloud computing. <span class="src">What the 2024 CrowdStrike Glitch Can Teach Us About Cyber Risk</span>

HBR frames four questions that neither CrowdStrike nor the industry can answer with systematic, quantifiable methods. The first is how risky an update actually is, since CrowdStrike's Falcon software issues content updates, sometimes several a day, to improve protection, and had done so thousands of times since 2013 with almost no reported problems, treating them as effectively zero-risk, yet no update is truly risk-free and its risk must be weighed against the risk it removes. The second is how to mitigate update risk, by applying immediately or delaying, which balances the risk of disruption from a faulty update, which falls with delay, against the risk of remaining exposed, which rises with it, and Delta's lawsuit noted that it had disabled automatic updates to preserve its change-management controls while CrowdStrike pushed the update anyway, a mistake CrowdStrike acknowledged by giving customers more control, although the industry still lacks agreement on what risk information vendors should disclose and how customers should act on it. The third is how to ensure business resiliency when an update proves faulty despite good testing and certification, and the vague claims of both parties about Delta's resiliency relative to other airlines show that no rigorous, comparable method exists for assessing organizational cyber resilience. The fourth is how to allocate accountability, since CrowdStrike cited a liability cap in the single-digit millions, and Microsoft was drawn in over certifying Windows kernel-access software, which shows that multi-party dependencies pose questions that contracts and law handle ambiguously. <span class="src">What the 2024 CrowdStrike Glitch Can Teach Us About Cyber Risk</span>

The proposed response is explainable cyber risk management, a paradigm meant to supply the end-to-end, credible and justifiable risk insight that current solutions, focused on local targets, do not. It requires five capabilities. Organizations must model the what-if downstream business impact, direct and escalated, of a cyber event at any supply-chain partner, since many, Delta apparently included, had not considered a faulty vendor update in advance. They must set explicit cyber-resiliency objectives and processes for impactful events at partners, given how widely recovery times and losses varied among organizations hit by the same incident, and must assess the likelihood and interdependence of events continuously rather than occasionally, since CrowdStrike allegedly failed to assess the likelihood of faults in its own update process. Transparent internal and external communication must underpin risk decisions, going beyond CrowdStrike's move to give customers control over update timing to share each update's risk attributes, and accountability boundaries between parties must be defined before an incident rather than left to litigation. The conclusion is that unintentional technology failures are inevitable and malicious attacks will grow in scale as reliance on digital technology increases, so more transparent, structured and agile risk management is needed, not just better individual security tools. <span class="src">What the 2024 CrowdStrike Glitch Can Teach Us About Cyber Risk</span>
