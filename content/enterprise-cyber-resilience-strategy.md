## Summary

How enterprises are restructuring cyber defense for 2026 — PwC's six priority areas (securing AI, cloud protection, OT/IT convergence, supply chain visibility, next-generation SOCs, and emerging tech like quantum and 6G) combined with proactive legacy-vulnerability visibility. Also covers PwC and Anthropic's approach to AI-accelerated vulnerability remediation deployed inside an organization's existing security tools, and the six-step, board-level leadership response PwC recommends now that AI can find and chain exploitable vulnerabilities at machine speed.

## Concept

Heading into 2026, PwC frames enterprise cybersecurity around six priorities that together describe where the threat landscape is shifting and where security investment needs to follow. Attackers increasingly "log in" rather than "break in" — exploiting legitimate accounts and authentication rather than breaching perimeters — in stealthier, persistent, identity-centric operations often tied to geopolitical and ideological conflict, while AI and increasingly accessible attack tools lower the bar for conducting covert, widespread campaigns. Against this backdrop, securing AI systems themselves has become the #1 cybersecurity investment priority in PwC's 2026 Global Digital Trust Insights survey, even as AI-powered threat hunting is the leading AI-driven defensive capability organizations are adopting; this requires extending existing security controls to cover AI systems, adding AI-specific safeguards, and embedding governance and risk controls early in AI development following secure-by-design principles. Cloud protection is the second priority and, notably, the threat category organizations feel least prepared to manage — traditional perimeter defenses cannot keep pace with sophisticated cloud-native threats (malware, insider threats, misconfigurations, supply chain vulnerabilities), and PwC recommends AI-driven real-time threat detection paired with strong data governance, continuous asset monitoring, and zero-trust principles. Third, the convergence of operational technology (OT) and IT — systems historically built for efficiency rather than security now being connected to internet-facing environments — is expanding the attack surface into physically consequential domains like utilities, requiring clear asset visibility, network segmentation, and OT security embedded into broader risk-management frameworks. Fourth, supply chain visibility matters because third parties have become common entry points for attackers, particularly in sectors like healthcare where supplier security failures directly threaten patient safety; traditional periodic risk assessments are giving way to continuous, real-time monitoring of digital supply chains. Fifth, "SOC 2.0" — security operations centers powered by automation, AI, machine learning, and intelligent agents deployed across endpoints and networks — shifts analysts away from reactive alert-triage toward proactive threat hunting. Sixth, emerging technologies introduce genuinely new risk categories: satellite communications face signal interception and jamming as space-based connectivity expands; quantum computing threatens current encryption on a timeline where nearly half of organizations haven't yet begun migrating to post-quantum cryptography, even though the migration itself takes years; and 6G (expected around 2030) will widen the attack surface through new, still-unproven protocols. Underlying all six priorities, PwC stresses that success depends on a skilled, empowered workforce — 53 percent of organizations are prioritizing AI and machine learning tools specifically to close cybersecurity talent gaps. <span class="src">What’s ahead for cybersecurity in 2026</span>

A companion PwC piece grounds two of these priorities — cloud and legacy-system exposure — in the firm's own operational experience. PwC's 2026 Global Digital Trust Insights survey ranks cloud-related threats as the single cyber threat organizations feel least prepared to address, with legacy systems and supply chain named as the top two vulnerabilities and more than half of respondents rating their organizations only "somewhat capable" of withstanding attacks in those areas — a gap PwC attributes less to a shortage of security signals than to those signals being scattered across disconnected tools, times, and systems, making it hard to see what's related and prioritize effectively. To address its own legacy exposure, PwC piloted a newly released Microsoft feature, Baseline Security Mode (BSM), which provides a single-dashboard configuration framework covering 20 focus areas Microsoft identified as the most commonly exploited legacy and misconfigured settings across Microsoft 365 environments (such as applications still connecting through outdated exchange web services or legacy file formats). The pilot gave PwC centralized visibility into legacy settings it previously had to manage across multiple locations, plus simple global shutoff switches for risky features — and notably, over half of BSM's recommendations turned out to be higher-impact fixes PwC hadn't previously prioritized, which it then ran through formal change-management processes. Based on this experience, PwC built complementary capabilities to help clients operationalize similar programs: automation tooling to consume and prioritize BSM's recommendations, a center of excellence for rollout support, and tailored playbooks. The broader argument is that foundational security is now a collective imperative rather than a purely internal one — in a hyperconnected digital ecosystem, one organization's weak legacy posture can affect many others, so strengthening the baseline everywhere raises collective resilience. <span class="src">Security at the speed of business</span>

A third PwC piece, produced jointly with Anthropic, addresses a specific driver behind the 2026 outlook's urgency: frontier AI models can now discover and exploit software vulnerabilities at a speed, scope, and complexity that exceeds what manual security research or traditional patch cycles can absorb, producing what PwC calls a coming "vulnerability onslaught" that will overwhelm even mature security teams constrained by headcount and specialized skills. Rather than proposing that organizations replace their existing security investments, PwC and Anthropic's approach integrates Claude Opus 4.7 directly into an organization's already-owned policy enforcement points, security controls, and test infrastructure via the Model Context Protocol and API integration — extending existing capabilities at machine speed rather than requiring migration to a new platform, which matters because most enterprises run complex mixed-technology environments (legacy and modern systems side by side, incompletely integrated acquisitions, mainframes) where wholesale code changes are rarely practical. Concretely, this means deploying agentic remediation (an AI harness, driven by frontier reasoning, that determines which defensive levers to pull across a client's existing control stack and activates them), agentic test-case generation (converting probabilistic model outputs into deterministic test cases so patches and rule changes can be automatically validated before deployment), and pre-production validation orchestration (generating structured proof, before any autonomous change lands, that the action achieved its security objective without unintended business impact). This validation capability is what unlocks genuine agent autonomy in security operations: once an organization can prove an agent's action didn't break the business, it can delegate real decision rights to that agent within defined boundaries — formalized in PwC's "autonomy envelope," comprising decision boundaries, standing authority frameworks, and an audit trail that let defensive agents act at machine speed on routine defensive actions while humans retain oversight over scope, escalation thresholds, and policy. <span class="src">Preparing for the vulnerability onslaught Build on what you can trust</span>

A companion PwC piece, published earlier and directly referenced by the vulnerability-onslaught playbook above, makes the leadership case for why this shift matters at the board level rather than purely the technical level. It centers on Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, described as the first AI model to demonstrate at scale a capability that upends a core assumption of cyber risk — that the most dangerous weaknesses in critical networks are difficult and time-consuming to find — by autonomously chaining multiple vulnerabilities into a working exploit, a task that previously required elite human operators. PwC stresses that this is a structural shift in the threat landscape, not a one-vendor story: similar capabilities are emerging across frontier AI labs and new models with comparable ability will arrive faster than most companies can prepare for them, meaning every organization should confront the essential truth that vulnerabilities neither it nor its security team is yet aware of already exist in its environment, some of which may become actively exploitable before they can be fixed. Collaborative, ecosystem-level responses — such as Anthropic's own technical consortium, Project Glasswing, which includes roughly 50 participating organizations — are a useful starting point but by definition cover only a fraction of the enterprise landscape; as widely used platforms get hardened through such efforts, PwC expects adversaries to redirect toward the enterprise-specific targets that only the organization itself can secure, which is why it frames security posture explicitly as "a leadership obligation" rather than a purely technical one. The credible response, in PwC's view, is for organizations to adopt AI-assisted defensive scanning themselves — described as the most significant capability defenders have gained in a decade — since every week an organization continues operating at human speed while attackers use AI only widens the gap, and as these tools become standard, choosing not to use them will become harder to justify as a defensible standard of care. PwC translates this into six steps leaders can direct immediately: raising the baseline of "basic" security (risk-based patching, universal multi-factor authentication, segmentation around crown-jewel assets, and behavioral detection that goes beyond known attack signatures, since PwC's own 2026 survey data shows AI mainly magnifies existing execution gaps rather than creating entirely new ones); turning AI inward before adversaries do, by pointing AI-based agents at the organization's own code, configurations, and dependencies, requiring agent-driven security review before every production merge, and building toward a standing Vulnerability Operations capability — while governing these defensive agents with the same rigor applied to any other privileged access, since autonomous security tools without audit trails simply create a new attack surface of their own; treating visibility as non-negotiable, starting with a consolidated, continuously updated inventory of the critical applications, data stores, identities, and external dependencies where compromise would cause the most damage; matching machine-speed attacks with machine-speed response, through deception techniques (canaries, honey tokens, decoy credentials) that generate high-confidence signals the moment an attacker moves laterally, paired with pre-authorized containment actions (isolating a host, disabling a credential, rotating a secret) that execute automatically once trigger conditions are met, so the first critical minutes of containment aren't lost to approval chains built for a slower era; briefing the board within 90 days on five specific dimensions — pace of adaptation, visibility and exposure, resilience and containment, remediation velocity, and governance/disclosure readiness (including whether SEC materiality assessment and four-business-day incident reporting requirements, and SOX IT General Controls, are actually designed for AI-driven attack timelines); and engaging the wider security ecosystem through sector-specific information-sharing groups and by holding critical third-party providers accountable for their own remediation pace. The underlying reassurance PwC offers alongside this urgency is that most cyber risk still comes from longstanding, known weaknesses and misaligned incentives rather than genuinely novel threats — meaning the tools now available (AI-assisted scanning chief among them) are powerful enough to close that gap at scale, if leadership teams choose to act on the exposure they already have rather than waiting for the standard of care to be set for them by regulators or by an actual breach. <span class="src">Leadership in the age of AI-exposed risk</span>
