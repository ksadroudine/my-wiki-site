## Summary

AI safety cannot be meaningfully assessed or guaranteed at the level of a model alone, because whether a use is harmful usually depends on the deployment context that the model cannot see. This implies that misuse defenses belong mainly at the downstream attack surface, that open versus closed release should be judged by marginal risk, and that red teaming should track adversary capability and be led by third parties. Because the resulting system-level evaluation depends on independent researchers, the sources also propose a legal and technical safe harbor that would protect good-faith evaluation of deployed models from lawsuits, account suspension and rate limiting.

## Concept

The first argument challenges what its authors call a pervasive, rarely examined assumption in the AI safety field, that safety is a property of the model itself, checkable and fixable through alignment training and red teaming applied to the model in isolation. The assumption shapes behavior across the field, since companies invest heavily in pre-release red teaming, researchers work to patch the brittleness of alignment techniques, some advocates push to restrict open-weight models on the theory that they pose unique risk, and policymakers look for a training-compute threshold above which intervention is justified, landing somewhat arbitrarily on 10^26 FLOPs for lack of a principled alternative. The authors argue that these efforts are inherently limited, because, with a few exceptions, safety questions cannot be meaningfully asked or answered at the model level. Safety depends on the context and environment of deployment, and a deployment context has to be specified before a safety question is even well formed. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>

Three examples show that the safety-relevant information lives in context rather than in the model. A phishing email is structurally just a persuasive email, and what makes it an attack is the destination of a link or the payload of an attachment, content the generating model never had, so the only way to make a model refuse phishing emails is to make it refuse persuasive emails in general, which breaks legitimate uses such as marketing. Concerns about models helping bioterrorists have the same structure, since most synthetic-biology information is already public, a claim the authors source to RAND, and the hard parts of an attack, namely sourcing materials, culturing pathogens safely and disseminating them, lie outside what a model could gate, while nearly all of the model's assistance would look like ordinary bioengineering education. Disinformation shows the pattern from another angle, because even a hypothetically perfect model that refuses every request for false information remains useful to a campaign, since true but misleading information is far more effective, up to 50x in one cited study of vaccine hesitancy, so an adversary can obtain accurate content, such as a summary of a real news story, and add the misleading framing outside the model's view. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>

The authors bound the scope of the argument. It applies most directly to misuse, both malicious, as in the examples above, and non-malicious, such as students using a model to cheat on homework, where the model again lacks the context to know whether a task is an assignment. Other failures, namely bias and toxicity, accidents, reward hacking and adversarial inputs such as prompt injection, are different problems to which the argument applies less strongly, and no separate analysis is offered. A few misuse cases are genuine exceptions because the content is intrinsically problematic regardless of use, such as AI-generated child sexual abuse material, where the content itself is the harm rather than something downstream, and verbatim reproduction of memorized copyrighted material, and for these model-level refusal makes sense. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>

Four recommendations follow. First, defenses against misuse should sit primarily outside the model, at the downstream attack surface where misuse becomes harmful, and the best defenses against phishing, whether a human or an LLM wrote the email, remain email scanners and URL blacklists that have improved for roughly two decades, whereas relying on alignment is predicted to produce both false negatives, misuse the model fails to prevent, and false positives, with the model acting as an overzealous censor of innocuous requests, because it lacks the context to tell them apart. Second, the debate over open versus closed release should be reframed around rigorously assessed marginal risk, the additional risk that a model's release adds relative to existing models and non-AI technology and to defenses that already exist outside the model, and the authors cite their own contribution to such a framework, which found low marginal cybersecurity risk from open models for automated vulnerability detection but substantial marginal risk for enabling non-consensual intimate imagery. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>

Third, red teaming should be refocused, since the answer to whether a model can be misused is essentially always yes, and its more useful function is tracking the advancing frontier of what adversaries can accomplish with current models, for example whether a model can now automate an entire multi-step cyberattack chain, so that it works as an early-warning system that informs which downstream defenses to build, rather than as a gate on whether a model ships. Fourth, red teaming for misuse capability should be led by third parties with incentives distinct from the developer's, because a developer has a structural disincentive to build and disclose the most effective offensive pipeline on its own model, since success would force it to admit publicly that it is releasing something it knows can be misused without a way to prevent it, whereas a company that does not look hard avoids the admission. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>

The authors suggest that the safety-as-a-model-property framing has persisted because it would be convenient if true. It would let companies make a clean technical determination that a model is safe enough to release, let researchers apply a familiar toolkit of alignment methods and give courts and regulators a cleaner liability question, under which companies are liable only when model-level guarantees fail. Accepting that there is no purely technical fix for misuse means that responsibility is distributed messily across models, deployment context and downstream defenses, with no consensus on how to allocate liability, a harder problem than the one the framing lets everyone pretend to solve. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>

System-level safety depends on independent evaluation, and the second argument starts from a premise shared across AI companies, academic researchers and civil society, that independent risk assessment of deployed models is an essential accountability mechanism, distinct from and complementary to pre-release red teaming done internally or by a few hand-selected experts. Independent post-release evaluation, sometimes called third-party algorithmic auditing, has a record of surfacing what internal testing misses, including vulnerabilities in low-resource languages, techniques for bypassing safety measures and many jailbreaks that coax a model past its restrictions. <span class="src">A safe harbor for AI evaluation and red teaming</span>

The structural problem is that the terms of service of popular models, including those of OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Inflection, Meta and Midjourney, generally prohibit exactly this research. They are written to deter malicious actors, but in practice they also discourage good-faith safety research, because companies retain the right to suspend accounts or pursue legal action, with limited transparency into enforcement and no formal mechanism for justification or appeal, and losing an account can derail unrelated research that depends on it. As a result only a small group of researchers selected by the companies have clear legal authorization to evaluate models already deployed to millions of users. Existing protections do not close the gap, since bug bounties with legal protections at OpenAI, Google and Meta are scoped to traditional security issues such as unauthorized account access, and companies such as Meta and Anthropic reserve discretion over whether a researcher acted in good faith, a standard that can itself chill legitimate research. Cohere, which explicitly permits intentional stress testing and adversarial attacks with appropriate disclosure, and OpenAI, which expanded its safe harbor to model-vulnerability and academic safety research in response to an early draft of the proposal, are partial exceptions, although the exact scope of their coverage remains ambiguous. <span class="src">A safe harbor for AI evaluation and red teaming</span>

The remedy has two complementary parts. A legal safe harbor would protect good-faith, public-interest evaluation from liability, in particular under the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act and Section 1201 of the Digital Millennium Copyright Act, both historically used against security researchers and both live risks in AI, as OpenAI's attempt to dismiss the New York Times lawsuit partly by framing the Times' research as unauthorized hacking shows, and the proposal is modeled on a Knight First Amendment Institute proposal for research on social media platforms. A technical safe harbor would address enforcement mechanisms such as account suspension and rate limiting, which operate independently of legal risk and can deter research even when a researcher is confident of winning in court. Both are bounded, applying only to research that follows established vulnerability disclosure practices, assessed after the fact, so research done in bad faith or without responsible disclosure would fail the test and enforcement against malicious misuse stays intact, since bad-faith actors never had protection. <span class="src">A safe harbor for AI evaluation and red teaming</span>

The technical safe harbor is harder to implement than it sounds, because it requires distinguishing legitimate research from malicious activity in something closer to real time, before the after-the-fact legal test can apply. The proposal suggests scaled-up, impartially reviewed researcher access programs, with review in advance or promptly when a suspension looks unfair, ideally involving independent third parties rather than leaving the determination solely to the company being scrutinized. <span class="src">A safe harbor for AI evaluation and red teaming</span>
