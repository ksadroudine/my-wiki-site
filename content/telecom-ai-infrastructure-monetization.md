## Summary

McKinsey's four paths by which telecom operators can turn their physical footprint into AI infrastructure revenue — connecting new data centers with fiber, selling intelligent network services, leasing unused space and power, and building a GPU-as-a-Service business. Covers the operating-model shifts — sales, partnerships, financial evaluation, communications — each path requires.

## Concept

Telecom operators are positioned to take on a genuinely new role in the AI era: not merely providing the connectivity that lets people and enterprises reach AI services, but directly building and monetizing the fiber, physical space, power, and GPU compute capacity that AI workloads themselves require. This positioning follows from operators' existing strengths — geographic reach and decades of experience managing large-scale networks under variable demand — mapped against a genuinely large and growing need, with global data-center demand projected to more than triple by 2030 as generative and agentic AI applications scale. The strategic urgency is real: absent action, operators risk falling further behind hyperscalers and other new entrants who have already captured most of the value from the last decade's growth in data consumption while telco revenues stayed largely flat. <span class="src">AI infrastructure — A new growth avenue for telco operators</span>

Four distinct paths, pursuable individually or in combination depending on an operator's regional market structure, asset base, and risk appetite, define the opportunity. Path 1, connecting new data centers with fiber, is estimated as a $30 billion to $50 billion global market: colocation providers and hyperscalers plan to build more than 2,600 new data centers by the early 2030s, and every one needs fiber connectivity either to other data centers or to end users. Operators hold a structural advantage in many markets — in much of Europe and Asia-Pacific, a telco operator license is legally required to lay fiber, giving incumbents first-mover choice over which national and metro markets to serve directly versus lease to hyperscalers — while hyperscalers themselves often prefer to lease existing "dark fiber" (unlit, unused fiber capacity) from multiple providers to build redundancy and scale connectivity faster than they could by building from scratch. Path 2, intelligent network services, addresses enterprises' growing need for more flexible, controllable networking as they run increasingly complex AI workloads on the cloud, including managing the $70 billion to $80 billion in annual cloud data-egress fees enterprises currently absorb; for telcos, this represents a potential path to reverse a decade of declining B2B wireline revenue by shifting from monthly usage/capacity-based pricing toward more lucrative value-based pricing. The catch is that this next generation of software-defined networking is still young enough that no settled market definition exists for which features and business models actually work, meaning operators pursuing this path need genuine investment in market research and software development, and must resolve build-versus-partner and monetization-model questions before committing significant resources. <span class="src">AI infrastructure — A new growth avenue for telco operators</span>

Path 3, converting unused data-center or central-office space and power into revenue, is presented as the lowest-risk entry point for operators that haven't yet exited the data-center business, since new data-center construction typically takes five or more years and many power grids are already running at capacity — a structural bottleneck that gives operators with existing underutilized space a multi-year window to capture hyperscaler, colocation, or GPUaaS-provider demand with comparatively minimal new investment, whether by acting as a straightforward landlord (colocation model) or entering a revenue-sharing arrangement where the operator commits some capital while the tenant brings the business and compute. The practical constraint is technical: AI-workload data centers typically need at least 500 kilowatts of power capacity to be economically attractive, and server racks built for gen-AI workloads pack enough density that spaces designed for conventional air cooling often require retrofitting to liquid cooling — meaning operators should prioritize facilities needing minimal retrofitting for their first deals, or only retrofit once a firm tenant commitment is in hand, to manage risk. Path 4, building a GPU-as-a-Service (GPUaaS) business — renting remote access to high-performance GPUs by the hour (spot) or longer term (reserved) for AI training, inferencing, or sovereign-AI use cases — addresses an estimated $35 billion to $70 billion addressable market by 2030 (excluding the share hyperscalers themselves will capture), with projected returns on invested capital of 6 to 14 percent depending heavily on an operator's existing data-center asset base and local electricity supply and cost. This is the highest capital-intensity, highest-risk path of the four, given uncertain demand, direct hyperscaler competition, fast technology cycles, and the possibility that GPU prices fall as supply increases; the recommended lower-risk entry is a modest initial investment focused on securing anchor tenants and building a partner ecosystem, rather than committing to full-scale capacity up front. Sovereign-AI demand — driven by government scrutiny of data security and a preference for nationally-controlled AI infrastructure — is specifically highlighted as a favorable tailwind for this path in markets with supportive regulatory frameworks. <span class="src">AI infrastructure — A new growth avenue for telco operators</span>

Whichever combination of paths an operator chooses, capturing the opportunity requires operating-model shifts across four dimensions. Sales strategy often needs a dedicated, specialized sales organization distinct from existing teams — hyperscaler-facing deals (fiber connectivity, space and power) demand fast design turnaround and regular cost analysis that a generalist B2B sales team isn't built for, while GPUaaS sales to large enterprises requires shifting from a traditional solution-selling posture to an advisory-led approach offering education, feasibility assessment, and proof-of-concept support. Partnerships need to be built deliberately and quickly: GPU chipmakers, data-center providers, data/AI platform vendors, systems integrators, power suppliers, and specialized retrofitting contractors each contribute a piece of full-stack capability an operator is unlikely to build entirely in-house fast enough to beat competitors to market. Financial evaluation for these opportunities often requires nontraditional methods — assessing a fiber route's cumulative future demand across multiple data centers rather than just the initial customer's return, or negotiating with colocation providers to limit their number of connectivity partners (favoring higher volume with fewer providers) to improve deal economics for everyone involved — and operators pursuing GPU-ready facilities specifically need to evaluate capital and operating costs across five dimensions: compute, power, cooling, networking, and memory. Finally, proactive communication matters in a genuinely competitive, fast-moving space: operators that get ahead of the public narrative about their role in the AI value chain — educating investors, enterprise customers, and industry analysts about their strategy and value-add rather than waiting to be asked — are positioning themselves as credible players before competitors define the market narrative first. <span class="src">AI infrastructure — A new growth avenue for telco operators</span>
