## Summary

The Economist argues that America's affordability crisis is mostly a mirage, because real wages have risen steadily for a decade and are near record highs across income groups, especially for the poorest. Perceptions diverge from the data because people focus on price levels, which are about 25 percent higher than before the pandemic, instead of on rates of change, and because they credit wage gains to their own skill while blaming price rises on outside forces. There are real exceptions, however, in housing in superstar cities, in mortgage rates and in electricity. Political anger over costs is real, polling shows strong support for price caps, and the Economist warns that price controls distort markets and tend to become permanent.

## Concept

The Economist's year-end 2025 briefing and leader address why "affordability" became the central political slogan for the 2026 midterms even though the data do not show a household purchasing-power crisis. Donald Trump won the 2024 election partly by promising to lower prices, an undeliverable pledge since economy-wide price falls are unprecedented outside deep recessions, and his tariffs have raised prices further; Democrats, from Zohran Mamdani's rent-freeze campaign to moderate governors' utility-cost pledges, have now adopted the theme. The administration's response — denying affordability is a problem — is politically ineffective, though on the economics it has a point: real wages have kept rising since the pandemic inflation, which was driven more by over-stimulus than supply shocks, and low earners gained most in the tight labour market. <span class="src">America’s affordability crisis is (mostly) a mirage</span>

Category by category, the briefing finds the strongest real grievances in housing and borrowing costs. In superstar cities, scarce land and restrictive zoning make ownership punishing; elsewhere most counties were affordable by the 30%-of-income rule of thumb until mortgage rates rose in 2022. Electricity is the one essential that has outpaced inflation, probably due to grid modernisation and rising liquefied-natural-gas exports rather than data centres. Inflation itself is running about 2.8%, a modest overshoot of the Fed's 2% target, so the persisting anger needs other explanations: price levels remain about 25% higher than before the pandemic, people attribute wage gains to their own merit while blaming prices on external forces (Stefanie Stantcheva's surveys), price volatility meant some purchase was always spiking, and borrowing costs matter more to sentiment than conventional inflation measures capture (Harvard and IMF research). Stanford researchers' estimated one-year "half-life" for inflation anger suggests it should have faded by now, but the shock after decades of flat prices appears to be different. <span class="src">America’s affordability crisis is (mostly) a mirage</span>

The accompanying leader generalises the argument: affordability combines phantom problems (milk, haircuts, fondness for 2019 prices) with real but affluence-driven ones. Services become costlier as an economy richer in goods-productivity shifts spending toward them, and asset prices have outrun wages (illustrated by two equally paid people, one of whom inherited $1 million and now holds $4 million in an index fund and competes for positional goods like a view apartment). These are hard for policymakers because solutions — easing housing and wind-farm permitting, ending occupational-licensing excess, lowering tariffs — are favoured in principle but opposed locally by protected interests. The danger the Economist stresses is misdiagnosis: price controls (which Nixon introduced and abandoned), a fiscal boost from tax cuts combined with pressure on the Fed to cut rates, and Trump's proposed $2,000 tariff-funded cheques, which together would tax imports and then hand out money to buy them. Ex-Biden aide Bharat Ramamurti and Neale Mahoney's "affordability conundrum" (voters want immediate relief while supply-side tools work slowly) is cited as the argument for pairing reforms with price controls, which the Economist rejects as perilous. The briefing concludes the only politically unsatisfying path is time, with inflation expectations resetting and real wages continuing upward. <span class="src">The truth about affordability</span> <span class="src">America’s affordability crisis is (mostly) a mirage</span>

## Open Questions

- The Economist's data-centre finding rests on a single cross-state comparison, and the source does not address local utility-rate effects within data-centre-dense areas.
- It is unclear how durable the "mirage" conclusion is if the labour market weakens, which the briefing itself flags would shift attention to unemployment.
