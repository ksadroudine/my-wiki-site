## Summary

McKinsey's four cornerstones of corporate finance hold that growth and return on invested capital create value, that only cash flow creates it, that share price tracks market expectations as well as performance, and that value depends on who manages a business. The concept applies them to growth, portfolio management, acquisitions and capital structure, and a study of stable firms shows that return on capital alone can drive market-beating returns.

## Concept

Value, from McKinsey's corporate finance practice, organizes the discipline of value-based management around four cornerstones intended to guide both strategic and financial decisions. The first and foundational cornerstone is that value is created when a company invests capital to generate future cash flows at a rate of return exceeding the cost of that capital, meaning the combination of revenue growth and return on invested capital (ROIC) — not either alone — is what drives value creation; the source's general prescription is that high-ROIC businesses should prioritize further growth, since each new unit of growth compounds value efficiently, while low-ROIC businesses should first improve their returns, since growing a low-return business merely multiplies value destruction. The second cornerstone, the conservation of value, follows directly from the first as a corollary: value for shareholders comes only from genuinely increasing cash flows, not from rearranging how investors' claims on those cash flows are structured (financial engineering, share buybacks framed as value creation, and similar restructurings do not by themselves create value, whatever their other merits). <span class="src">Value</span>

The third cornerstone, the "expectations treadmill," explains a phenomenon that trips up many investors and executives alike: a company's share-price performance is driven by changes in the market's expectations about future performance, not by the company's actual performance in isolation, meaning a good company and a good investment are frequently not the same thing. If a company's future excellence is already fully priced in, merely delivering that excellence produces only ordinary shareholder returns going forward, since matching high expectations is already the baseline — while a weaker-performing company facing low expectations can be a better investment precisely because those expectations are easier to beat. The fourth cornerstone, "best owner," holds that a given business's value is not a fixed, intrinsic property of the business itself but depends on who owns and manages it and what strategy they pursue, since different owners will generate different cash flows from the identical set of assets; this implies executives should continually reassess both which businesses they are the best owner of (and should retain or grow) and which businesses others could manage better (and should be divested), treating portfolio composition as an ongoing strategic decision rather than a settled one. <span class="src">Value</span>

Growth itself is decomposed into four distinct types — gains in market share, price increases, growth in the underlying market the company already competes in, and acquisitions — each of which creates a different amount of value and provokes a different competitive response, since a useful way to assess any specific growth move is to ask who loses as a result and how those losers are likely to retaliate. Sustaining high growth is described as structurally harder than sustaining high ROIC, because most individual products follow a natural S-curve toward market maturity and eventual decline; to keep total revenue growing, a company faces a "portfolio treadmill" in which each maturing product must be replaced by a similarly-sized new source of growth just to stand still, which requires sustained experimentation and a longer investment time horizon than many companies are willing to fund. On acquisitions specifically, the source cites McKinsey research covering roughly 1,400 deals finding that the combined value of acquirer and target rises only about 4 percent on average — and that acquiring shareholders in particular rarely capture much of even that modest gain — identifying five archetypal strategic rationales that are actually associated with value creation for the acquirer: improving the target company's own performance, consolidating excess capacity out of an industry, accelerating market access for either party's existing products, acquiring skills or technology faster or more cheaply than building them internally, and picking likely winners early and helping them scale; deals outside these five archetypes (rollups, "transformational" mergers, simple bargain-hunting) are described as rarely creating value in practice, even though they remain common justifications offered by executives. <span class="src">Value</span>

On capital structure, the source argues the trade-off between financial flexibility (having enough capital cushion to pursue opportunities and weather shocks) and financial discipline (the market pressure that comes with leverage) is the decisive consideration in setting a company's debt-equity mix — far outweighing the tax benefits of debt financing that finance textbooks often emphasize — and recommends that most large companies target at least an investment-grade credit rating, since falling below that threshold typically brings materially higher borrowing costs and restrictive covenants. Finally, on investor relations, the source recommends explicitly segmenting a company's investor base into intrinsic investors (who conduct genuine due diligence and hold long-horizon positions based on their own view of a company's fundamental value), traders (who bet on short-term price movements without forming a view on intrinsic value), and mechanical or index investors (who follow fixed rules or benchmarks); because intrinsic investors are identified as the group that ultimately drives share price over periods longer than about a month, the source recommends that management concentrate its investor communication effort specifically on that segment rather than attempting to satisfy the market as an undifferentiated whole, and recommends replacing traditional earnings-per-share guidance with disclosure of the underlying operational drivers (volume targets, revenue targets, cost initiatives) that intrinsic investors actually need to form an independent view. <span class="src">Value</span>

<span class="src">Growth Isn’t the Only Way for Companies to Create Value</span> (HBR, Ulrich Pidun et al., June 2025) supplies an empirical counterpoint that operationalizes exactly one half of this concept's foundational cornerstone — that value comes from growth *and* ROIC — for the specific case where growth is structurally unavailable. Studying more than 10,000 companies across North America, Europe, and Japan over 20 years, the authors identified 172 "stable" firms with steady, near-zero revenue growth throughout the period; these firms delivered shareholder returns comparable to the broader market but at 12% lower volatility, were half as likely as the average firm to suffer a severe value collapse (losing 90%+ of market capitalization), and averaged nearly 100 years of age, evidence that low growth need not mean high fragility. Critically, 57 of the 172 — one in three — actually outperformed the market on total shareholder return, and these outperformers did not conform to a single industry or business-model profile, appearing across both B2B and B2C, products and services, though less often in fast-growing sectors. What they shared instead was governance and discipline: a full 25% had an owner with a controlling interest, compared with less than 8% of the S&P 1500 broadly, and they systematically avoided the large-scale, growth-chasing M&A that fails 70-75% of the time — both patterns consistent with this concept's own "best owner" cornerstone and its skepticism of financial engineering as a substitute for genuine cash-flow growth. The 57 outperformers pursued four distinct strategies, none requiring revenue growth, each mapping onto a different lever within this concept's ROIC-and-growth framework. The asset-light play shifts from physical products toward services and software to deepen existing customer relationships and lower capital intensity — Siemens' decade-long pivot toward software-as-a-service and digital-twin technology lifted its average EBIT margin by 8 percentage points (driven by 50% lower capex and 25% lower cost of sales) and delivered a 12% annualized TSR. The gross-margin play moves upmarket through genuine quality improvement rather than cost-cutting or brand alone, strengthening pricing power in a way that is difficult for competitors to erode — Morgan Advanced Materials, a UK ceramics and carbon-materials manufacturer serving aerospace and semiconductor customers, doubled its margins over 20 years through superior thermal and mechanical performance, without growing revenue in real terms. The balance-sheet play grows the asset base through vertical integration to capture a larger share of an existing value chain rather than expanding into new markets — Whitbread's Premier Inn, unlike hotel chains that franchise to accelerate growth, owns and directly operates all its hotels including centralized digital booking and revenue management, delivering a 10% annualized TSR despite the resulting constraint on expansion speed. And the dividend play prioritizes consistent, bond-like payouts over chasing dividend growth — railcar lessor GATX has paid uninterrupted quarterly dividends since 1919, and its dividend volatility 1.5 standard deviations below the sample average drove a 12% annualized TSR almost entirely through cash-flow contribution and valuation-multiple expansion. The article cautions that these levers are ultimately finite — margins cannot exceed 100% and dividend volatility cannot fall below zero — so stability strategies buy time and reduce risk rather than permanently obviating the need to eventually revisit growth. <span class="src">Growth Isn’t the Only Way for Companies to Create Value</span>
