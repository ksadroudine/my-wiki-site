## Summary

LLMs boost creative ideation through persistence (generating many variations without fatigue) and flexibility (combining distant concepts), but only when users deliberately break from the linear, single-best-answer prompting habits carried over from search engines. Getting genuinely original output requires specific techniques — fine-tuning, persona modifiers, chain-of-thought prompting, and treating AI as a collaborator rather than a tool — and even then LLMs currently lag humans at big, breakthrough ideas. A companion field study of four companies extends the same ideation techniques across the full product development pipeline, from early concept generation through customer validation to complex-engineering natural-language interfaces.

## Highlights

- Most people prompt generative AI the same way they search Google — asking a direct question with filtered criteria for a single best answer — which reproduces a "new status quo" of linear, predictable thinking rather than tapping into what makes AI collaboration genuinely generative; a deliberate "mindshift" is required to treat AI as a creative partner rather than a faster search box.
- Idea generation and brainstorming was the fastest-growing enterprise gen AI use case between 2024 and 2025 in one adoption survey, jumping nine positions in a single year (+12 percentage points) to rank among the top five most common use cases.
- Two pathways underlie human creativity that LLMs emulate through different mechanisms: persistence (focused generation of many variations within a narrow space, which LLMs achieve through tireless productivity — research shows the more ideas an LLM generates, the more original the pool becomes, up to a plateau) and flexibility (combining distant concepts, which LLMs achieve through the semantic breadth of training on enormous, diverse datasets).
- Because LLMs are trained to predict the most likely continuation of a prompt, their default output gravitates toward familiar patterns — the opposite of what ideation needs — so techniques like persona modifiers ("think like Steve Jobs"), hybrid prompting (breaking generation into varied smaller prompts and combining results), chain-of-thought prompting (explicit brainstorm-edit-summarize steps), and higher temperature settings are needed to deliberately extract rarer, more unconventional ideas.
- Fine-tuning on specialized content, few-shot prompting with high-quality examples, and retrieval-augmented generation on real-time domain data all help align AI-generated ideas with actual business context and appropriateness, not just raw originality.
- LLMs can occupy either of two ideation postures: "lead ideator" (the Designer, versioning and multivariate-testing content variants; the Writer, sharpening how ideas are communicated) or "thought partner" (the Interviewer, asking Socratic questions to surface blind spots; the Actor, roughly simulating customer responses — though personas may skew toward dominant online voices rather than representing all groups accurately).
- A genuine tension in AI-assisted ideation: while individual users may generate more original ideas with AI help, the overall diversity of ideas across a group of AI-assisted users can narrow, since many people converging on similar prompting patterns and similar training data tends to make everyone's output look more alike — raising the competitive bar for what counts as genuinely differentiated.
- Twelve concrete practices for training oneself to prompt more creatively include: daily open-ended "exploratory prompting" instead of goal-directed questions; framing prompts as "What if" and "How might we" rather than direct asks; deliberately embracing ambiguity ("What might I be overlooking?"); chaining prompts iteratively to deepen ideas rather than stopping at the first answer; thinking metaphorically or analogically; requesting multiple expert or fictional personas' perspectives simultaneously; and asking impossibility-framed questions ("What would completely eliminate the need for X?").
- Both sources independently converge on treating AI personas — role-play prompts invoking a named expert, historical figure, or archetype — as one of the single highest-leverage techniques for breaking out of an AI model's default, most-likely-next-token output and reaching genuinely unexpected creative territory.
- Current-generation LLMs are consistently better at producing a high volume of small, incremental ideas than at generating truly big, breakthrough ones — implying a strategic division of labor where AI takes the lead when the goal is broad exploration or volume, while humans should stay in the driver's seat when the goal is a small number of genuinely bold, high-impact ideas.
- Boston design agency Loft used GPT-4 for early product-feature ideation and Midjourney for visual concept sketches, deliberately tolerating hallucinations at this stage since the team was only looking for ideas — but paused generative AI entirely once development moved into engineering, where outputs need to be reliable, and used it again later specifically to analyze and cluster customer-feedback transcripts, cutting the company's overall product development time in half.
- Czech innovation firm Creative Dock fine-tunes pretrained LLMs into AI agents that simulate qualitative customer interviews across specific personas, using proprietary market-research data for rapid concept testing — reporting a 30% technical-development efficiency gain, a 40% graphic-design efficiency gain, tripled content-creation speed, and 50% year-over-year growth without adding full-time headcount.
- Siemens paired its sophisticated Simcenter engineering-simulation software (which normally requires long training ramp-up and specialist expertise to operate) with a GenAI natural-language interface, HiSimcenter, letting far more engineers set up and interpret complex physics simulations directly — yielding a more than 50% increase in modeling efficiency, but requiring Siemens to train selected employees to assess training-data quality first, since a reliable "ground truth" was the critical bottleneck in building the hybrid system.
- Three distinct GenAI roles recur across the product development pipeline studied: pure ideation/design tools (tolerant of hallucination, since the goal is volume of raw ideas), customer-insight and concept-validation tools (requiring fine-tuning on proprietary data for context-specific reliability), and natural-language interfaces to existing precision engineering systems (requiring the highest data-quality discipline, since incorrect simulation results carry real physical or financial consequences) — the right level of AI trust and human oversight differs meaningfully across all three.

## Concept

<span class="src">Train Your Brain to Work Creatively with Gen AI</span> (HBR, November 2024) diagnoses a specific failure mode in how most people already use generative AI: because everyday tools like Google search train users to ask direct questions for a single filtered best answer, that same linear habit carries over into AI prompting, reproducing predictable, incremental output even from a technology capable of much more. The article's core prescription is a "mindshift" — treating AI as a creative collaborator rather than a faster version of an existing tool — illustrated through a simple example (asking an AI for dinner recipes from available ingredients, then pushing the request toward increasingly unconventional constraints like "in the style of a celebrity chef from any century") that shows how quickly a linear query can be redirected toward genuinely generative territory. The article's substantive contribution is twelve concrete exercises for training this habit: daily open-ended "exploratory prompting" sessions; reframing direct questions as "What if" and "How might we" prompts; deliberately embracing ambiguity rather than precision; using prompts to explore rather than immediately solve; chaining prompts iteratively rather than stopping at a first answer; thinking metaphorically or analogically; requesting multiple perspectives (an artist's, a scientist's, a philosopher's) on the same problem simultaneously; experimenting with role-play prompts invoking named experts or fictional personas; asking for outright impossibilities to reframe a problem; treating the AI itself as a design partner ("How would you, as an AI, design this?"); holding a weekly dedicated future-oriented prompting session; and keeping a running journal of unusually valuable "breakthrough prompts" to study over time. <span class="src">Train Your Brain to Work Creatively with Gen AI</span>

<span class="src">When Used Correctly, LLMs Can Unlock More Creative Ideas</span> (HBR, December 2025) supplies the theoretical and empirical grounding the first article's practical exercises implicitly rest on. Drawing on classic creativity science plus emerging generative-AI-specific research, the authors define ideation quality along two axes — originality (how much an idea deviates from what exists) and appropriateness (whether it's actually feasible) — and map two classic pathways to human creativity onto LLM capability: persistence (focused, exhaustive generation of variations within a narrow space, which LLMs achieve through tireless productivity — research cited shows originality across a generated pool increases with volume up to a plateau) and flexibility (combining distant concepts, which LLMs achieve via the semantic breadth of their training data). Because LLMs are fundamentally trained to predict the statistically most likely continuation of a prompt, their unguided default tends toward familiar rather than novel territory — the opposite of what ideation needs — so the article catalogs specific counteracting techniques: fine-tuning on brand-specific content, few-shot prompting with curated high-quality examples, and retrieval-augmented generation for appropriateness; and persona modifiers, hybrid prompting (many varied smaller prompts combined), chain-of-thought prompting, and elevated temperature settings for originality. The article proposes a taxonomy of ideation "roles" an LLM can play: as lead ideator, either the Designer (versioning content variants, running multivariate tests, surfacing hidden confounding variables) or the Writer (sharpening how an idea is communicated); or as thought partner, either the Interviewer (Socratic questioning that surfaces blind spots) or the Actor (roughly simulating customer responses, though these personas risk skewing toward dominant online voices rather than genuinely representative ones — a limitation the authors connect to a companion HBR piece on synthetic personas in market research). The article's central tension, empirically distinct from anything in the companion piece, is that while AI assistance can make individual ideators more original, it can simultaneously make groups of AI-assisted ideators converge on more similar ideas overall, since shared prompting patterns and shared training data pull output toward the same statistical center — meaning organizations that want genuine differentiation need to actively counteract this convergence (through techniques like hybrid prompting and persona variation) rather than assume individual-level gains automatically add up to group-level originality. The authors also note that current LLMs consistently produce more small, incremental ideas than big, breakthrough ones, implying humans should retain the lead role specifically when the goal is bold, high-impact innovation rather than broad exploration. <span class="src">When Used Correctly, LLMs Can Unlock More Creative Ideas</span>

Read together, the two articles describe the same underlying opportunity from complementary registers: the earlier, practitioner-voiced piece supplies twelve concrete habits for individuals to build the "mindshift" that unlocks creative AI collaboration, while the later, research-grounded piece explains mechanistically why those habits work (LLMs' persistence and flexibility, and the specific prompting techniques that counteract their default pull toward familiar output) and adds the crucial organizational-scale caveat that individual creative gains from AI can come at the cost of overall group-level diversity if left unmanaged.

<span class="src">When Generative AI Meets Product Development</span> (MIT SMR, Tucker J. Marion, Mahdi Srour, and Frank Piller, July 2024) extends this concept's ideation-technique focus across the full arc of product development, drawn from field research and dozens of manager interviews across four companies. In the earliest, most exploratory stage, Boston design agency Loft used GPT-4 to suggest new product features from known customer preferences and Midjourney to iterate visual concepts from designers' initial sketches, deliberately tolerating the risk of hallucination at this stage since the goal was pure idea volume rather than precision — directly illustrating this concept's "persistence" mechanism in a real product-design context. Loft explicitly paused its use of generative AI once the process moved into engineering and prototyping, where trust in reliable output becomes essential, then reintroduced it later to transcribe and analyze consumer focus-group feedback on physical prototypes, again avoiding open-ended questions that might invite hallucination; the company estimates this combination has cut its overall product development time in half, and used it to generate 50 new concept variations for a guitar toy that would previously have taken many hours of manual sketching. Czech innovation firm Creative Dock extends the pipeline into concept validation specifically: rather than general-purpose ideation, its AI agents are fine-tuned on proprietary market-research data to simulate qualitative customer interviews with specific personas, letting teams test alternative value propositions and gather structured feedback long before a real customer is involved — reporting a 30% technical-development efficiency gain, a 40% graphic-design efficiency gain, tripled content-creation speed, and 50% year-over-year company growth without adding headcount, directly paralleling this concept's fine-tuning and few-shot techniques for improving appropriateness rather than just originality. Swiss startup FlecheTech applies the same fine-tuning logic to a narrower, higher-precision domain: a pretrained LLM fine-tuned on a database of printed-circuit-board designs lets non-engineers describe a custom circuit in plain language ("make X rotate at Y speed"), reducing the six-to-eight-week timeline a human PCB designer would normally need by more than 80%, with the largest gains for novice users specifically. Siemens supplies the highest-stakes example, pairing its Simcenter engineering-simulation software — which normally demands long training ramp-up and specialist expertise — with a GenAI natural-language front end, HiSimcenter, that lets a much broader population of engineers set up and interpret complex physics simulations directly, yielding a more than 50% increase in modeling efficiency; critically, the Siemens team found that having a reliable "ground truth" was the central bottleneck in building this kind of hybrid expert system, and responded by training a small group of selected employees specifically to assess training-data quality across major engineering task types before that data feeds the model, rather than assuming off-the-shelf data would suffice. Read against this concept's ideation-technique framework, the four companies collectively demonstrate that the right level of AI trust and human oversight varies systematically by pipeline stage: pure ideation and design tolerate (even benefit from) hallucination risk, since the value is in volume and unexpected combination; customer-insight and concept-validation tools require fine-tuning on proprietary data for genuine contextual reliability; and natural-language interfaces to precision engineering systems demand the highest data-quality discipline of all, since an incorrect simulation result carries real physical or financial consequences rather than merely being a discarded idea. <span class="src">When Generative AI Meets Product Development</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses how an organization should measure whether its AI-assisted ideation is actually producing more original output over time, versus just producing more output that individual evaluators subjectively rate as more original in the moment.
- The group-convergence problem (AI-assisted ideators producing more similar ideas even as individuals feel more creative) is identified but not resolved: neither source specifies how much hybrid prompting or persona variation is actually needed to counteract convergence, or how an organization would know if its countermeasures were working.
- The product-development field study doesn't address whether the group-convergence risk this concept's other sources identify (AI-assisted ideators converging on similar outputs) shows up in its own case studies — none of Loft, Creative Dock, FlecheTech, or Siemens report measuring idea diversity across their teams, only speed and efficiency gains.
