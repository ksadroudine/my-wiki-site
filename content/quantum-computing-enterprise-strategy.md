## Summary

Quantum computing is a general-purpose enabling technology whose business value is likely to emerge gradually through co-invention between vendors and users, so enterprises are better served by early, low-stakes experimentation than by waiting for the technology to look ready. Its commercial promise concentrates in chemistry and materials simulation, some optimization and fraud detection, while its largest security consequence is Q-Day, the point at which machines can break current public-key encryption and prompt migration to post-quantum cryptography. Investment is surging and early telecom applications such as radio network optimization and quantum-safe networking are emerging, but exponential speed-ups exist for few problem types, error correction remains unsolved at scale and credible skeptics doubt the timelines.

## Concept

The strategic case for early engagement rests on how enabling technologies create value. Quantum computing is described as a general-purpose technology in the tradition of electricity and classical computing, so its economic value is not expected to arrive suddenly once some qubit count or error rate is reached. It emerges gradually through co-invention cycles between vendors and business users, much as electricity's value depended on downstream innovations in motors and factory layouts, and computing's on complementary software and organizational process. That creates a managerial catch-22, since companies want proof the technology works before investing in experiments, yet the proof usually exists only because companies experimented. Waiting therefore sustains the uncertainty that makes waiting seem sensible, because vendors lack the demand signals needed to know what to optimize. <span class="src">Why Businesses Should Experiment With Quantum Computing Now</span>

Two examples show engagement whose value came from learning. Lockheed Martin moved in 2011 from a year of passive evaluation to a multiyear agreement of about USD 10 million for a D-Wave One quantum annealer, paired with a university research center for sustained, iterative learning. IBM's 2016 cloud release of a 5-qubit processor, which had no commercial utility alone, attracted 7,000 registered users in a week and many more over time, showing how broad, low-stakes access can accelerate an enabling technology. The recommended leadership actions are to develop internal boundary spanners who translate quantum progress into company-specific questions, to anchor learning in near-term opportunities where even modest quantum-inspired improvements justify experimentation, as with Fujitsu's Digital Annealer in logistics routing and portfolio optimization, and to create organizational space for longer-term process redesign insulated from short-term financial metrics. The contrast is between Walmart's persistent process-level investment in e-commerce and Sears's treatment of the internet as a peripheral channel bolted onto unchanged stores, since the biggest winners from an enabling technology redesign processes around what it makes newly possible. <span class="src">Why Businesses Should Experiment With Quantum Computing Now</span>

The industry landscape shows why the timing question matters. Google, IBM and Microsoft, with its Majorana 2 chip, target commercially useful machines around 2029-2030, Honeywell-backed Quantinuum's Nasdaq IPO valued it above USD 15 billion, IonQ's stock has risen more than 700% since September 2024, and venture investment reached over USD 4 billion in 2026 by late September, almost as much as all of 2025, with Oratomic raising USD 300 million, the largest first quantum round, to build a full-scale machine before 2030. McKinsey counted USD 12.6 billion invested in quantum start-ups in 2025, a six-fold rise, and the US government said it would take USD 2 billion of equity in nine companies. A sentiment reversal drove this, since Oratomic's founder had left for academia two years earlier judging practical machines too far away, but the sector remains what one investor calls a perpetual five-year technology, in which each advance reveals new constraints. <span class="src">The quantum computing revolution is closer than you think</span> <span class="src">Can quantum turn ‘magic’ into money?</span> <span class="src">Quantum computers promise mathematical superpowers</span>

Where value will appear is contested. Quantum machines are powerful but narrow, because superposition and entanglement let qubits represent exponentially many states while reading out the answer collapses the superposition, so only problems with exploitable mathematical structure benefit. Exponential speed-ups are known for only two kinds of task, breaking common encryption and simulating quantum systems, and Scott Aaronson estimates no advantage for about 90% of tasks. IonQ's chief executive argues that most near-term value will come from quadratic speed-ups, such as finishing a 24-hour bank task in 17 minutes, while Nvidia's Jensen Huang has said many optimization problems can already be solved classically, and a 2025 Fraunhofer study found classical methods usually beat the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm on real financial data, although hardware limits constrained the test. Simulation of chemistry and materials is the strongest case, since density-functional theory sometimes fails badly, as with silicon's band gap, and a hybrid quantum-classical simulation of a light-activated cancer drug by Algorithmiq, the Cleveland Clinic and IBM won a USD 2 million Wellcome prize. Backers predict profit-sharing deals with pharmaceutical and chemical firms worth hundreds of billions of dollars over a decade, and a 2026 result from researchers including Google and Oratomic staff claimed an exponential advantage for processing massive data sets in machine learning, hinting at a later link with AI. <span class="src">Can quantum turn ‘magic’ into money?</span> <span class="src">Quantum computers promise mathematical superpowers</span> <span class="src">The quantum computing revolution is closer than you think</span>

Partnerships and fraud detection are the most concrete near-term applications. Google has worked with Boehringer Ingelheim on drug discovery, Bosch on materials science, Mercedes-Benz on battery technology and Volkswagen on traffic optimization, reflecting a consensus that near-term promise lies where quantum mechanics itself models the phenomenon. In financial services, Mastercard and Oxford Quantum Circuits' experimental fraud-detection system produced fewer false positives than existing techniques and Unisys's research achieved zero false negatives in testing, addressing the weakness of conventional AI in fraud, where rare cases and shifting tactics leave too little training data. Large companies are also hedging across hardware, since Google has started a neutral-atom program and IBM bought a silicon-spin company, and the routes of superconducting circuits at Google and IBM, trapped ions at Quantinuum and IonQ, neutral atoms, photons and spin remain unsettled, with IBM targeting a 200-logical-qubit fault-tolerant chip by 2029 and DARPA's benchmarking initiative seeking to identify by 2033 which approaches can yield an industrially useful machine. <span class="src">The quantum computing revolution is closer than you think</span> <span class="src">Can quantum turn ‘magic’ into money?</span> <span class="src">Quantum computers promise mathematical superpowers</span>

The main security risk is Q-Day, the anticipated point at which quantum computers can break the cryptography based on the difficulty of factoring large prime numbers, which most digital security relies on. Shor's 1994 algorithm remains the template, and error-correction advances have reduced estimated hardware needs, with Oratomic's March 2026 work suggesting attacks might need only tens of thousands of physical qubits rather than hundreds of thousands and Google outlining breaking cryptocurrency codes in minutes with 1,200 logical qubits. Google published a zero-knowledge proof rather than its method and is migrating internally to post-quantum cryptography by 2029, against a US standards-agency recommendation of 2035. The threat already shapes behavior through harvest-now-decrypt-later collection, in which adversaries steal encrypted data today to decrypt later, and through a global standards effort, and it leaves a long tail of systems that cannot easily be upgraded, such as hospital equipment, cash machines, toll roads and sewage plants. <span class="src">The quantum computing revolution is closer than you think</span> <span class="src">Quantum computers promise mathematical superpowers</span>

Technical limits temper the enthusiasm. Current machines are highly error-prone because of noise from environmental disturbance, a working general-purpose quantum computer is estimated to need at least 1,000 logical qubits against Oxford Quantum Circuits' 16-qubit Genesis system, and credentialed skeptics argue that current error-correction approaches may never scale to the required robustness. Early claims of quantum advantage have not yet become commercial practicality, and the cautions refine rather than contradict the strategic argument, because uncertainty is exactly what makes experimentation useful. <span class="src">The quantum computing revolution is closer than you think</span> <span class="src">Can quantum turn ‘magic’ into money?</span> <span class="src">Why Businesses Should Experiment With Quantum Computing Now</span>

Telecom operators show the pattern in one industry, pursuing two largely separate tracks. On performance, SoftBank demonstrated in a 2025 proof of concept that an Ising machine, a quantum architecture suited to combinatorial optimization, could tune radio access network base station settings, yielding a 10% increase in 5G downlink speed and up to 50% more data capacity through carrier aggregation, and it intends to extend the approach to broader network architecture and operational efficiency, while Vodafone, BT Group, SK Telecom and Verizon explore their own network use cases with less public detail. Adoption accelerated in 2025, a shift not widely anticipated when attention was on AI and agentic AI. <span class="src">Quantum Computing in Telecom Picks Up Pace in 2025</span>

On security, operators are investing in quantum-safe networking, which spans Quantum Key Distribution, which uses quantum mechanical properties to detect eavesdropping on a key exchange, and Post-Quantum Cryptography, classical algorithms designed to resist quantum attack. An industry survey reported by TelecomTV found that seven of the top ten organizations leading in this area are European and that most surveyed operators intend to implement quantum-safe measures within four years, so the effort is treated as a near-term operational priority rather than a distant concern. <span class="src">Quantum Computing in Telecom Picks Up Pace in 2025</span>
