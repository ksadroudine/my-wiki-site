## Summary

Quantum computing is an enabling technology whose economic value emerges gradually through co-invention between vendors and business users, not suddenly once a technical threshold is crossed. Companies across pharmaceuticals, financial services, and fraud detection are already experimenting with today's immature, error-prone machines to build the organizational capability to act once more powerful applications become feasible. Fresh 2026 reporting adds the investment boom, the unresolved search for a profitable use case, and a sharper cryptography timeline.

## Highlights

- Quantum computing is a "general-purpose" enabling technology like electricity or classical computing, whose economic value historically has emerged gradually through repeated co-invention cycles between the technology-producing sector and complementary innovations from downstream users, not all at once when a technical milestone is crossed.
- Waiting for quantum computing to become clearly "ready" before engaging is a trap: near-term value-capture uncertainty discourages companies from experimenting, which in turn deprives vendors of the demand signals needed to know what to optimize for, sustaining the very technological uncertainty that made companies hesitant in the first place.
- Lockheed Martin moved from a year of passive evaluation to hands-on experimentation in 2011, entering a roughly $10 million multiyear agreement for a D-Wave One quantum annealing machine and co-founding a research center to build sustained institutional familiarity with the technology rather than expecting immediate transformation.
- IBM's 2016 launch of cloud-based access to a 5-qubit quantum processor — commercially useless on its own — attracted 7,000 registered users within a week and hundreds of thousands over time, illustrating how broad, low-stakes experimental access can accelerate an enabling technology's development even before it does anything commercially useful.
- Quantum computing investment already produces "quantum-inspired" innovations that run on ordinary classical hardware, such as Fujitsu's Digital Annealer (used in logistics routing and financial portfolio optimization) and improved recommendation-system and materials-discovery algorithms — meaning some returns on quantum experimentation arrive well before quantum hardware itself is commercially mature.
- Financial services and fraud detection are emerging as some of the earliest practical commercial applications: Mastercard and Oxford Quantum Circuits' experimental quantum fraud-detection system showed fewer false positives than existing techniques, and Unisys's quantum-based fraud research achieved zero false negatives while minimizing false positives — addressing a specific weakness of AI-based fraud detection, which lacks large training datasets because fraud cases are comparatively rare and tactics constantly shift.
- Google has paired with Boehringer Ingelheim on drug discovery, Bosch on materials science, Mercedes-Benz on battery technology, and Volkswagen on traffic optimization, reflecting industry consensus that quantum computing's near-term commercial promise concentrates in chemistry, materials science, and complex optimization problems where quantum mechanics itself models the underlying phenomena.
- "Q-Day" — the anticipated point at which quantum computers become powerful enough to break the prime-number-factorization cryptography underlying most modern digital security — is driving "harvest now, decrypt later" concerns (adversaries stealing encrypted data today to decrypt once quantum capability arrives) and a global push toward post-quantum, quantum-resistant cryptographic standards.
- Serious technical obstacles remain — existing machines are highly error-prone due to "noise" from environmental disturbances, a working general-purpose quantum computer is estimated to require at least 1,000 logical qubits against OQC's 16-qubit Genesis system as one example of the current gap, and credentialed skeptics argue current error-correction approaches may never scale to the robustness required.
- Companies that ultimately benefit most from enabling technologies are those that redesign business processes around what the technology makes newly possible, rather than merely bolting it onto existing operations — illustrated by the difference between Walmart's persistent, process-level investment in e-commerce and Sears's treatment of the internet as a peripheral sales channel layered onto unchanged brick-and-mortar operations.
- More than $4 billion of venture capital went into quantum companies in 2026 by late September (PitchBook), almost as much as all of 2025, with Quantinuum's US IPO and IQM's Finnish listing marking the sector's first public offerings; Oratomic raised $300 million, the largest first quantum round, aiming for a full-scale machine before 2030. <span class="src">Can quantum turn ‘magic’ into money?</span>
- Algorithms offering exponential speed-ups exist for only two task types — breaking common encryption and simulating quantum systems — so investors question which technologies and companies will profit; IonQ's CEO argues most near-term value will come from quadratic speed-ups, such as finishing a 24-hour task in 17 minutes for banks, while Nvidia's Jensen Huang has said many optimisation problems can already be solved classically. <span class="src">Can quantum turn ‘magic’ into money?</span>
- McKinsey reported $12.6 billion invested in quantum start-ups in 2025, a six-fold increase, and the US government said it would take $2 billion of equity in nine quantum companies; Scott Aaronson estimates a quantum computer offers no advantage over a conventional one for 90% of tasks. <span class="src">Quantum computers promise mathematical superpowers</span>
- Oratomic's March 2026 error-correction work suggested attacks on existing cryptography might need only tens of thousands of physical qubits rather than hundreds of thousands, and Google outlined breaking cryptocurrency codes in minutes with 1,200 logical qubits, publishing a zero-knowledge proof rather than its method and targeting internal post-quantum migration by 2029. <span class="src">Quantum computers promise mathematical superpowers</span>
- Hardware routes remain unsettled (superconducting circuits at Google and IBM, trapped ions at Quantinuum and IonQ, neutral atoms, photons, spin), IBM targets a 200-logical-qubit fault-tolerant chip by 2029, and DARPA's benchmarking initiative seeks to identify which approaches could yield an industrially useful machine by 2033. <span class="src">Quantum computers promise mathematical superpowers</span>

## Concept

<span class="src">Why Businesses Should Experiment With Quantum Computing Now</span> (MIT SMR, May 2026) makes the core strategic argument for engaging with quantum computing well before the technology matures. Framing quantum as a "general-purpose" enabling technology in the tradition of electricity and classical computing, the article argues its economic value will not arrive suddenly once some qubit-count or error-rate threshold is crossed, but will instead emerge gradually through co-invention cycles between vendors and business users — much as electricity's value depended on downstream innovations in motors and factory layouts, and classical computing's depended on complementary advances in software and organizational process. This creates a managerial catch-22: companies want proof the technology works before they invest in experimentation, but that proof typically only becomes available because companies experiment. The article cites Lockheed Martin's 2011 multiyear D-Wave One agreement (roughly $10 million, paired with a dedicated university research center for sustained iterative learning) and IBM's 2016 cloud-based release of a 5-qubit processor (which drew tens of thousands of experimental users despite having no commercial utility on its own) as examples of engagement whose value came from building organizational familiarity rather than immediate operational transformation. The article recommends three concrete leadership actions: developing internal "boundary spanners" who can translate quantum technical progress into company-specific questions; anchoring learning in near-term opportunities where even modest, quantum-inspired performance improvements (such as Fujitsu's Digital Annealer, used in logistics and portfolio optimization) justify experimentation; and deliberately creating organizational space for longer-term process redesign insulated from short-term financial performance metrics, contrasting Walmart's sustained e-commerce process investment with Sears's more peripheral, bolted-on internet strategy as a cautionary parallel.

<span class="src">The quantum computing revolution is closer than you think</span> (Financial Times, June 2026) supplies the industry-wide commercial and competitive context the strategy article's argument depends on, surveying where quantum computing investment and early applications actually stand as of mid-2026. Major technology companies — Google, IBM, Microsoft (with its Majorana 2 chip) — are targeting commercially useful quantum machines by around 2029-2030, and investor enthusiasm is already substantial: Honeywell-backed Quantinuum's Nasdaq IPO valued the company above $15 billion, and rival IonQ's stock has risen more than 700 percent since September 2024. The article surveys concrete commercial partnerships directly overlapping with the strategy piece's framework — Google's collaborations with Boehringer Ingelheim (drug discovery), Bosch (materials science), Mercedes-Benz (battery technology), and Volkswagen (traffic optimization) — and details financial services and fraud-detection use cases in more depth: Mastercard and Oxford Quantum Circuits' collaborative fraud-detection system and Unisys's quantum fraud-detection research (achieving zero false negatives in testing), both addressing the specific weakness that fraud's rarity and adversarial tactics leave conventional AI models chronically short of good training data. The article also introduces a strategic risk absent from the MIT SMR piece: "Q-Day," the anticipated point at which quantum computers can break the cryptographic methods (based on the difficulty of factoring large prime numbers) that most digital security currently relies on, which is already driving "harvest now, decrypt later" attacks and a global standards effort around post-quantum, quantum-resistant cryptography. On the technical limits side, the article cites a Caltech estimate that a working general-purpose quantum computer requires at least 1,000 logical qubits against Oxford Quantum Circuits' current 16-qubit Genesis system, and quotes a mathematician skeptical that current error-correction techniques can ever be made sufficiently robust — a note of caution that tempers, without contradicting, the MIT SMR article's central claim that the right response to this uncertainty is active experimentation rather than passive waiting.

Read together, the two sources describe the same emerging technology from complementary vantage points: the FT piece establishes what is actually happening across the quantum computing industry today — the money, the players, the early applications, and the specific Q-Day security risk — while the MIT SMR piece supplies the strategic and organizational logic for why individual companies should engage with that landscape now rather than wait for it to mature, and what concrete steps that engagement should take.

Two further 2026 pieces update this concept's commercial and security picture. <span class="src">Can quantum turn ‘magic’ into money?</span> (FT, September 2026) describes a sentiment reversal — Oratomic's founder had left for academia two years earlier judging practical quantum computers too far from commercialisation — driven by technical breakthroughs and a surge of capital, while noting that the sector remains a "perpetual five-year technology" in the words of a Quantonation investor, where each advance reveals new constraints. Early claims of quantum advantage have not yet crossed into commercial practicality, large companies are hedging across qubit technologies (Google has started a neutral-atom programme, IBM bought a silicon-spin company), and executives disagree on where value lies: encryption-breaking will not create a large market for most quantum firms, simulation of subatomic systems is the most-cited near-term opportunity (with backers predicting profit-sharing deals with pharmaceutical and chemical firms worth hundreds of billions of dollars over a decade), and optimisation is contested. A 2026 result from researchers including Google and Oratomic staff claimed an exponential advantage in processing massive data sets for machine learning, pointing to a possible later link with AI. This extends the MIT SMR argument above by showing the market is already pricing in co-invention, while the open question of who captures the downstream value remains unanswered. <span class="src">Can quantum turn ‘magic’ into money?</span>

<span class="src">Quantum computers promise mathematical superpowers</span> (The Economist, July 2026) explains why quantum machines are powerful but narrow: superposition and entanglement let qubits represent exponentially many states, but reading out the answer collapses the superposition, so only problems with exploitable mathematical structure benefit. It sharpens this concept's Q-Day concern with newer numbers — Shor's 1994 algorithm remains the template, error-correction advances have cut estimated qubit requirements, and Google, worried enough about its own findings to abandon open publication, is migrating internally to post-quantum cryptography by 2029 against a US standards-agency recommendation of 2035 — and warns of a long tail of systems that cannot be upgraded (hospital equipment, cash machines, toll roads, sewage plants) and of "harvest now, decrypt later" collection. On the constructive side it explains why quantum simulation of chemistry is the stronger business case (density-functional theory sometimes fails badly, for example on silicon's band gap), citing a $2 million Wellcome prize won by Algorithmiq, the Cleveland Clinic and IBM for a hybrid quantum-classical simulation of a light-activated cancer drug. For finance, the "third continent" of problems such as Monte Carlo pricing and portfolio optimisation shows modest or uncertain speed-ups: a 2025 Fraunhofer study found classical methods usually outperformed the Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm on real data, though hardware limits constrained the test. <span class="src">Quantum computers promise mathematical superpowers</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses how a company should decide when its own quantum-experimentation investment is no longer worth continuing, if the "co-invention" payoff described in the MIT SMR framework fails to materialize within a reasonable multi-year window.
- The FT article's 2029-2030 timeline for "commercially viable, reasonable problems" is sourced from vendor and industry-observer statements (Microsoft, Google, McKinsey) rather than independent verification, and the article itself notes credentialed skeptics who doubt useful machines can be built at all — the gap between these two positions is not resolved.
- The two 2026 sources disagree on how soon cryptographically relevant machines arrive and on which qubit technology will win; neither can verify vendor timelines independently, and the Economist notes DARPA's benchmarking is meant to answer exactly that question by 2033.
