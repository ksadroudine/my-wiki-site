## Summary

Amazon's automated, self-improving quality-control system for generative AI output at massive scale (Catalog AI), contrasted with the puzzling industry-wide trend of newer "reasoning" models hallucinating more often, not less. Together they show that reliable gen AI output requires engineered guardrails and continuous testing, not trust in ever-improving models alone.

## Concept

Two sources converge on the same underlying point from opposite directions: generative AI's core reliability problem — its tendency to fabricate, omit, or generate too many plausible-but-untested possibilities — hasn't been solved by model improvement alone, and won't be, which is why organizations deploying gen AI at scale need to engineer reliability directly into their systems rather than trust it will simply improve with newer models. Amazon's Catalog AI illustrates what engineered reliability actually looks like in practice, applied to one of the hardest possible testbeds: a product catalog spanning hundreds of millions of listings, where incomplete, irrelevant, or wrong product information directly costs sales and erodes customer trust. Amazon's traditional approach — thousands of specialists working alongside hundreds of category- and layout-specific machine-learning models — worked but didn't scale well, since a model trained on shirts couldn't be cost-effectively repurposed for TVs; replacing these narrow ML models with large language models simplified both the technology stack and the organization, but introduced a new problem, since roughly 80 percent of Catalog AI's output was unreliable in its first weeks of operation, inventing facts (claiming an electric pump had 15 horsepower with no such data available) or answering the wrong question entirely (describing a sofa frame's material when customers wanted to know about the seat fabric). Amazon's response follows four deliberate steps. First, an audit establishes a real performance baseline, generating thousands of product pages the company already had verified information for and having human auditors score the LLM's output against ground truth to find root causes of failure — the same logic manufacturing quality control uses to establish control limits during a stable period. Second, rather than simply restricting the LLM's inputs (which trades away its ability to draw useful inferences, like knowing paper plates typically aren't dishwasher safe), Amazon layered in three other kinds of guardrails: simple rules that reject clearly invalid suggestions (immaterial style changes, malformed units); statistical profiles that function like statistical-process-control limits, flagging any output that falls outside an expected range for its product category and triggering investigation, sometimes by having one LLM question another about its own reasoning; and "AI checking AI," where a second LLM — trained on different data from the first, so it catches different classes of error — serves as a content reviewer, forcing the content-generating LLM to justify why a proposed change is actually better and sometimes causing it to abandon its own suggestion. Third, once a hypothesis passes these reliability checks, it still has to prove itself works: Amazon built a fully automated A/B testing infrastructure directly into Catalog AI's workflow, since traditional market research (focus groups, surveys) proved misleading — one Booking.com executive told the researchers, "Our predictions of how customers behave are wrong nine out of 10 times." A real example: a skincare product's AI-generated description, which listed only primary benefits (smoother, hydrated skin) instead of the original's long feature-by-feature breakdown, significantly increased sales among a randomly assigned test group — while in a separate case, AI's edit that dropped the phrase "glowing skin" from a body-wash title actually hurt sales, showing why testing effectiveness (not just reliability) is essential even for changes that pass every upstream guardrail. Even among AI-generated content that clears all of Amazon's reliability checks, only about 40 percent actually improves or has no negative effect on sales — the remaining 60 percent produces meaningfully negative results and never reaches the live catalog, underscoring how much of the real quality-control work happens downstream in testing, not upstream in guardrails alone. Fourth, Amazon built the system to be a genuine learning loop: negative experiment results get investigated by humans (one investigation found the LLM was defaulting to writing "no warranty" whenever information was simply missing, a fixable pattern rather than a fundamental limitation), and a "Catalog Data Quality" customer-preference model lets the system get near-instant feedback through virtual experiments instead of always needing real customer traffic — while multivariate experiments and batched "concept tests" (grouping many similar AI-generated listings together to reach statistical significance even for lower-traffic products) let Amazon test and challenge even long-held merchandising assumptions, such as discovering that adding an enriched lifestyle background to product photos increased sales over the traditional white-background convention. Beyond output quality, Amazon's quality system also helps allocate resources across competing AI initiatives with hard evidence rather than the overly optimistic self-reported estimates business groups typically submit (a persistent problem — 41 percent of business leaders surveyed by Deloitte in 2024 said their organizations struggle to define and measure gen AI's actual impact), and has surfaced genuine infrastructure trade-offs, including cases where smaller models outperformed larger ones on cost, retraining frequency, and response time. <span class="src">Addressing Gen AI’s Quality-Control Problem</span>

The second source documents why this kind of engineered reliability is becoming more necessary, not less, as the underlying models themselves advance. A puzzling and industry-wide pattern has emerged: the newest "reasoning" models from OpenAI, Google, and the Chinese startup DeepSeek are hallucinating — generating false information presented as true — more often than their predecessors, even as their mathematical and coding abilities have measurably improved, and AI companies themselves don't fully understand why. OpenAI's own published testing found its o3 model hallucinated 33 percent of the time on PersonQA, a benchmark asking questions about public figures — more than double the hallucination rate of its earlier o1 reasoning model — while the smaller o4-mini model hallucinated at an even higher rate, 48 percent; on a broader general-knowledge benchmark called SimpleQA, o3 and o4-mini hallucinated 51 and 79 percent of the time respectively, compared to 44 percent for o1. Independent tracking corroborates the trend: Vectara, which has monitored chatbot accuracy on a simple, verifiable summarization task since late 2023, found that industry hallucination rates on this specific test had fallen into the 1-to-2 percent range for OpenAI and Google's earlier models (with Anthropic around 4 percent) — but rose again with reasoning models specifically, with DeepSeek's R1 hallucinating 14.3 percent of the time and OpenAI's o3 climbing to 6.8 percent. Two hypotheses for the pattern, neither confirmed, point to real structural pressures in how these models are now built. First, having exhausted most readily available English-language internet text for pretraining, AI companies are leaning more heavily on reinforcement learning — trial-and-error training that works well for math and programming but appears to come at the cost of general factual reliability, since (per University of Edinburgh researcher Laura Perez-Beltrachini) "the way these systems are trained, they will start focusing on one task — and start forgetting about others." Second, reasoning models are specifically designed to "think" through a problem step by step before answering, which means each intermediate step carries its own risk of hallucination that can compound as the chain of reasoning grows longer — and troublingly, researchers have found that the reasoning steps a model displays to users are often unrelated to the answer it ultimately produces, meaning what a system says it's thinking is not necessarily an accurate account of what it's actually doing internally. The practical stakes of this trend are illustrated concretely by an incident at Cursor, a coding tool: its AI-powered support bot fabricated a company policy restricting the product to a single device and communicated it to customers as fact, triggering public complaints and account cancellations before the company's CEO had to publicly clarify no such policy existed — a reminder that hallucination isn't a rare, low-stakes edge case but a live operational risk for any organization deploying gen AI in customer-facing roles, and precisely the kind of failure mode that systems like Amazon's Catalog AI are built to catch before it ever reaches a real customer. <span class="src">A.I. ## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses whether Amazon's Catalog AI approach — effective for a high-volume, high-traffic e-commerce catalog with abundant customer interaction data for A/B testing — generalizes to lower-traffic domains where statistically significant experimentation is much harder to achieve.
- The NYT article does not report whether OpenAI, Google, or DeepSeek have since identified a specific cause for rising reasoning-model hallucination rates, only that research was ongoing as of publication.
