## Summary

Forrester's Go-To-Market Maturity Assessment gives B2B portfolio marketing leaders a structured way to evaluate their organization's go-to-market capability across four areas — business context, market strategy, buyer strategy, and engagement strategy — and progress through beginner, intermediate, and advanced stages.

## Highlights

- Changing B2B buyer behavior and digital proliferation have exposed a structural gap in traditional go-to-market strategies: historically they were built around internal constructs (product lines, business units, sales regions) rather than external constructs (market segments, target audiences) — a mismatch a well-executed go-to-market strategy corrects by aligning sales, marketing, and product teams around core decisions.
- The Go-To-Market Maturity Assessment scores an organization across four areas on a 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree) scale: business context (a well-defined, audience-first decision process with alignment across product, marketing, and sales leadership), market strategy (identifying and prioritizing the most viable market segments using both internal and external insights), buyer strategy (documenting buying centers, buying groups, and prioritized personas), and engagement strategy (coordinating cross-team activities into a cohesive, integrated buyer experience via persona profiles, buyer's journey maps, and buyer messaging).
- No single business function owns go-to-market strategy, since it is a corporate asset touching every part of the business — in most organizations, portfolio marketing takes on the coordinating role, which requires navigating a product-first organizational culture, the complexity of a multi-product portfolio, and the tension between committing to a strategic direction versus staying opportunistic.
- At the beginner stage, functional teams execute disparate go-to-market strategies with little cross-functional alignment and a product-focused rather than audience-centric orientation; the priority is addressing business context and market strategy first, since decisions made there determine everything downstream, and identifying an executive sponsor who genuinely believes in audience-centricity to champion the effort across the C-suite.
- At the intermediate stage, an organization has mastered go-to-market basics but should identify which of the four assessment areas scored lowest and secure agreement on where to focus — for instance, using relative-targeting research to more methodically select and prioritize market segments — while beginning to tighten (not yet complete) alignment across sales, product, marketing, and customer success.
- At the advanced stage, an organization has demonstrated leadership commitment, mastered most elements across all four areas, and uses both internal and external insights to drive downstream execution decisions — the focus shifts to incremental improvement (refreshing personas, repackaging them for different audiences) and staying vigilant for triggers to revisit the whole strategy, such as leadership changes or shifting market conditions and buyer behavior.

## Concept

<span class="src">Evaluate Your Go-To-Market Strategy Maturity</span> (Forrester, March 2024) addresses a structural gap the report identifies at the root of many underperforming B2B go-to-market efforts: strategies historically built around internal organizational constructs — product lines, business units, sales regions — rather than external constructs like market segments and target audiences, a mismatch increasingly exposed as buyer behavior changes and digital channels proliferate. When executed well, a go-to-market strategy functions as a genuine growth catalyst by aligning sales, marketing, and product teams around a shared set of core decisions rather than letting each function optimize independently. Forrester's Go-To-Market Maturity Assessment operationalizes this by scoring an organization across four areas on a 1-to-5 agreement scale. Go-to-market business context evaluates whether the organization has a well-defined, audience-first decision process for making go-to-market choices that maximize revenue growth potential, with genuine alignment across product, marketing, and sales leadership as a critical success factor. Market strategy assesses whether the organization identifies and prioritizes the market segments most viable and attractive for its business, using an insights-driven method that draws on both internal data and external market research rather than intuition alone. Buyer strategy evaluates whether the organization has documented its buying centers, buying groups, and prioritized personas clearly enough to fuel genuinely buyer-focused engagement, including a real understanding of the issues buyers care about most, which in turn informs which specific offerings in a portfolio are best positioned for go-to-market efforts. Engagement strategy assesses whether the organization has developed strategies to coordinate activities across teams into a cohesive, integrated buyer experience, with concrete deliverables including persona profiles, buyer's-journey maps, and buyer messaging. <span class="src">Evaluate Your Go-To-Market Strategy Maturity</span>

Because go-to-market strategy is a corporate asset that touches every part of the business rather than belonging to any single function, Forrester notes it typically falls to portfolio marketing to coordinate — a role that requires navigating a product-first organizational culture (and the time it takes to shift stakeholders toward audience-centric thinking), the sheer complexity of managing a multi-product portfolio's go-to-market needs simultaneously, and an ongoing tension between committing to a focused strategic direction versus remaining opportunistically responsive to emerging opportunities. To help portfolio marketing leaders gauge where their organization actually stands and what to do next, Forrester characterizes three maturity stages. At the beginner stage, functional teams typically execute disparate, uncoordinated go-to-market strategies lacking cross-functional alignment, often still organized around a product-first rather than audience-centric approach that takes real time and effort to shift; the recommended priority is addressing business context and market strategy first, since the decisions made in those two areas will shape every downstream execution effort, and identifying an executive sponsor who genuinely believes in audience-centricity and can champion the strategy's development across the C-suite, using the Forrester Go-To-Market Architecture as a high-level starting guide. At the intermediate stage, an organization has generally mastered the basics but still has room to improve specific practices; the recommended approach is to identify which of the four assessment areas scored lowest, secure organizational agreement on where to focus improvement effort, and prioritize accordingly — for example, an organization scoring low on market-segment prioritization specifically should apply more methodical approaches like relative targeting research rather than continuing to prioritize segments informally. Some genuine cross-functional alignment exists at this stage across sales, product, marketing, and customer success, but the opportunity is to tighten that alignment further across all execution activities, addressing key deficiencies first before moving to other improvement areas. At the advanced stage, an organization has demonstrated genuine leadership commitment to a well-aligned go-to-market strategy, mastered most elements across all four assessment areas, and established a well-defined, methodical approach to identifying ideal target segments and buyers, using both internal and external insights to drive downstream execution decisions rather than working from assumption. Advanced organizations should focus on incremental improvement — refreshing personas or repackaging them differently for different audiences, for instance — while regularly collaborating with colleagues responsible for downstream execution and listening to their feedback; they should also remain vigilant for specific triggers that warrant revisiting the go-to-market strategy as a whole, such as leadership changes within the organization or meaningful shifts in market conditions or buyer behavior, rather than treating an advanced strategy as permanently finished. <span class="src">Evaluate Your Go-To-Market Strategy Maturity</span>

## Open Questions

- The source doesn't specify how long organizations typically take to progress from one maturity stage to the next, or what specific leading indicators best predict whether an organization is actually advancing versus merely accumulating documentation without changing underlying practice.
- It's not established how this B2B-focused framework would need to be adapted for B2C go-to-market strategy, where buying centers and buying groups (a core B2B concept in the buyer-strategy area) don't apply in the same way.
