## Summary

Most corporate boards rank AI as a low priority and rarely use it, but pioneering boards are using it to close the information gap with management, improve scenario planning and even seat a non-voting AI observer. A 2024 focus group of more than 50 board chairs suggests that the risks, including leaks, sample bias and anchoring in the past, are more manageable than most directors assume.

## Highlights

- In 2014, Hong Kong-based Deep Knowledge Ventures gave an algorithm formal voting power on its VC board — dismissed at the time as a gimmick — but a decade later, focus groups with 50+ board chairs from companies including Nestlé, Shell, and Novo Nordisk found most directors still ranked AI as a low priority, well below issues like global context or CEO relations, with most using AI occasionally for personal needs but rarely for board work.
- Nonexecutive directors face a structural information gap: they meet only a few times a year, often serve multiple boards, and traditionally close the gap through site visits, subcommittees, and board books — but a properly trained LLM can analyze far more data than any director could manually, continuously monitor risks for early warnings, and condense information into formats directors can process in the limited time available.
- A Danish nonexecutive director calls ChatGPT her "sparring partner," using it to structure management presentations, find benchmarks, formulate boardroom questions, and run simulations — reporting improved meeting preparation and organizational understanding alongside a lighter workload.
- Boards rarely practice genuine scenario planning despite widespread belief in its value, typically citing complexity and limited resources — AI needs far less time than a team of human experts to identify and assess potential changes in underlying variables, and one board used an LLM to generate three scenarios for a proposed Eastern European acquisition, concluding the investment exceeded its risk appetite and rejecting the deal.
- The logical next step — AI actually participating in boardroom discussion — is already happening: in 2024 UAE's largest publicly traded entity appointed a virtual human, "Aiden Insight," as a non-voting board observer whose contributions go into the official minutes, while G42's BoardNavigator tool listens to live board conversations and offers real-time insights and recommendations.
- Three risks dominate director concern, and all are more manageable than assumed: information leaks are a data-governance problem that predates AI and has known mitigations (guardrails, procedural access rules, vendor no-training-on-customer-data guarantees), not an inherent AI risk; sample bias (AI reflecting the biases embedded in its training data, illustrated by a European industrial company's expensive safety strategy that missed contractor-managed operations excluded from the original employee survey) is mitigated through regular data audits and demographic-specific AI queries; and anchoring in the past is countered by using reasoning models capable of causal (not just correlational) explanation, so boards can identify and challenge specific outdated assumptions a model's recommendation relies on.
- A three-stage learning-by-doing process moves a board toward genuine AI fluency: creating engagement through one-on-one conversations gauging each director's literacy and comfort level, paired with personalized training; practicing collective experimentation by having the entire board use the same general LLM for two or three meetings before graduating to an enterprise-trained governance coach and eventually firm-specific data access; and maintaining momentum by building AI progress into director evaluations and having the chair model sustained personal commitment, including visible openness about their own learning struggles.

## Concept

<span class="src">How Pioneering Boards Are Using AI</span> (HBR, Stanislav Shekshnia and Valery Yakubovich, July 2025) opens with a decade-old cautionary bookend: in 2014, Hong Kong-based Deep Knowledge Ventures formally appointed an algorithm to its VC board with real voting power on investment decisions, an appointment widely dismissed at the time as a publicity gimmick. A decade later, despite remarkable advances in the underlying technology, board-level adoption has lagged: focus groups the authors conducted from June to September 2024 with more than 50 board chairs, vice chairs, and committee chairs from public and private companies across Europe, Asia, and North America (including ASM, Lazard, Nestlé, Novo Nordisk, Randstad, Sandoz, and Shell) found participants ranked AI relatively low in importance — well beneath global context or CEO/shareholder relations — and reported using AI occasionally for personal needs but rarely, if ever, for their board work, with one chair admitting it had "never occurred to me to consider AI for preparing for a board meeting." Even so, a meaningful minority of participants described genuinely positive experiences, and the authors argue that recent advances in AI technology and the normalization of virtual-human business interfaces mean an AI participant in boardroom discussion no longer feels like a gimmick. <span class="src">How Pioneering Boards Are Using AI</span>

The article identifies three distinct ways AI is already contributing to board work, with adoption deepening at each level. Assisting individual board members addresses the structural information gap nonexecutive directors face: they typically meet only a handful of times a year, often serve on multiple boards simultaneously, and are structurally detached from day-to-day operations despite making consequential decisions — a documented challenge across a 17-country European study from 2016-2020. Traditional mitigations (site visits, employee meetings, subcommittees, board books) help but don't fully close the gap; a properly trained LLM can analyze far larger data volumes than a director could manually to surface patterns and trends, continuously monitor risks for early warning, and condense complex information into formats directors can actually process given their limited time. One Danish nonexecutive director on five boards described ChatGPT as her "sparring partner," using it to structure management presentations, benchmark performance, formulate discussion questions, and run simulations, reporting both better meeting preparation and a lighter overall workload; a Swiss board chair similarly feeds board books to ChatGPT before meetings to generate discussion questions and decision options. Supplying the entire board with better information extends this to genuine scenario planning — a practice most directors say they value but few boards actually undertake, typically citing complexity and resource constraints as the barrier. AI needs far less time than a team of human experts to identify and assess potential changes in underlying variables (research the authors cite shows this is exactly how LLMs improve human forecasting performance); one Austrian board chair described a board member asking an LLM to produce three scenarios for a proposed Eastern European acquisition, after which the board concluded the investment exceeded its risk appetite and rejected the deal — with the more durable outcome being that management began supporting all subsequent proposals with formal scenario analysis. A Finnish board used ChatGPT to sanity-check its own strategy-retreat conclusions (the LLM confirmed answers similar to the board's own), and a Dutch board chair used Claude 3.7 Sonnet the same way, with the AI confirming three of four board conclusions and prompting deeper debate specifically on the fourth. AI can also analyze the board's own process — one Swiss industrial company's board uses AI to track speaking time, group dynamics, and conversational tone, generating specific recommendations like redistributing airtime among directors or avoiding dismissive phrases like "no-brainer." Joining the board is the most advanced and most novel form of adoption: in 2024, UAE's largest publicly traded entity, IHC, appointed "Aiden Insight," a virtual human built by Abu Dhabi's G42, as a non-voting "board observer" whose contributions are recorded in official minutes, while G42's separate BoardNavigator tool listens to live board conversations, identifies key discussion points, and offers real-time data, recommendations, and frameworks. Current AI board participants have real limitations — a lack of emotional and situational awareness makes it hard for them to participate without explicit prompting or to argue for their own recommendations under pushback, which the authors note may stem from safety programming unlikely to be "unlearned." <span class="src">How Pioneering Boards Are Using AI</span>

Three risks dominate director concern, and the article argues each is more tractable than commonly assumed. Information leaks — the fear that feeding sensitive board materials into an AI tool effectively opens confidential deliberations to competitors — are, per the authors, not actually a consequence of using AI at all but a preexisting data-governance risk that companies already know how to manage through software guardrails, procedural access rules, and employee data-security training that can straightforwardly extend to board members; major vendors like OpenAI already guarantee enterprise customer data isn't used to train foundational models, and SAP goes further by building smaller, client-exclusive models trained only on a single customer's proprietary data. Sample bias is a more genuine concern, since AI recommendations directly reflect whatever biases are embedded in training data — illustrated by a European industrial company whose board approved an expensive, comprehensive health-and-safety strategy built from an employee survey that, in retrospect, excluded contractor-managed operations entirely; incidents there rose even as the company's own operations improved. Mitigations include regular data audits and bias-detection protocols at the technical level, and, more valuably, direct user awareness — prompting an AI to break down its analysis by gender or ethnic group specifically to surface latent skew, where demographic markers exist to do so. Anchoring in the past is a particular risk for boards, whose job is often to change rather than preserve the status quo; one Belgian food-company CEO explicitly distrusts AI for strategic choices on the reasoning that "strategy is about the future, and AI knows nothing about the future" — though the authors note his own human instincts are equally anchored in past experience, just less legibly so. The most effective countermeasure is using the newest generation of "reasoning models," whose responses are built through explicit chains of causal reasoning rather than pure correlation, letting a board identify precisely which variable or assumption a given recommendation depends on and directly debate whether that assumption still holds (for instance, challenging a borrowing-reduction recommendation premised on rising interest rates if the board's own read of the environment points the other way); boards can also proactively request AI-generated scenario analyses and simulations to project forward rather than simply extrapolating from historical data. <span class="src">How Pioneering Boards Are Using AI</span>

Because few current directors were trained to use AI and many came of age before the digital era, the authors recommend a deliberate three-stage learning-by-doing process rather than expecting fluency to emerge organically. Creating engagement starts with the chair holding one-on-one conversations with each director to gauge their literacy and interest, sharing the chair's own experience and attitude, and surfacing both real and imagined risks explicitly so directors understand they can be managed — followed by personalized training tailored to each director's learning style, ideally through one-on-one tutorials with an internal AI coach that emphasizes personal and collective board effectiveness rather than pure interface mechanics. Practicing collective experimentation moves from individual comfort to shared board practice: for two or three meetings, directors sign onto the same general-purpose LLM and design their own prompts before and during meetings, debriefing afterward to share lessons — once the board finds this genuinely useful, the company can graduate to an enterprise LLM trained on corporate-governance best practices that functions as a performance coach, and eventually grant the tool access to firm-specific data incrementally, starting with a single upcoming board topic rather than a full data dump; the chair should explicitly resist bypassing collective decision-making in favor of pushing AI adoption through personal authority, since whole-board ownership of the decision (and its consequences) is itself part of the goal. Maintaining momentum requires making a board's progress with AI part of postmeeting and annual evaluations, recognizing directors and boards that engage regardless of outcome, and — critically — sustained personal commitment from the chair, who should model continued learning and openly acknowledge their own struggles rather than promoting AI for others while personally avoiding it, since visible chair vulnerability is what gives other directors permission to experiment openly themselves. <span class="src">How Pioneering Boards Are Using AI</span>

## Open Questions

- The article doesn't address how board composition itself might need to change as AI-native, digitally literate directors become more common — whether boards should actively recruit for AI fluency now, or whether the three-stage training process is sufficient to bring existing directors up to speed indefinitely.
- Aiden Insight and BoardNavigator are both presented as early, largely successful examples, but the article doesn't address governance failure modes specific to AI board participants — for instance, how a board should handle a case where an AI observer's recorded minutes contradict human directors' recollection of a discussion.
