## Summary

Artificial general intelligence is AI with human-comparable cognitive breadth, tested by the Turing test, and it differs from today's narrow AI systems. Researchers believe it needs eight capabilities before it can emerge, and most place its arrival decades away, although evidence of models accelerating their own development suggests a nearer timeline.

## Concept

Artificial general intelligence (AGI) describes a theorized future class of AI with cognitive capabilities that genuinely rival a human's — reasoning, problem-solving, perception, learning, and language comprehension performed with human-level nuance rather than narrow task-specific competence. The benchmark most commonly cited for AGI is the Turing test, proposed by Alan Turing in 1950: if an AI's behavior in conversation is indistinguishable from a human's to an observer, it has passed. No AI system has yet passed this test in a rigorous sense, and the consensus among researchers and academics is that AGI remains decades away at minimum, with some serious researchers (MIT roboticist and iRobot cofounder Rodney Brooks among them, who has placed AGI's arrival as far off as the year 2300) doubting it will arrive within this century, or ever. This is a deliberately sober framing against the popular tendency to treat today's most impressive generative AI systems as a small step away from AGI: even the most capable current tools like ChatGPT and DALL-E are, underneath their fluent output, prediction machines — systems that predict a high-probability response to a specific prompt based on patterns in their training data — which is a genuinely different and narrower capability than AGI's theorized capacity for reasoning, perception, and understanding meaning the way a human does. Nearly every AI application in production use today, including the most capable chatbots, falls instead into the category of narrow AI: AI techniques applied to a specific, well-defined problem (detecting fraudulent transactions, processing legal documents with NLP), competent within that scope but with no ability to generalize outside it the way AGI theoretically would. <span class="src">What Is Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)</span>

Researchers identify eight capabilities that AI systems need to master, individually and in combination, before AGI becomes achievable: visual perception, audio perception, fine motor skills, natural language processing, problem-solving, navigation, creativity, and social and emotional engagement. Robotics is treated as a central front in this pursuit, since embodying AI in a physical system that can sense and act in the real world is seen as necessary for several of these capabilities (fine motor skills and navigation especially) rather than something that can be achieved through purely digital, disembodied training. Robots themselves are categorized into five functional types: stand-alone autonomous industrial robots, used in manufacturing and equipped with sensors to navigate and interact with other machines; collaborative robots ("cobots"), specifically designed to work alongside humans and take on repetitive or hazardous tasks in shared environments like restaurant kitchens; mobile robots, wheel-based systems used for materials handling in warehouses and for military applications like reconnaissance and bomb disposal; human-hybrid robots, combining human and robotic features (a robotic limb attached to a human, or a human-appearing robot with cognitive capabilities); and humanoids or androids, designed to emulate human appearance, movement, and communication together while continuously improving their cognitive capability through deep learning. <span class="src">What Is Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)</span>

A specific research direction gaining attention is "embodied cognition": the idea, borrowed from developmental psychology, that robots — like human infants — need to learn rapidly through direct physical, multi-sensory experience of the world, rather than purely through abstract data-driven training divorced from physical interaction, precisely because our physical environments and objects were designed around how human bodies and minds actually work. Current AI-based robot systems are combining two distinct technologies to pursue this: large language models, which give robots the same advanced natural-language-processing capability seen in generative AI chatbots generally, and large behavior models (LBMs), trained on large data sets of observed human physical actions and movements, which let a robot emulate human physical behavior with comparatively limited task-specific training — the theorized end state being robots that can perform a wide range of physical activities without needing to be separately trained for each one. Beyond algorithmic and robotics advances, further acceleration toward AGI is expected to depend on continued computing advances (the source specifically flags quantum computing as a potential future contributor, while noting today's quantum computers aren't yet practical for everyday use) and genuinely new sources of training data beyond what's already been captured from the internet, with autonomous vehicles' on-road data collection offered as one working example of a new data source already accumulating in real time. <span class="src">What Is Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)</span>

Given AGI's uncertain and likely distant timeline, the practical guidance for executives is to treat it as a long-horizon strategic question to monitor rather than an operational problem to solve now, while continuing to invest aggressively in the current generation of narrow AI regardless of that distant timeline, since the source argues "the cost of doing nothing" is already high given how broadly AI investment has become a board-level priority across industries. Specific recommended actions include staying informed on AGI-relevant developments through a deliberate tracking framework, keeping humans at the center of AI deployment through human-in-the-loop augmentation rather than full automation, proactively addressing the ethical and security implications of AI deployment (cybersecurity, data privacy, algorithmic bias), building a strong foundation of high-quality data and AI talent, reorganizing work toward more flexible "flow-to-the-work" models rather than rigid structures poorly suited to fast-moving AI capability, and placing small, deliberately hedged strategic bets — such as minority investments in ambitious AI research efforts relevant to the business's own industry — to preserve strategic optionality against long-term disruption risk without over-committing to any single uncertain outcome. <span class="src">What Is Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)</span>

<span class="src">How artificial intelligence got better at building itself</span> (The Economist, June 2026) offers a sharply more concrete and near-term update to this concept's original "decades away, possibly centuries" AGI timeline, centered on "recursive self-improvement" (RSI): a closed loop in which one AI model designs a faster, more capable successor, which designs a still more capable successor in turn, without further human engineering effort. Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark estimates a 60% chance that an AI system will be capable of creating its own successor with no human involvement at all by the end of 2028 — a claim striking enough that Anthropic itself, at the peak of its commercial success (Claude Code now writes more than four-fifths of the code Anthropic's own engineers publish, and the company was expected to command one of the largest IPOs in history later in 2026), called in June 2026 for the world to have "the option to slow or temporarily pause frontier AI development," a stance the article frames as more sincere than self-serving given Anthropic's leadership has worried publicly about out-of-control AI for years. Full RSI requires automating every specialist task in the AI-development pipeline — the theoretical research data scientists do, the implementation work coders do, the production-scale infrastructure systems engineers build, the sourcing or generation of novel training data, and the alignment and safety verification that checks training output for harm — and which tasks yield to automation first is genuinely hard to predict: designing new algorithms was assumed one of the more AI-resistant specialties until Google DeepMind's AlphaEvolve began doing exactly that in May 2025, independently proposing a data-center workload-scheduling change that saved 0.7% of Google's worldwide computing power and finding faster matrix-multiplication methods that sped up Gemini's training by 1%. A smaller, more personal demonstration reinforces the same pattern: researcher Andrej Karpathy (an OpenAI founding team member and former five-year head of AI at Tesla) had already sped up training his small language model Nanochat from 168 hours down to just over two hours through his own optimization work, then handed further optimization to an AI agent called Autoresearch — which cut training time by a further 18% within five days, entirely unsupervised, through unglamorous fixes (better starting values, a widened attention window, noticing the model's focus was wandering) that Karpathy says he had personally missed despite his expertise. The article identifies three physical constraints currently capping how fast any RSI loop could actually run even once closed: compute access (newer models keep requiring more training compute despite efficiency gains, tying overall progress to the pace of data-center construction this vault's [ai investment bubble](#/concept/ai-investment-bubble) tracks in detail); competing demand for finite AI data-center capacity across serving paying customers, training future models, and running open-ended R&D simultaneously; and training data, since techniques relying on "verifiable rewards" (code that either runs or doesn't, a mathematical proof that's either correct or isn't) let AI safely generate its own synthetic training data for self-improvement without the accuracy degrading, but this safe-self-generation approach doesn't obviously extend to domains like creative writing or legal judgment that lack any comparably verifiable ground truth. The CSET (Centre for Security and Emerging Technology) think tank's January 2026 report projected that as the fraction of AI R&D performed by AI systems increases, the resulting productivity boost over human-only R&D could compound tenfold, then a hundredfold, then a thousandfold, warning that RSI-capable systems "pose extreme risks" that "warrant preparatory action now" — a warning MIT physicist Max Tegmark, who led an earlier 2023 call for a general AI development pause, echoes in describing unchecked RSI as akin to "a driver flooring the accelerator on the motorway with their eyes closed." <span class="src">How artificial intelligence got better at building itself</span>

## Open Questions

- The original source's timeline estimates (decades away, possibly centuries per Rodney Brooks) reflect views current as of the article's 2024 publication date and had already been substantially overtaken by the 2026 RSI source's much closer estimates — no source in this concept reconciles why expert consensus shifted so quickly, or whether the 2024 source's eight-capability framework (visual/audio perception, fine motor skills, etc.) is even the right lens for assessing RSI-driven AGI, which could in principle arrive through recursive software self-improvement alone without ever mastering embodied/physical capabilities.
- Jack Clark's 60%-by-2028 estimate and the CSET report's compounding-productivity projections are presented without a clear methodology in the source excerpt available for this ingest, making it hard to assess how much confidence to place in either figure versus treating them as informed speculation.
