## Summary

Access to AI is unlikely to give any company a lasting competitive advantage, because its inputs are becoming commodities, the gains from early use are easy to copy and first movers' choices add to the data that later movers draw on. Advantage therefore shifts to what AI leaves standing or strengthens, namely assets that rivals cannot copy, trusted relationships, data on live flows of activity, human creativity and the ability to frame the right problem. Advantages based on knowledge, stored data and operational excellence erode while revenue still looks intact, and profit pools move among sectors and competitors. Because expertise is becoming abundant and cheap, firms must keep deciding which expertise to build, which to buy and how to capture the judgment of their own best people. Startups need clear strategy, not technology alone, to scale.

## Concept

The core claim of HBR's analysis is that generative AI creates substantial value but is unlikely to be a source of sustained advantage for any single company, because once early movers demonstrate a use case, the same gains are available to everyone. Value creation and value capture diverge, since cost reductions such as those at Ally Financial, Cisco, Dow and Klarna, and even AI-generated product ideas, converge across competitors as similar algorithms on similar datasets give similar results, consistent with the history of the steam engine, electric motor and personal computer, which rarely conferred lasting advantage because near-universal adoption erased any edge. A subtler mechanism is that gen AI trains on continually updated data, so a company's own observable strategic choices enter the dataset that competitors' later queries draw on, and first movers unintentionally subsidize fast followers' catch-up. Proprietary data is a weaker moat than it looks, since functionally equivalent datasets tend to produce similar outputs, larger datasets show diminishing returns past a sample size, AI can increasingly infer a data-driven strategy from observable results and breaches are common. <span class="src">AI Won’t Give You a New Sustainable Advantage</span>

The article's silver lining is that a company with pre-existing, hard-to-replicate resources, such as Amazon's supplier network and logistics operations, can use gen AI to amplify that advantage, and that a company could in principle rebuild its entire business model around AI so deeply that the resulting agility is hard to copy, although none had yet done so as of 2024. <span class="src">AI Won’t Give You a New Sustainable Advantage</span>

MIT Sloan Management Review reaches the same conclusion from formal strategy theory. A sustainable advantage must be valuable, unique to the organization and inimitable by rivals, and AI is valuable but fails the other two tests, because its inputs are commoditizing, with scaling laws giving diminishing model gains per dollar, semiconductor progress widening hardware access, open publication spreading algorithmic insight and AI PhD production outpacing other fields. As AI homogenizes business capabilities, the greatest remaining value lies in residual heterogeneity, whatever a company creates beyond what AI makes universally accessible, and since AI interpolates within its training data but lags humans at extrapolation, such as inventing new artistic styles or solving arbitrary novel problems, human creativity, novel partnerships and relationship-building remain the durable levers, illustrated by a hypothetical AI-therapy market in which all providers converge on similar models and differentiation must come from elsewhere. <span class="src">Why AI Will Not Provide Sustainable Competitive Advantage</span>

A third article asks which existing advantages AI leaves standing, using the idea of moats. Rapid advances in compute, models and data are lowering entry barriers and letting well-resourced entrants challenge incumbents in markets long thought defensible. Moats under pressure include the human-capital and brand advantages of professional-services firms, as service as software substitutes AI for expert labor, content-industry moats built on production scale and curatorial judgment, as generative multimedia tools mature, and elite higher education's brand barriers, as AI tutoring scales personalized instruction. Moats likely to persist or strengthen include brand trust, particularly as AI-generated fakes proliferate, large and hard-to-simulate proprietary datasets, certain intellectual property, operational effectiveness in adopting AI quickly, lobbying and government relations, new AI-native business models and durable personal relationships that still influence purchases. <span class="src">Could Gen AI End Incumbent Firms’ Competitive Advantage</span>

Disruption theory gives the mirror image. Julian Birkinshaw separates supply-side effects, how AI changes the cost of developing and making a product, from demand-side effects, how it changes what customers want and how they get it, argues that demand-side effects are the more perilous and offers ten diagnostic questions. A company keeps its advantage over AI-native entrants while it holds complementary assets that AI cannot replicate, such as expertise in complex regulatory approval, as in the biotech versus Big Pharma precedent, or deep, iterative client relationships. Demand-side risk is uneven, since translation, stock photography and tutoring face near-existential threat because AI can perform the service, while sellers of physical products face little because customers still want the physical thing however it is made or marketed. The headline conclusion is that for most industries AI will be sustaining rather than disruptive provided incumbents stay on top of developments. <span class="src">Will AI Disrupt Your Business? Key Questions to Ask</span>

The innovation domain shows the same logic, since generative AI has made producing many plausible product ideas nearly free and ideation has stopped being the scarce resource separating winners, so the edge has moved to problem framing, which the article calls Question Zero, choosing which problem is worth solving. The best problems are hidden because the people who have them cannot articulate them, so surveys and focus groups surface only superficial framings, and AI's contribution is surfacing behavioral patterns at a scale no human team can match, as when Cursor's founders reframed write code faster as understand code faster after seeing that developers spend most of their time reading code, Netflix found that users browse by mood rather than genre, Duolingo's Birdbrain system found that excessive similarity between languages bred overconfidence and silent dropout, and Procter & Gamble found that parents applying adult skincare to babies signaled a trust problem and not a product-quality problem. In each case the human supplied the reframing while AI supplied the telescope that made the pattern visible. <span class="src">The Innovation Advantage GenAI Can’t Give You</span>

BCG locates where moats migrate. Because foundation models and inference economics converge to parity within two to three years, advantage moves to the assets beneath the technology, and BCG argues that the story is shifting value pools rather than productivity, with hyperscaler capex on track for USD 750 billion in 2026, legacy enterprise software having lost about USD 2 trillion of market value and compute becoming a structural claimant on every AI-enabled transaction. Five factors decide who captures the AI dividend, with task substitutability, market concentration and data defensibility setting direction and compute intensity and regulatory friction setting speed and leakage, which produces opposite outcomes by sector, such as retail passing nearly all value to consumers while pharmaceuticals also reward shareholders. BCG maps conventional advantages on moat depth and AI durability. Knowledge and expertise, stock data and operational excellence are deep but fragile and most likely to look intact from inside while eroding, a pattern it calls hollowing, in which revenue holds while margin drains, whereas physical assets, flow data, such as Visa's more than 257 billion transactions a year, network scale and trusted relationships, such as the Mayo Clinic Platform's independent validation of clinical AI, compound with use. <span class="src">Who Will Win as AI Rewrites Competitive Advantage?</span>

Mid-scale incumbents are squeezed between scaled incumbents and AI-native attackers, whose structural advantage is organizational, with no legacy headcount or workflow, and who target the highest-margin slice, as Chime, retail media, Augury and AI service agents such as Sierra and Decagon do. BCG's five imperatives are to audit each advantage as strengthened, eroded or hollowing, to defend against margin-extracting attackers, to make bets robust across compute-cost, market-repricing and model-access scenarios, to redesign the organization for AI-first execution and to set the shareholder narrative rather than let the market write it. <span class="src">Who Will Win as AI Rewrites Competitive Advantage?</span>

Bain sizes the same redistribution, estimating that AI puts USD 4.7 trillion of profit at stake between 2025 and 2035, more than triple the Internet's USD 1.4 trillion in half the time, with about 75% beyond productivity, namely productivity USD 1.1 trillion, innovation USD 2.2 trillion and competitive share shifts USD 1.3 trillion, and 71% of sectors structurally transformed against 41% for the Internet. It contrasts the Internet, a distribution technology that collapsed the cost of reaching customers, with AI as a production technology, cognitive and embodied, that collapses the cost of making the product and extends into pharma, healthcare delivery, industrial manufacturing and professional services. Of the total, about 24% is productivity profit that companies keep and about 29% is redistribution among competitors, so most disruption comes from known rivals that use AI best. Bain sorts sectors into four clusters, technology foundation at USD 1.5 trillion, rewired at USD 1.5 trillion, augmentation at USD 1.3 trillion and revolution at USD 0.3 trillion. In rewired sectors incumbents with proprietary data and regulatory position, such as healthcare imaging concentrating in health systems and device makers, can reinforce leadership, open races such as enterprise software, consulting, corporate law and cybersecurity have no predetermined winners and revolution sectors such as customer support face replacement of the delivery model, and Bain urges CEOs to make an explicit ten-year prediction, wire it into how the organization learns and build for a compounding gap rather than a 10% advantage. <span class="src">AI Will Shift $4.7 Trillion in Profits. Geography shows that technology is not a substitute for strategy. Nataliya Langburd Wright finds that generative AI helps ventures pitch globally but disproportionately improves English-language pitches, and persuasive AI-written tool descriptions make tool choice harder, so managers fall back on local or familiar defaults, with judges in one accelerator favoring their own regions and passing over 1 in 20 promising startups. Startups outside hubs thus fall into one of two traps, expanding globally too early or defaulting to local, familiar tools. The remedy is strategic clarity through four questions, covering the core value proposition, the target market, how technology reinforces advantage and where to begin, and it turns on whether advantage lies in access, as for Grab and GoJek, which adapted ride-hailing to cash payments and motorbikes, or in quality, as for Grammarly and Spotify, built on distinct local assets. <span class="src">The Global Scaling Gap Why Strategic Clarity Is Crucial in the Age of AI</span>

The deeper frame is expertise. HBR's Coase-grounded analysis treats a business as a differentiated bundle of expertise, theoretical knowledge and practical know-how, organized to accomplish tasks for customers, with broad expertise across functions but real advantage from superior proficiency in a handful of activities, such as Toyota's lean manufacturing, Walmart's distribution, Procter & Gamble's consumer marketing and Nvidia's GPU design. Because competition is relentless, companies must keep evolving how they deploy expertise, as Nokia's failure shows, since its 40% share of the mobile-phone market in 2007 rested on hardware and manufacturing expertise, the smartphone era required software-ecosystem expertise that it, with Motorola, Sony Ericsson and BlackBerry, could not develop in time, and virtually all its share went to Apple and Samsung. Two forces accelerate such displacement, the body of relevant expertise growing beyond what any firm can track, with published academic papers on AI in biology rising from under 200 in 2001 to more than 45,000 twenty years later, and the cost of accessing existing expertise falling. <span class="src">Strategy in an Era of Abundant Expertise</span>

Ronald Coase's 1937 theory of the firm implies that as external costs fall relative to internal ones, companies should narrow their internal scope and rely more on external sourcing, a pattern visible in direct-to-consumer retailers that assemble an operating stack from Shopify for e-commerce, Google for advertising, Stripe for payments, Amazon for logistics, Salesforce and Workday for back office and Microsoft for cloud and AI, which lets small firms compete credibly with vertically integrated incumbents. AI is the newest chapter, as providers embed copilots, bots and assistants such as GitHub Copilot, ServiceNow's Now Assist and Salesforce's Agentforce in workflows, with relatively high-quality expertise at collapsing cost, since the price of accessing OpenAI's GPT-4 through its API fell more than 99% over 18 months. Companies can now access expertise for individual steps within a process rather than outsourcing an entire process, which gives process-level improvement more granularity and lower switching cost, and workers at every level may shift toward supervising AI actions and managing exceptions. <span class="src">Strategy in an Era of Abundant Expertise</span>

Companies that exploit this realize a triple product. The first part is cost and time savings, as developers using AI coding tools finish tasks 20-55% faster across studies, a large study found that gen AI assistants helped call-center representatives resolve 14% more issues per hour, security professionals using an assistant were 7% more accurate and 23% faster, and Moderna's 6,000 employees built more than 900 specialized assistants that cut tasks from weeks to minutes. The second is workforce productivity, since expertise is normally distributed and AI puts baseline expertise in every user's hands, so in the BCG and Harvard randomized trial AI-augmented consultants completed 12% more tasks 25% faster, with quality up 43% for lower-skilled and 17% for top consultants, which narrows the gap between average and top performers, with implications for onboarding, flexibility and wider managerial spans. The third is more investment in activities that matter, as smart companies double down on the few processes where they have world-class expertise and rely on AI-enabled platforms for the rest, as FocusFuel, a caffeinated-gummy startup founded in 2023, used gen AI for market analysis, supplier identification, packaging design and marketing to launch within months and reach seven-figure revenue in eight months while its founders concentrated on product strategy and customer relationships. <span class="src">Strategy in an Era of Abundant Expertise</span>

Capturing the return needs a foundation and a deliberate rollout, with prior digital transformation in digitized data, cloud adoption and security and governance protocols, and four steps. Start with one process where AI has already proven valuable, such as coding, customer service or marketing, treat safety and governance as foundational given risks of bias, misinformation, deepfakes and cyberattack, which are easier to define against one process, cultivate internal early-adopter champions and peer mentors reinforced by company-wide training, as in Moderna's mandatory 20-hour AI academy, since initiatives often fail at the front line without it, and budget AI as a core line item across every division rather than a pilot expense, as Coursera did by training its software engineers, deploying an AI coding assistant and adding AI capabilities to several products in about a year. <span class="src">Strategy in an Era of Abundant Expertise</span>

Every company should reevaluate strategy against three questions as expertise becomes abundant. Which parts of the problem it solves will customers solve themselves with AI, as travel agents have lost destination research and booking to AI itinerary apps and must reinvent around experiences that AI cannot originate. Which of its expertise must evolve most to stay ahead of AI's growing capability, as doctors, where AI can already outperform physicians at some image-based diagnosis, must lean into empathy, caregiving and care coordination. And which durable assets it can build that AI is unlikely to erode, such as brands, deep customer relationships, scarce physical assets and network effects, since a designer can generate prototypes with AI but their quality depends on the nuance of the specifications, so deep, trusted customer research may become more differentiating than design generation. The argument is that which expertise confers advantage keeps shifting, so thriving companies continuously re-examine that bundle. <span class="src">Strategy in an Era of Abundant Expertise</span>

A complementary, internally focused path captures the judgment of a company's own best experts instead of buying expertise externally, as HBR describes in three anonymized cases. At a German venture capital firm, junior analysts overwhelmed by startup applications lacked the experience to spot promising deals even with the firm's template, so it built a hybrid machine-learning and language-processing system that mimics how an experienced managing partner ranks applications, with a senior analyst who reviews and can overrule it, each override retraining its criteria weights in near-real-time, and it proved accurate even on small datasets. At an Indian satellite-analytics firm, overloaded remote-sensing specialists were not replaced by rule-based automation but supported by analysis avatars built by observing what the best specialists actually did with data, not by asking them to explain their process, since practitioners are often poor at articulating tacit expertise, so juniors absorb best practice without monopolizing senior time. At an Australian company, R&D leadership deployed a roster of AI agents, each representing a distinct area of expertise, that employees choose from and rate for usefulness, creating a peer-driven recommender for which agent suits which problem. Observing behavior avoids the failure of 1980s expert systems, which relied on self-reported rules and struggled with fast-changing contexts, and the new systems stay current through feedback. <span class="src">How 3 Companies Are Using AI to Mimic Expert Judgement</span>

The two expertise sources describe complementary paths to the same shift, expertise becoming abundant and cheap to access, whether bought from an AI-powered external market or captured from a company's own best people and scaled, and they converge on durability, since expertise-based advantage does not disappear but must be continuously re-earned by evolving which expertise a firm holds and by retraining captured systems against new data and overrides. <span class="src">Strategy in an Era of Abundant Expertise</span> <span class="src">How 3 Companies Are Using AI to Mimic Expert Judgement</span>
