## Summary

Generative AI is already producing measurable value for early adopters in five business functions, namely software and product development, customer support, sales and marketing, new products and features, and back-office work. Bain finds that the companies capturing the most value follow a particular sequence of diagnosing the business, setting targets, redesigning processes and only then deploying AI tools, and that they treat AI as a priority set by the chief executive. Recurring barriers include scattered data sources, weak technology support and the difficulty of moving from pilots to production. A 2026 update reports that enterprises running AI as a business transformation sponsored by the executive team see 10 to 25 percent EBITDA growth, while many remain at tool deployment, and that the main constraint is now how fast enterprises can absorb AI.

## Concept

<span class="src">Five Functions Where AI Is Already Delivering</span> (Bain & Company, David Crawford, September 2024) argues that generative AI is following a fundamentally different adoption pattern than past technology disruptions like the internet. With most prior waves, "fast followers" could profitably wait and learn from first movers' mistakes before committing; with AI, Bain finds early adopters are already realizing performance gains worth up to 20 percent of earnings within 18 to 36 months, building capabilities and organizational confidence that compound into a durable competitive advantage rather than a temporary lead competitors can quickly close. This is fueling a sharp rise in commitment: the number of large companies investing more than $100 million to implement AI more than doubled in a single year. Yet despite hundreds of experimental use cases being tried across these investments, Bain's research finds most of the realized value today concentrates in just five core functional areas, rather than being spread evenly across the enterprise. <span class="src">Five Functions Where AI Is Already Delivering</span>

In software and product development, the leading generative AI use cases are code generation, documentation, refactoring, debugging, testing, and run/maintenance, with some developer organizations already saving 15-40 percent on code generation and documentation and 30-50 percent or more on refactoring, select testing, and debugging — gains Bain attributes to AI's ability to draw on patterns and datasets that extend beyond an organization's own codebase. Intuit illustrates the depth this can reach in practice: the company has tested and scaled more than 30 distinct generative AI use cases across its software development life cycle, tuning its coding assistant on Intuit-specific code context and repositories for above-average efficiency gains, and using AI-assisted refactoring specifically to accelerate its code-base modernization efforts. In customer support, generative AI's most valuable role may not be automating existing interactions but reducing how much support is needed in the first place, through analytics that anticipate and deflect potential issues before they require a human interaction at all; where support interactions do still happen, Bain finds generative AI can reduce adviser response time by up to 35 percent and improve resolution quality by up to 40 percent, illustrated by a technology and manufacturing company's field-services maintenance copilot and its system for coordinating emergency-responder decisions from unstructured building-sensor data. In sales and marketing, generative AI is being deployed for dynamic personalized content, personalized email marketing, automated account planning, and social-media engagement automation, boosting sales-rep and marketing-staff productivity while shortening cycle times and improving click-through rates through hyper-personalization — one technology hardware company set out to cut time spent on content by 30 percent through automated content workflows and met or exceeded that target across its initial pilots. In new products and features, companies are using generative AI to build simpler, more customizable and personalized product interfaces — Carrefour's generative AI shopping assistant generates personalized shopping lists and menu suggestions directly from customer information and input, simplifying and making more engaging what was previously a manual browsing experience. And in the back office, generative AI is particularly well suited to the large volume of routine, comparatively simple processes those functions contain: Deutsche Telekom built a procurement chatbot trained on the company's own policies and historical procurement strategy that can answer policy-compliance questions and recommend vendors, contracts, or fair pricing for a given request for proposal, with pilot results suggesting it could save business users up to 2,000 hours per month and procurement users up to 5,000 hours per month. <span class="src">Five Functions Where AI Is Already Delivering</span>

Bain identifies three recurring barriers standing between experimentation and the extraordinary value these five functions can deliver. First, companies risk automating existing complexity rather than removing it: deploying AI onto an unredesigned business process just makes an already-inefficient process run somewhat faster, so the recommended sequence is to fix, streamline, and simplify a process first and only then automate it. Second, sprawling databases, multiple sources of truth, and complex application environments hinder rapid, reliable AI deployment, meaning investment in data modernization and governance ahead of scaling AI unlocks an additional wave of productivity that scaling onto messy data cannot capture. Third, technology and services support — reference architectures, LLM recommendations, prompt-engineering guidance, and especially GPU infrastructure — is in genuinely short supply, in part because so many technology providers are simultaneously racing to embed foundation-model AI into their own products rather than purely supporting customers' deployments. Building on these lessons, Bain names six best practices that recur across the companies capturing the most value: prioritizing AI as a value-generation initiative from the CEO down, with explicit ROI targets and budget accountability rather than treating AI spend as a discretionary IT line item; conducting a business diagnostic before automating, so investment goes toward redesigning genuinely value-creating processes rather than encoding existing inefficiency; defining a clear roadmap that concentrates effort on the highest-value functional areas (sales and marketing, customer support, software development, operations) rather than spreading thin; using multiple AI delivery models in parallel — self-service knowledge-worker tools, prebuilt commercial AI systems, and custom-built models — matched to how much differentiation or data sensitivity a given use case actually requires; building shared datasets, models, and platform components to capture economies of scale across many individual AI solutions rather than reinventing infrastructure project by project; and developing risk management, responsible AI, and workforce/talent strategies in parallel with the technical rollout rather than as an afterthought once deployment is already underway. <span class="src">Five Functions Where AI Is Already Delivering</span>

<span class="src">The AI-Native Enterprise Absorption Is the New Advantage</span> (Bain, September 2026) updates the function-level evidence with a view on the stack. Bain argues model profits are migrating toward harnesses (the context, memory, tools, permissions, routing and evaluation that connect models to the enterprise) because enterprises want a portfolio of swappable models, although multistep workflows still reward small accuracy gains (a 97%-accurate model completes a 34-step process correctly 36% of the time, versus 71% for a 99%-accurate one). Executives blame data security (40%), hallucinations (36%) and expertise (33%) for slow adoption, but Bain cites MIT research that organizational barriers such as change management and unwillingness to adopt tools matter more, and advises designing workflows as learning systems that recirculate operational data without waiting for review cycles. HubSpot's agents resolving over 70% of support tickets illustrate an abstraction layer that lets a company swap models. <span class="src">The AI-Native Enterprise Absorption Is the New Advantage</span>

## Open Questions

- The article doesn't specify what share of the "up to 20% of earnings" performance gain figure is attributable to each of the five functions individually, making it hard to prioritize investment among them using this source alone.
- Named company examples (Intuit, Deutsche Telekom, Carrefour) are the more fully identified cases; several other examples are described only by industry category ("one technology and manufacturing company," "one technology hardware company"), limiting independent verification.
- The 10-25% EBITDA range and function-level improvements come from Bain engagements and public company claims, without sample sizes or baselines.
