## Summary

Realizing AI's productivity potential requires redesigning the underlying structure of work itself, not simply layering AI onto existing jobs and processes. Six complementary frameworks — a "kaizen 2.0" continuous-improvement lens, a task-level "deconstruct, redeploy, reconstruct" methodology, a "Design for Dialogue" framework treating AI as a dynamic collaborator, a revival of classical business process management, a diagnosis of why pilot-stage redesign stalls before reaching enterprise scale, and a cost-of-errors/type-of-knowledge task-classification test — all find that treating AI as a stand-alone substitution tool leaves most of its productivity potential unrealized.

## Highlights

- Toyota's kaizen (continuous-improvement) philosophy, built on employee empowerment and incremental process enhancement since the 1940s, is being extended into "kaizen 2.0" — generative AI's natural-language interfaces let nontechnical employees drive process improvements directly, rather than requiring dedicated technical specialists.
- At Mercedes-Benz, the MO360 Data Platform lets shop-floor employees query production data in plain language and receive data-rich insights, while GitHub Copilot frees software developers to focus on complex integration issues — process improvement becomes part of everyday work rather than a specialized skill.
- Merck used generative AI (generative adversarial networks and variational autoencoders) to create synthetic defect-image training data, reducing false rejects in automated drug-inspection systems by more than 50%, addressing a problem caused by having too few real examples of complex, rare defects to train on.
- A single NASA research engineer used commercial AI design software to redesign the process for creating one-off spacecraft components, cutting a task that traditionally took months to one to two hours while producing structures two-thirds lighter and ten times more stress-resistant — illustrating individual employee empowerment at the extreme end.
- A "deconstruct, redeploy, reconstruct" methodology treats work as a fluid system of discrete tasks (not fixed job roles) that can each be substituted, augmented, or transformed by AI, then redeployed to the optimal combination of automation, junior talent, senior talent, or an external capability center — a "work-backward" approach contrasted with a "tech-forward" one that just applies technology to existing roles.
- A global financial-services company applying this methodology to a customer-order process automated 68 tasks, shifted 17 tasks from senior to junior staff (aided by generative AI), redeployed 29 analytically demanding tasks to an existing global capability center, and augmented 14 senior-employee tasks like client relationship management — producing a 50% reduction in workforce hours, a 40% reduction in operating cost, and an 18% reduction in employee turnover.
- Both sources converge on treating AI as a "force multiplier" for an organization's entire technology stack rather than a stand-alone tool: the financial-services case combined generative AI with existing RPA and machine learning systems, and Mercedes-Benz's process gains depend as much on data-platform infrastructure and employee upskilling (its Turn2Learn program) as on generative AI itself.
- Both sources also converge on autonomous, multi-agent systems as the emerging next stage of process redesign — from DoNotPay's single autonomous agent finding and canceling unnecessary subscriptions, to Stanford researchers' multi-agent system achieving 93% step-identification accuracy on real hospital revenue-cycle and B2B invoice-processing workflows — but both emphasize that human oversight, alignment, and feedback remain essential even as agent autonomy increases.
- Traditional business process reengineering (BPR) — the "don't automate, obliterate" approach pioneered by Michael Hammer in 1990 — rigidly assigns each task to either a human or a fixed automation and then locks in the resulting process, a top-down method that fails to capitalize on generative AI's iterative, dialogue-based nature; a "Design for Dialogue" framework instead treats AI as a dynamic team member whose leadership on a given task shifts fluidly based on context, built on three components: task analysis, interaction protocols, and feedback loops.
- Jerry, a $450 million car-ownership platform receiving over 200,000 customer messages a month, applied Design for Dialogue to customer service: after installing its gen AI solution in May 2023, the share of inquiries answered within 24 hours rose from 54% (fully human-led) to over 96% answered within 30 seconds (AI-led 100% of the time), projecting $4 million in annual savings without reducing headcount, since AI absorbed routine information-hunting and left agents to handle escalations with fuller context.
- Interaction protocols — Design for Dialogue's rules for when AI hands off to a human — are illustrated concretely at Jerry: escalation triggers include the AI detecting negative customer sentiment, being unable to parse what the customer needs, or lacking confidence in its own answer, mirroring how a human team member would seek help when hitting the edge of their competence.
- A Harvard Business School/Microsoft summit of leaders from a dozen enterprise-scale AI adopters found seven specific frictions stalling the transition from individual pilots to company-wide operating-model change, none of which is model quality or data availability — including "islands of productivity" that don't scale (an AI agent drafts a contract in seconds, but it still sits in a two-week manual legal review queue because governance wasn't redesigned), a "productivity gap" where individual time savings get reabsorbed into low-value work instead of being structurally harvested through role redesign, and "process debt" where AI exposes decades of accumulated workflow inconsistency faster than it can be resolved.
- The "identity problem of tribal knowledge" is a distinct friction from a pure reskilling challenge: when expertise has historically been defined by being the person who "knows," asking that person to externalize their judgment into an AI system threatens professional identity directly, and initiatives stall unless this is addressed as identity work, not just training.
- As organizations deploy AI agents that take action rather than just advise, governance shifts from model oversight to a fundamentally different problem resembling human-resources management — one large asset-servicing firm running over 100 agents, planning for tens of thousands, found human-in-the-loop controls that work for isolated cases collapse under multi-agent architectures, requiring new answers to how a "digital worker" is onboarded, evaluated, secured, and retired.
- The proposed counterweight blueprint has four structural pillars matching the frictions one-for-one: "clean-sheet process redesign" (discarding legacy workflows entirely and asking how the process would be built today with AI agents as first-class participants, rather than bolting AI onto an unchanged process); "strategic knowledge capture" (treating tribal knowledge as a strategic asset to be externalized, positioned to experts as legacy-building rather than replacement); "managing the digital workforce" (agentic control planes that monitor agent performance and permissions much like an HR system tracks a human workforce); and "role redesign and career pathing" (explicitly shifting human roles toward orchestration, verification, and storytelling around AI output, since without explicit redesign AI adoption feels like career erosion rather than evolution).
- A task-classification framework crosses cost of errors (low/high) with type of knowledge required (explicit/tacit) to sort any job's component tasks into four zones: "no regrets" (low cost, explicit knowledge — AI does it all, e.g. screening résumés or approving low-dollar reimbursements); "creative catalyst" (low cost, tacit knowledge — AI generates options, a human selects, e.g. drafting ad taglines); "quality control" (high cost, explicit knowledge — AI produces, a human verifies, e.g. drafting legal contracts or code); and "human-first" (high cost, tacit knowledge — AI only assists, e.g. setting strategy or making disciplinary decisions).
- The "paradox of access" argues that because every competitor can use the same generative AI tools, applying them to the same tasks in the same way erodes rather than builds advantage — margins flow to customers and suppliers instead (as happened with airline e-ticketing and CAD/ERP software), and worse, customers and suppliers can use gen AI to disintermediate a firm entirely, the way in-house counsel using AI legal-research tools has steadily displaced work once sent to outside law firms.
- Freed-up time from gen AI adoption doesn't automatically become profit: without deliberate management, saved hours evaporate into idle tinkering or low-value busywork, so realizing gains requires managers to track how much time AI saves on key tasks, set explicit expectations for how that time gets redeployed, and tie recognition to effective redeployment — otherwise gen AI efficiency never shows up in the P&L despite being real at the task level.
- Classical business process management (BPM) — 1990s-era process reengineering, later softened into lean and Six Sigma — and AI reinforce each other in both directions: AI helps scale up an already-well-defined process far beyond what manual redesign could achieve, while a well-managed, end-to-end process is what actually produces the large volumes of clean, consistent data an AI system needs to be trained and deployed reliably.
- A seven-step methodology for reviving BPM with AI: establish process ownership (a named "process owner" with cross-departmental authority); identify process customers and their actual needs; map the existing process (increasingly done with AI-driven process mining rather than manual whiteboard sessions); set performance targets; select process enablers (RPA for small processes, gen AI and traditional ML for larger ones); redesign the process (Deutsche Telekom used Pega's Blueprint tool to streamline 250 of its roughly 800 global HR processes, letting business experts describe processes in plain language rather than requiring IT translation); and implement and continuously monitor via process mining.
- PepsiCo's accounts-payable process-mining effort (started 2019, later extended to nine more processes including order-to-cash) shaved thousands of labor hours and millions in write-offs annually, and used AI to diagnose a 30% order-rejection rate after a new SAP rollout down to 4% — illustrating process mining's dual role as both a redesign diagnostic and an ongoing control mechanism.

## Concept

The earliest of this concept's sources, <span class="src">Your Organization Isn’t Designed to Work with GenAI</span> (HBR, February 2024), argues that the standard playbook for integrating new technology into business processes doesn't fit generative AI. Michael Hammer's 1990 business process reengineering (BPR) methodology — "don't automate, obliterate" — works by rigidly assigning each task to either a human or a fixed automated step and then locking the resulting workflow in place, an approach well suited to technologies with fixed capabilities but poorly suited to generative AI's iterative, dialogue-based, continuously improving nature. The article proposes "Design for Dialogue" as an alternative: rather than taking capabilities "out of" humans and putting them "into" a machine, the framework treats AI more like a team member whose leadership on a given task shifts fluidly based on context and competence, mirroring how a high-performing human team dynamically reallocates leadership during a project. The framework rests on three components: task analysis (breaking a process into component tasks and assessing which — human or AI — should lead each, akin to a project manager assessing who is best suited to a role), interaction protocols (rules for how AI and humans hand off to each other, such as AI escalating to a human when it detects an anomaly or lacks confidence, rather than a fixed, one-way process), and feedback loops (ongoing mechanisms — error reporting, satisfaction surveys, AI decision explanations — that let the human-AI collaboration keep improving, the way a team debriefs and adjusts after each project stage). The article's central case study is Jerry, a $450 million car-ownership platform handling over 200,000 customer messages monthly: after installing a gen AI solution in May 2023, the company moved from humans leading 100 percent of customer service and answering only 54 percent of inquiries within 24 hours, to AI leading 100 percent of interactions and answering over 96 percent within 30 seconds by June 2023, projecting $4 million in annual savings while maintaining headcount, since AI absorbed the laborious information-hunting work and left human agents to handle only the escalations the AI's interaction protocols flagged. The article closes with a four-step implementation sequence — identify high-value "WINS work" (processes rich in words, images, numbers, and sounds), perform task analysis, design interaction protocols and feedback mechanisms, then train, evaluate, and scale — arguing organizations that build deep expertise in designing for dialogue gain a durable advantage as conversational interfaces become the default way work gets done.

<span class="src">The Secret to Successful AI-Driven Process Redesign</span> (HBR, January 2025) frames generative AI's arrival as a second wave of Toyota's kaizen philosophy — continuous, incremental process improvement driven by empowered employees — made newly universal because natural-language interfaces put process-improvement capability in the hands of nontechnical workers across an organization, not just specialists. The article surveys this pattern across industries and functions: at Mercedes-Benz, shop-floor employees query production data in plain language via the MO360 Data Platform while the company's Turn2Learn program provides more than 40,000 AI and data courses to build workforce capability; Merck used generative AI to synthesize defect-image training data, cutting false rejects in automated inspection systems by more than 50 percent; a single NASA research engineer used commercial AI design software to redesign one-off spacecraft component creation, compressing a months-long process to one to two hours while producing lighter, more stress-resistant parts; and Sereact's PickGPT lets warehouse operators direct robotic "pick and pack" operations through plain-language chat commands. The article's second half turns to autonomous agents as kaizen's next stage — from DoNotPay's single agent autonomously finding and canceling a CEO's unnecessary subscriptions, to a Stanford multi-agent system that achieved 93 percent accuracy identifying workflow steps in real hospital revenue-cycle and B2B invoice-processing processes, outperforming traditional robotic process automation specifically because agents built on foundation models can reason through the variations and exceptions that trip up hard-coded RPA bots. Even so, the article insists human oversight, alignment, and feedback remain the "linchpin" of success as agent autonomy increases, not an eventually dispensable stopgap.

<span class="src">Want AI-Driven Productivity? Redesign Work</span> (MIT SMR, May 2025) supplies the structured methodology the HBR article's case studies largely lack: a "deconstruct, redeploy, reconstruct" approach that treats work as a fluid system of discrete tasks rather than fixed job roles, explicitly favoring a "work-backward" approach (start from the tasks that need doing) over a "tech-forward" one (start from what a new technology can automate). The article argues this reframing is necessary because AI, gig-platform access, and agile talent models are collectively "bending the demand curve" of work — decoupling productivity growth from resource intensity in ways the traditional wage/labor-supply economics of work didn't anticipate. Its central case study, a global financial-services company redesigning a customer-order process, demonstrates the methodology's mechanics in granular detail: of the tasks analyzed, 68 were automated outright, 17 were shifted from senior managers to junior staff with generative AI support, 29 analytically demanding tasks were redeployed to an existing global capability center, and 14 senior-employee tasks (like client relationship management) were augmented rather than replaced — combining to produce a 50 percent reduction in workforce hours, a 40 percent reduction in operating cost, and an 18 percent reduction in turnover, benefits the company's original technology-only business case had not anticipated. The article closes with five practical steps: start with the work itself rather than the technology; consider the entire technology stack rather than treating generative AI as a stand-alone tool (the case study combined it with existing RPA and machine learning); think beyond AI to where redeployed tasks might actually be best performed, including by junior talent or global capability centers; explicitly plan how freed-up senior capacity will be redeployed rather than assuming it will self-organize; and treat work design itself as a permanent organizational capability rather than a one-time project.

<span class="src">The “Last Mile” Problem Slowing AI Transformation</span> (HBR, March 2026) shifts the frame from how to redesign a single process to why redesign efforts stall before reaching enterprise scale. Drawing on a closed-door Harvard Business School/Microsoft summit of senior leaders from a dozen large organizations — enthusiastic AI adopters, not laggards, several already running hundreds of active deployments — the article reports that the primary obstacle to transformation is rarely model quality or data availability but the "last mile" where technical capability meets organizational design. It identifies seven structural frictions: the proliferation of ungeneralizable pilots (a global investment bank had documented 250-plus LLM-connected applications with no repeatable path to a standard operating model); a "productivity gap" where individual-level time savings fail to show up on the balance sheet because saved time gets reabsorbed into low-value work instead of being structurally harvested; accumulated "process debt" that AI exposes faster than it can resolve; an "identity problem" around externalizing tribal knowledge that threatens experts' sense of professional worth; an agentic-governance gap where human-in-the-loop controls built for isolated cases collapse under multi-agent architectures; architectural complexity from multi-hyperscaler technology stacks; and an "efficiency trap" where framing AI purely as a cost-reduction tool narrows transformation ambition and triggers defensive behavior from middle management. Against these frictions, the article proposes a four-pillar blueprint: clean-sheet process redesign (asking how a process would be built today with AI agents as first-class participants, rather than retrofitting AI onto an unchanged workflow); strategic knowledge capture (treating tribal knowledge as a strategic asset, positioned to the experts who hold it as legacy-building rather than obsolescence); managing the digital workforce through "agentic control planes" that monitor AI agents' performance and permissions much like an HR system tracks a human workforce; and explicit role redesign and career pathing toward orchestration, verification, and storytelling roles, so that AI adoption reads as career evolution rather than erosion. <span class="src">The “Last Mile” Problem Slowing AI Transformation</span>

<span class="src">The Gen AI Playbook for Organizations</span> (HBR, November 2025) supplies the task-level decision framework the other four sources' methodologies largely assume rather than spell out: given a job broken into component tasks, which specific tasks should go to AI, which to a human, and which to some human-AI combination? The article argues leaders ask the wrong question when they focus on how intelligent gen AI is or how fast it's improving; the right question is how to use it effectively today, task by task, regardless of its current limitations. Its framework crosses two factors — the cost of errors (how serious the consequences of a mistake would be) and the type of knowledge required (explicit, structured information versus tacit knowledge like empathy, ethical reasoning, and contextual judgment) — into four zones. In the "no regrets" zone (low cost, explicit knowledge), AI should simply do the task, since the value comes from speed and scale rather than perfect accuracy, and real breakthroughs often come from tackling work that was previously too tedious or expensive to do at all, not just automating existing work. In the "creative catalyst" zone (low cost, tacit knowledge), AI expands human creative capacity by generating a volume of options — ad taglines, design variations, presentation outlines — for a human to select from and refine, since there's no objectively "correct" creative output to get wrong. In the "quality control" zone (high cost, explicit knowledge — legal contracts, code, financial due diligence), AI produces and a human verifies, since the underlying information is codified but error tolerance is very low. And in the "human-first" zone (high cost, tacit knowledge — hiring, strategy, crisis management), AI may assist but never leads, since mistakes carry serious, hard-to-reverse consequences and the judgment required resists codification. The article's second major contribution is the "paradox of access": because every competitor can use the same gen AI tools, applying them to the same tasks the same way erodes advantage rather than building it, as margins flow to customers and suppliers (echoing the pattern of airline e-ticketing and CAD/ERP software becoming table stakes rather than differentiators) — and worse, a firm's own customers and suppliers can use gen AI to disintermediate it, as has happened with corporate legal departments increasingly pulling work in-house from outside law firms. Sustainable differentiation, the article argues, comes from three sources: applying gen AI differently and faster than competitors while they remain paralyzed by concerns about imperfect accuracy; proprietary, centralized data that makes gen AI perform better inside one organization than another; and the "complementary assets" — unique people, processes, and culture — that make a given deployment of gen AI more valuable in one firm's hands than in a rival's, framing gen AI as a technology that levels a widening set of individual tasks while leaving the underlying strategic differentiation question exactly where it was for other commoditizing technologies. The article closes with a practical warning echoed nowhere else in this concept: freed-up time doesn't automatically become profit, since without deliberate management saved hours evaporate into idle tinkering or low-value busywork, so managers need to explicitly track, redeploy, and incentivize the time gen AI frees up rather than assuming efficiency at the task level will show up unprompted in the P&L. <span class="src">The Gen AI Playbook for Organizations</span>

<span class="src">How to Marry Process Management and AI</span> (HBR, January 2025) approaches work redesign from an older, more established discipline than the other five sources: classical business process management (BPM), the 1990s-era process-reengineering movement that fell out of favor after a wave of failed projects and layoffs but is now, the authors argue, positioned for a genuine revival because AI and BPM reinforce each other in both directions — AI can scale an already-well-managed process far beyond what manual redesign achieves, while an end-to-end, well-managed process is precisely what produces the large volumes of clean, consistent data an AI system needs to be trained and deployed reliably. The article's seven-step methodology (establish a cross-departmental "process owner"; identify who the process actually serves; map the existing process, increasingly via AI-driven process mining rather than manual whiteboard sessions; set performance targets; select the right mix of process enablers, from RPA for narrow processes to gen AI and traditional ML for broader ones; redesign with AI assistance — Deutsche Telekom used Pega's Blueprint tool to streamline 250 of its roughly 800 global HR processes by letting business experts describe workflows in plain language instead of requiring IT translation; and implement with continuous process-mining-based monitoring) is grounded throughout in named case studies: Mars Wrigley's AI-augmented supply chain (a digital twin of its production line, decision-intelligence software, and improved truck utilization), PepsiCo's multiyear process-mining program (which cut a new SAP system's order-rejection rate from 30% to 4% and saved millions in accounts-payable write-offs), and Siemens's, BMW's, and Reckitt's standardized cross-functional order-to-cash processes. Where the concept's other sources largely treat AI-driven redesign as a relatively new discipline built from scratch around AI's dialogue-based or task-decomposable nature, this source's distinctive contribution is showing that a much older, well-tested management discipline — with its own vocabulary of process owners, process mining, and end-to-end cycle-time metrics — provides a complementary and in some ways more mature foundation for the same underlying goal, particularly for large, cross-functional, multi-department processes (order-to-cash being the canonical example) that the other sources' more task- or job-level frameworks don't directly address. <span class="src">How to Marry Process Management and AI</span>

Read together, the six articles describe the same underlying imperative from complementary angles, spanning roughly two years of practitioner-facing research. The February 2024 "Design for Dialogue" piece makes the foundational case that generative AI's dialogue-based, iterative nature breaks the assumptions behind decades of prior process-reengineering methodology, and supplies a framework (task analysis, interaction protocols, feedback loops) for building genuine human-AI collaboration rather than rigid task assignment. The January 2025 "kaizen 2.0" piece demonstrates, through breadth across many industries and functions, that generative AI's greatest value comes from empowering employees to redesign processes themselves rather than having redesign imposed on them as a replacement technology. The same month's "How to Marry Process Management and AI" revives an older, complementary discipline — classical BPM's process-owner, process-mining, and end-to-end-metrics toolkit — best suited to the large, cross-functional processes (order-to-cash being the canonical case) the other sources' task- and job-level frameworks don't directly address. The May 2025 "deconstruct, redeploy, reconstruct" piece supplies a more granular task-level methodology and a single deeply quantified case study showing exactly how that redesign gets executed and what results it can produce. The November 2025 "Gen AI Playbook" piece supplies the task-level decision layer underneath all of the above — the concrete cost-of-errors/type-of-knowledge test for deciding which specific tasks within any redesigned process actually belong to AI, a human, or some combination — plus the strategic argument for why redesigning work around gen AI is a source of competitive differentiation rather than a commodity capability everyone converges on identically. The March 2026 "Last Mile" piece completes the arc by explaining why methodologies like these so often fail to scale past a single successful pilot, and supplies an enterprise-level, governance-and-identity-focused blueprint for the transition the earlier sources largely take for granted. All six explicitly warn against the same failure mode — deploying AI as an isolated substitution tool bolted onto unchanged jobs, processes, or governance structures — and several identify multi-agent, increasingly autonomous systems as the direction this work redesign is heading next, with human oversight remaining essential throughout rather than fading out as agent capability grows.

## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses how the "deconstruct, redeploy, reconstruct" methodology should handle jobs or tasks where legal, regulatory, or safety requirements constrain which tasks can be redeployed to junior talent, automation, or an external capability center — the financial-services case study does not mention whether any tasks were excluded from redesign for such reasons.
- The HBR article's autonomous-agent examples (DoNotPay, the Stanford multi-agent research) are not integrated with the MIT SMR article's task-level redesign methodology; neither source addresses how an organization would apply "deconstruct, redeploy, reconstruct" thinking specifically to deciding which tasks should be handed to autonomous agents versus augmented, automated conventionally, or redeployed to humans.
