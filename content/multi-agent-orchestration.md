## Concept

As organizations move beyond isolated single-agent AI use cases, a new coordination challenge emerges: multi-agentic systems, in which several AI agents interact, share data, and act — often without direct human oversight — to jointly accomplish work. PwC illustrates the concept concretely with a smart complaints-management system: a classification agent processes incoming complaints from email, chat, or calls using generative AI to detect category, urgency, and sentiment; a routing-and-escalation agent decides, based on severity and customer profile, whether to send the case to self-service, a human agent, or immediate escalation; a resolution agent, trained on historical cases, recommends probable fixes and updates its suggestions based on customer feedback and outcomes; and a sentiment-monitoring agent tracks how sentiment evolves across open cases, flagging those at risk of escalating or triggering regulatory concern. Three forces are converging to accelerate adoption of systems like this: the sheer volume and velocity of enterprise data has outpaced what centralized models can process, and multi-agent systems let organizations act on information closer to its source, often in real time; static, monolithic models struggle to keep pace with fast-changing environments, whereas specialized agents can adapt and adjust strategy on the fly; and agents interacting in complex environments can generate genuinely novel solutions, leading organizations to increasingly view multi-agent systems not just as automation tooling but as co-creative partners. <span class="src">Mastering multi-agent AI systems for competitive advantage</span>

The central design challenge, per PwC, is not technical integration alone but organizational design: leaders must approach multi-agentic systems the way they would structure a high-performing team, through deliberate orchestration. Orchestration rests on three components. Role clarity means every agent is assigned clear boundaries, responsibilities, and escalation paths, just as a well-run team assigns roles to its members. Communication protocols give agents standardized ways to share state, make joint decisions, and resolve conflicts with one another — functioning for agent interoperability much as APIs function for conventional system interoperability. Supervisory logic provides a meta-layer of oversight, whether human or algorithmic, capable of detecting anomalies, prioritizing competing actions, and applying ethical or compliance filters in real time. Absent these three elements, what starts as straightforward automation can quickly degrade into misalignment or, worse, decision pathways with real-world consequences that no one can trace after the fact. Concretely, three failure modes emerge without orchestration: coordination complexity, where agents duplicate each other's work, act at cross-purposes, or trigger unintended feedback loops; trust and explainability gaps, which are especially dangerous in regulated industries where autonomous decisions must remain transparent and auditable to both regulators and internal stakeholders; and integration-at-scale friction, since moving a promising proof-of-concept into enterprise-wide deployment demands careful integration into existing workflows, data environments, and control systems that a small pilot never had to contend with. <span class="src">Mastering multi-agent AI systems for competitive advantage</span>

To capture the upside of multi-agent systems while managing these risks, PwC recommends three must-do strategies. First, design with intent rather than simply adding scale — the article's framing is that adding more AI agents to a system without architectural intention is "like adding more musicians to an orchestra without a conductor," so leaders should focus each agent's addition on a clear purpose rather than treating proliferation itself as progress. Second, balance autonomy with control: while autonomy is what enables the speed and responsiveness multi-agent systems promise, unchecked autonomy invites real risk, so executive teams need to explicitly define thresholds for how much independence agents are given, with particular care in domains involving finance, legal exposure, or public trust. Third, invest in explainability and governance from the outset — as systems grow more complex, the need for transparency grows with them, so multi-agentic systems should be built to be auditable, traceable, and aligned with corporate governance frameworks from day one rather than retrofitted once problems surface. For organizations taking their first step, PwC recommends beginning with a strategic agentic-readiness assessment that identifies where decision processes currently slow down due to organizational fragmentation and where semi-autonomous coordination could create measurable value — these are the prime candidates for a first multi-agentic pilot. From there, a controlled test with a small constellation of agents, governed by clear interaction rules, should be evaluated not only on task performance but on emergent behavior, with the orchestration layer itself treated as something to iterate on as new insights emerge, rather than a fixed design decided once at the outset. <span class="src">Mastering multi-agent AI systems for competitive advantage</span>
