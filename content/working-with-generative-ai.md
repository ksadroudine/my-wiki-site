## Summary

Working well with generative AI depends on knowing where it helps and where it misleads, and on building deliberate habits of use rather than relying on access to the best model. Mollick's co-intelligence rules and the BCG and Harvard field experiment show large gains in speed, quality and skill-leveling inside the jagged frontier of AI capability, but worse performance outside it, which makes human judgment and Centaur and Cyborg working patterns essential. Practical discipline includes self-auditing sessions, consulting a virtual board of AI personas, saving successful sessions as custom assistants and moving from simple context-rich prompting toward configuring agents that are directed rather than prompted.

## Concept

Co-Intelligence frames current generative AI as a general-purpose technology comparable in scope to steam power or the internet, but argues its impact on work and education may exceed theirs, because it acts as a co-intelligence that directly augments or substitutes for human thinking rather than mainly automating mechanical or physical labor. Early studies finding productivity improvements of 20-80% across many job types are cited as evidence of scale, while noting that these gains fall within the plausible range for earlier general-purpose technologies. The book proposes four rules for working with AI productively instead of avoiding it or over-trusting it. The first is to always invite AI to the table, because with no instruction manual the only reliable way to learn its value and limits for a task is direct, repeated experimentation, including attempts that disappoint or unnerve, since even a failure shows where the technology's boundary lies for that work. The second is to be the human in the loop, since human judgment remains essential, in part because an AI implicitly optimizes to give an answer the user finds satisfying, which can outweigh accuracy and let a confident but fabricated answer persuade an insistent user. <span class="src">Co-Intelligence</span>

The third rule is to treat AI like a person but tell it what kind of person it is. Research cited in the book finds that models give better answers when a request is framed as personally consequential, such as important to the user's career, and that explicit guidance on the persona to adopt improves the interest and usefulness of output, although the framing carries real risk, since treating AI as a person can create unrealistic expectations, obscure its nature as software and shape emotional relationships in ways that may not serve well-being, so the book offers it as a pragmatic heuristic rather than a claim of personhood. The fourth rule is to assume this is the worst AI you will ever use, which keeps today's limitations from being treated as permanent. Hallucination is presented as a structural consequence of how models work rather than a solvable bug, because a language model has no ground-truth store and generates each output as a statistically plausible continuation, so tasks needing exact recall are especially prone, models are generally not optimized to say they do not know, and a request to explain its reasoning after an answer yields another plausible piece of generated text rather than an account of the process that actually produced the answer, since the model has no introspective access to it. <span class="src">Co-Intelligence</span>

The largest study of the question is a pre-registered field experiment by social scientists from Harvard, Warwick, MIT and Wharton with Boston Consulting Group, using 758 consultants, 7% of the firm's consulting force, on 18 realistic tasks spanning creative work such as proposing shoe ideas for an underserved market, analytical work such as market segmentation, writing and marketing work such as press releases and persuasion such as an inspirational internal memo. Consultants were randomly given plain GPT-4, the publicly available version with no special fine-tuning or prompting, or no AI, and human and AI graders who agreed closely rated completion, speed and quality. AI-assisted consultants completed 12.2% more tasks, finished 25.1% faster and produced 40% higher-quality output, consistently across every measure. AI also leveled skill, since the lowest-scoring consultants on a baseline assessment gained most, by 43%, while top performers improved less, a pattern the source compares to the steam shovel erasing the difference between good and bad diggers and which it says changes what skill differentiation means. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>

The study's central concept is the jagged frontier, the fact that AI capability is unevenly distributed across tasks of similar apparent difficulty. Pictured as an irregular fortress wall with some towers jutting out and others folding back, it puts equally hard-looking tasks on opposite sides of an invisible boundary, so GPT-4 handles open-ended idea generation easily but reliably fails to write a poem of exactly 50 words because it works with tokens rather than words and has no native sense of word count. Because the boundary does not track intuitive difficulty, users can learn where it lies for their own work only through extensive hands-on use, and the authors say plainly that no one, including a model's developers, knows the full range of a frontier model's abilities in advance. Co-Intelligence applies the same idea as the reason that familiarity from repeated use matters, since reasoning about the frontier in the abstract does not work. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span> <span class="src">Co-Intelligence</span>

The experiment included a task engineered to fall outside GPT-4's frontier by exploiting a known blind spot so that the model would give a wrong but convincing answer, which proved hard to construct because GPT-4 solved most attempts. Unaided consultants got it right 84% of the time, whereas consultants using AI did worse, at 60-70% accuracy, while writing up their wrong answers in better prose, a direct overreliance effect. The source links it to a related study by a co-author in which recruiters using high-quality AI became lazier and less attentive and made worse hiring decisions than recruiters using low-quality AI or none, a dynamic called falling asleep at the wheel, in which an AI good enough to seem consistently trustworthy removes the incentive to stay critically engaged. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>

Two patterns are proposed to capture the benefits while avoiding overreliance. Centaur work keeps a clear division of labor between person and machine, routing whole tasks to whichever is stronger given where each falls relative to the frontier, as in choosing a statistical technique personally while delegating graph production to the AI. Cyborg work blends effort at a finer grain within one task, such as having the AI complete a sentence the human began and moving back and forth across the frontier repeatedly, and the source recommends it for AI-assisted writing. Neither is exotic, since both emerge from sustained hands-on use, which is how a person comes to perceive the shape of their own frontier, and combining human and AI effort also recovers variety, since AI output, while better on average, was more homogeneous across consultants. Co-Intelligence recommends starting by inviting AI into everything, letting Centaur-style task switching emerge as the frontier sharpens and Cyborg-style blending develop organically after that, and it offers a task-allocation framework along a spectrum of trust. Just Me tasks stay human either because AI is not yet useful or because a person chooses to keep them, such as raising children, making important decisions or expressing values, Delegated tasks are assigned to AI and checked, typically tedious or low-importance work although delegated does not mean low stakes, and Automated tasks are left to AI entirely, still a small category but expected to grow in well-bounded domains. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span> <span class="src">Co-Intelligence</span>

The book highlights an organizational risk in shadow AI use going unreported. A worker who discovers how to automate much of their own job has a rational reason not to disclose it, since the natural managerial response to a visible productivity breakthrough is often to fire people and save money rather than reinvest the capacity, so organizations that want to capture employees' bottom-up discoveries must deliberately and visibly reduce the fear of disclosure through incentives and psychological safety, not merely permission. <span class="src">Co-Intelligence</span>

Personal discipline determines who gains. In MIT Sloan Management Review, Vipin Gupta describes building, after a year of daily use with no benchmark for whether his sessions were any good, a reusable macro-prompt that audits a significant AI session against 30 habits under five goals. He grounds it in a randomized field experiment, including MIT Sloan's Jackson Lu, that assigned 250 employees at a Chinese technology consulting firm to ChatGPT or not and found that access made employees significantly more creative by supervisor and outside-evaluator judgment, but only among those with strong metacognitive strategies, meaning the ability to reflect on their thinking, recognize gaps and adjust, so the audit is a way of installing metacognition where it does not occur naturally rather than of judging the AI's output. <span class="src">Audit Yourself to Get More From GenAI</span>

The five goals are set up, refine, verify, own and systematize. Setting up means passing the intern test, giving role, context, constraints and materials as if briefing a new hire, and the highest-leverage and most skipped habit is stating negative constraints, such as no generic SWOT and no hedging. Refining means iterating on your own instructions instead of just asking for a better answer, epitomized by asking what question you should be asking that you are not. Verifying means treating fluent output as a new category of quality risk, illustrated by a near-miss in which a summary of a familiar regulatory history came back beautifully written, logically structured and wrong on two key points and almost reached a committee meeting, countered with a temporal-awareness check on the date of the model's latest information and a confidence stress-test of each factual claim. Owning means imposing a personal voice and position on output that defaults to hedged, generically competent prose, including through a virtual personal board of directors, and systematizing means converting a successful session into a reusable macro-prompt template with variable placeholders rather than losing the accumulated context when the chat closes. Applied to a USD 5 billion competitive due-diligence analysis, the habits surfaced a material gap between a competitor's public narrative and its internal employee-sentiment data and caught a USD 42 million enforcement action that the AI's own output had understated, evidence that the discipline rather than the model produced the value. <span class="src">Audit Yourself to Get More From GenAI</span>

The virtual personal board of directors, developed in a companion article cited by the audit as the source of its own-stage technique, is a set of AI personas modeled on real and archetypal leaders, each with a distinct lens, consulted through structured multi-persona prompts. The author's board includes a tactical persona modeled on Sun Tzu for power dynamics, an empathetic strategist modeled on former PepsiCo CEO Indra Nooyi, a bold realist modeled on Mark Cuban, a systems builder modeled on Meg Whitman, a visionary disrupter modeled on Steve Jobs, a moral reconciler modeled on Nelson Mandela and others spanning ethics, storytelling and even a family member's engineering view. It is framed as a hybrid brain trust rather than a replacement for real advisers, who contribute trust, depth, shared history and emotional intelligence that no algorithm replicates, while the virtual board contributes breadth, always-on availability, deliberately engineered cognitive diversity and psychological safety for testing ideas too early or unpopular to share with people. In a build-versus-buy decision about acquiring a fintech company's AI-native loan-servicing platform or building one, it surfaced post-acquisition integration costs, IP ownership and roadmap control, the human experience of organizational change and whether nostalgia for the legacy system was driving the instinct to build, and the advice is to start with three to five personas, treat the board as a living system and seek perspective rather than perfection. <span class="src">How I Built a Personal Board of Directors With GenAI</span> <span class="src">Audit Yourself to Get More From GenAI</span>

The two articles describe one author's evolving practice and converge on the point that AI widens the gap between disciplined and undisciplined users rather than closing it, since people who skip deliberate practice generate more volume without more insight, a pattern researchers have called unsustainable intensity, while those who build reusable habits and structures compound their capability with every session. <span class="src">Audit Yourself to Get More From GenAI</span> <span class="src">How I Built a Personal Board of Directors With GenAI</span>

Custom assistants are the practical vehicle for the systematize habit. Most major platforms let a user create a persistent assistant, ChatGPT's custom GPT, Claude's Project or Google Gemini's Gem, that stores background files and recurring instructions so they need not be re-uploaded and retyped for each similar task, and Alexandra Samuel reports building assistants for marketing content, strategy sounding, tech support, research summarization and project tracking, most in under 20 minutes. Building one has four steps, choosing a platform for its strengths, such as ChatGPT for voice conversation, Claude for writing style and Gemini for integrating Google Docs and Gmail, experimenting first in an ordinary chat and synthesizing what worked into draft instructions, even asking the AI to write them, writing the instructions in the second person with persona, output format, goals and tone, and supplying reference files as examples or context. Use cases fall into writing, marketing and communications, how-tos and troubleshooting such as tech support, tax helpers and equipment manuals, productivity and project management such as sorting tasks and extracting action items from transcripts, and strategic advice, coaching and training, an always-on sounding board with configurable tone, similar to the virtual board but as a single persona. An assistant is never right at first and improves through explicit feedback, revised instructions and added files, with the advantage over a long chat thread that each session starts already loaded with its context instead of risking a thread that forgets earlier instructions. <span class="src">How to Build Your Own AI Assistant</span>

Prompting in practical terms is shaping a model's output through the wording, context and structure of the input. Mollick argues against treating it as a discipline with secret techniques, since models are improving rapidly at inferring intent, heavily engineered prompts full of superlatives such as act as the smartest person ever do not outperform plain, direct requests, and the returns to prompt-crafting should keep shrinking. What reliably helps is context and constraints. Because models predict likely continuations, unconstrained prompts yield generic output that mirrors common training patterns, and telling the model what persona or perspective to take, what style to use, what to avoid and what source material to draw on breaks that default, although assigning a named identity such as Bill Gates does not confer that person's expertise, and pasting reference text, asking for a summary or extension, or using retrieval where available narrows the space of completions. <span class="src">A guide to prompting AI (for what it is worth)</span>

For demanding tasks, prompts can be structured like a program written in prose, with explicit, step-by-step instructions that make intermediate output checkable and tend to improve accuracy, and Chain of Thought prompting, which shows the model an example of the reasoning before the real request, is one documented technique. A few phrases reliably shift behavior across models, namely asking the model to be creative, to show its work or cite sources, to write and explain code or to produce a draft rather than decline, while most other prompt hacks do not generalize, and skill comes from sustained interactive practice of trying, refining and iterating rather than from a fixed template. <span class="src">A guide to prompting AI (for what it is worth)</span>

Prompting also democratizes innovation, because a well-written prompt is a program that a non-programmer can write and share. A four-level progression describes how. At level one people use pre-made prompts written and tested by others, a useful start although a prompt built for one context often needs adaptation. At level two they customize or write prompts for their own needs, which needs testing and revision like any prompt, at level three they build blueprints, prompts that generate other prompts, which package an expert's prompt-crafting skill for people who lack it, such as a prompt that generates a tailored AI tutor for a topic, and at level four they simply describe a goal to an autonomous agent, illustrated by an early GPT-4-based coding agent, which plans and executes the steps and is expected to make the manual levels less necessary. The innovation compounds only if practitioners share what they build with peers, the harder, cooperative half of the pattern. <span class="src">Innovation through prompting</span>

Sloan and Glaser describe configuration as the successor to prompting, distinguishing prompting, which is reactive and held in a human-held thread, from directing intelligence, in which an agent is configured through context, meaning what it can access, capabilities, meaning what it can do, and orientation, meaning what it attends to, and several agents with different orientations can be orchestrated against the same data. They describe four approaches to discovery, namely multiple lenses, with agents applying Porter, VRIO, Rumelt and Martin, surfacing silences, meaning what is in the data but absent from discourse, bridging levels, tracing a symptom to a cause at another organizational level, and stress-testing categories against operational reality. Their examples include a metal-parts manufacturer whose four framework agents jointly pointed to narrowing scope to aerospace, a professional-services practice whose agent found that about a third of interview discussions implicitly depended on one leader, a diversified industrial whose ROIC decline traced to a growth-weighted capital allocation formula and a concrete company whose rejection categories hid a recurring site-not-ready cause, and the guidance is to configure for discovery rather than answers, treat unexpected output as signal, evaluate proposals rather than conclusions and track which proposals are advanced or rejected. <span class="src">Stop Prompting AI. Start Directing It</span>

The knowledge-work use of agentic coding tools is the same shift from conversation to delegated, tool-using work. MIT Sloan's Rama Ramakrishnan argues that such tools suit knowledge work because of multistep reasoning, adaptive execution and tool use, with examples in competitive intelligence, meeting preparation and campaign variants, and cautions to restrict access to trusted folders because of prompt-injection risk and to review proposed actions. <span class="src">Putting AI to work</span>

## Open Questions

- The book's later material on AI as coworker in specific domains and on AI-driven organizational restructuring was only partially captured in the notes available for this wiki. <span class="src">Co-Intelligence</span>
- The jagged-frontier source summarizes a working paper that had not been peer reviewed, and its author flags that any simplification errors are his own, so the underlying paper should be consulted for full methodology. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>
- The practice sources do not say how the habits should be taught or enforced at team or organizational scale, or how a manager could verify that people apply the five-goal audit rather than only claiming to. <span class="src">Audit Yourself to Get More From GenAI</span>
- The virtual-board technique depends on an AI faithfully simulating a named person's judgment from public information, and the sources do not address how closely a persona reflects that person's reasoning rather than a stereotype of their public reputation. <span class="src">How I Built a Personal Board of Directors With GenAI</span>
- The custom-assistant source does not address the privacy and governance implications of uploading sensitive background files and instructions to a third-party platform's persistent assistant feature. <span class="src">How to Build Your Own AI Assistant</span>
- The prompting sources predate models with very large context windows, native tool use and reasoning modes, so they do not say how prompting practice should adapt, and the directing-intelligence examples are illustrative composites without comparative evidence that agent systems find more insight than skilled prompting. <span class="src">A guide to prompting AI (for what it is worth)</span> <span class="src">Innovation through prompting</span> <span class="src">Stop Prompting AI.
