## Summary

Economists argue that whether AI helps workers depends on design choices, with automation-first deployment favouring capital while new-task-creating AI raises the value of human expertise. China shows the displacement side at speed, as AI-generated video, driver-assist trucks and robots reshape jobs faster than policy can respond.

## Highlights

- The Brookings Hamilton Project paper "Building Pro-Worker Artificial Intelligence" (Acemoglu, Autor and Johnson, February 2026) defines pro-worker AI as AI that increases the demand for human expertise, so that people become more valuable and are paid more. <span class="src">Pro-worker AI, explained</span>
- The paper sorts technologies into five types — labor-augmenting, capital-augmenting, automating, expertise-leveling and new-task-creating — and treats only new-task-creating technologies as unambiguously pro-worker. <span class="src">Pro-worker AI, explained</span>
- Simon Johnson calls replacing people with machines "the path of least resistance that does not require much management imagination," and argues companies using AI only to compress existing work may leave value unrealised. <span class="src">Pro-worker AI, explained</span>
- Schneider Electric's troubleshooting assistant for electricians, which uses images, hardware data and a database of documented problems, halved the time to complete maintenance reports and illustrates AI that extends field expertise rather than replacing it. <span class="src">Pro-worker AI, explained</span>
- Daron Acemoglu argues organizations are deploying AI as automation when it is really an information technology, which helps explain weak macroeconomic productivity gains, since automation benefits capital owners while workers would gain from insight and judgment support. <span class="src">Putting AI to work</span>
- China's microdrama industry (100 billion yuan and about 700,000 direct jobs in 2025) saw production costs fall up to 90% and actors' pay fall by half within months of ByteDance's Seedance 2.0 launch, replacing crews with "card-drawers" who prompt and sequence AI scenes. <span class="src">China’s AI drive threatens the world’s largest workforce</span>
- China's 38 million lorry drivers carry over 70% of freight, and driver-assist systems from Inceptio allow about 30% fewer drivers and 3-4% lower transport costs, while JD.com plans 3 million robots, 1 million unmanned vehicles and 100,000 drones over five years. <span class="src">China’s AI drive threatens the world’s largest workforce</span>
- Chinese courts have ruled it illegal to dismiss a worker simply because AI can do the job, firms must try retraining before firing, and policy advisers have floated robot-productivity taxes and AI-unemployment insurance. <span class="src">China’s AI drive threatens the world’s largest workforce</span>

## Concept

The pro-worker framing starts from the premise, associated with Acemoglu's book Power and Progress, that technology's effects are not preordained and that organizations have agency over which version of AI they build. In the MIT Sloan explainer on the Brookings paper, Johnson distinguishes productivity gained by lowering the cost of existing work from productivity gained by expanding what people can do. The pulse oximeter illustrates the ambiguity of expertise-leveling tools, which let technicians do what once needed a phlebotomist, lab technician and clinician, and so make some workers more capable while reducing demand for other expertise. By contrast, Ethernet, fibre cabling and occupancy-aware building systems created new work for electricians. For AI, the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office's AI-assisted prior-art search shows both outcomes: it saves examiner time if it only speeds the same work, but makes specialised judgment more valuable if it enables deeper analysis. <span class="src">Pro-worker AI, explained</span>

The business case is that, as AI tools spread, competitive advantage depends on how work is redesigned around them. Investments should therefore be evaluated beyond time and labor saved, asking what new capabilities, work and valued expertise they create and how product and customer experience improve. Johnson and colleagues add a policy agenda (public investment in healthcare and education, tax changes that stop favouring capital over labor, antitrust, worker voice and reform of occupational licensing), but the article stresses that practitioners can act now by asking at the start of each initiative whether the system lets employees do something more valuable, makes judgment more or less necessary, and creates new responsibilities and learning paths. <span class="src">Pro-worker AI, explained</span>

MIT Sloan Management Review's roundup of April 2026 reports Acemoglu's complementary diagnosis, that automation-oriented use explains why AI is not lifting aggregate productivity, and his call for "pro-worker, pro-human technologies" that decentralise information to front-line decision makers, along with greater vigilance over mergers and acquisitions that align startups with capital owners. The same roundup carries MIT's guidance that agentic coding tools can be used for knowledge work, covered in [working with generative ai](#/concept/working-with-generative-ai), and a typology of AI startups, covered in [agentic ai startups vs incumbents](#/concept/agentic-ai-startups-vs-incumbents). <span class="src">Putting AI to work</span>

The Economist's August 2026 briefing shows the automation path at national scale. China treats AI dominance as nearly existential and sees it as a remedy for an ageing workforce (working-age population down 25% by 2050), yet the party worries about displacement because, in the words of economist Cai Fang, displacement often precedes and exceeds job creation. Microdramas fell to AI in a matter of months; trucking is moving gradually through driver assistance while regulation still requires a human at the wheel; China installed almost 300,000 industrial robots in 2024 (over half the world's total), and humanoids are expected to reach 50,000 units in 2026 (Morgan Stanley), still needing human trainers for a decade or two. Youth prospects are weak amid a property crisis, and in July the government omitted an urban job-creation target from its five-year plan for the first time in decades. Chinese incentives differ from American ones, since firms are not rewarded for announcing AI-justified layoffs and officials press them to add jobs, while the safety net is thin and the gig workforce is growing toward 320 million. <span class="src">China’s AI drive threatens the world’s largest workforce</span>

## Open Questions

- The Brookings paper's typology is conceptual, and the sources give no measured examples of firms whose pro-worker deployments outperformed automation-first ones; Schneider's result is a reported time saving, not a financial outcome.
- The China briefing relies on interviews and anecdote for the microdrama and trucking examples, and does not say how enforcement of the court rulings against AI-justified dismissals will work for private firms.
- It is unclear whether the proposed robot-productivity taxes or AI-unemployment insurance are under serious policy consideration, since the briefing says no ambitious scheme seems likely soon.
