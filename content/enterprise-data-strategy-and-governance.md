## Summary

Enterprise data strategy treats data as a managed organizational asset. Accountability for a dataset is separated from ownership, senior roles such as the Chief Data Officer coordinate it, and the strategy starts small around the most critical business needs. Value then depends on preventing quality problems at the source through collaboration between the people who create data and the people who use it. It also depends on monetization that begins with existing partners and chooses between direct and indirect models and between selling raw data, insights or finished products. Cross-functional leadership is needed, as in marketing's role and in Caterpillar's business-owned data platform led by its chief executive. Managers must also ask the right questions about the data behind AI models, and treat risks to data quality, protection, availability, compliance and exposure as a business problem and not only an IT problem.

## Concept

Modern Data Strategy argues that data deserves the discipline applied to any other significant corporate asset, such as a fleet of trucks or a real-estate portfolio, rather than being allowed to accumulate as a byproduct of business processes. An organization would weigh a new physical asset against a specific business need, and data investment should likewise be justified against business priority, with dedicated roles, most visibly the Chief Data Officer, whose seniority gives real authority across departments and not only within IT, and with formal management domains, namely data quality, governance, architecture, warehousing, master data management, interoperability, privacy and security, treated as disciplines with their own standards and certifications. The work behind this is mostly unglamorous, since preparing data for analysis, often called wrangling or munging, commonly takes 50-80% of an analyst's time. <span class="src">Modern Data Strategy</span>

The book identifies a common failure mode in which business units behaved as though they owned the data in their systems, which was rational for each unit's efficiency and competitive position but fragmented and duplicated data across the enterprise and hampered enterprise management. The remedy is to separate data accountability, meaning who answers for a dataset's quality, security and appropriate use, from data ownership, which should sit at the enterprise level instead of with whichever department generates or first uses the data. <span class="src">Modern Data Strategy</span> <span class="src">1. Evolution to Modern Data Management</span>

Scale drives the discipline. Big data is defined by volume, variety, a complex mix of structured and unstructured formats, and velocity, the speed at which data changes, severe enough on all three that conventional data management cannot cope, and this shift moved pipeline design from ETL, in which data is transformed to fit its target before loading, to ELT, in which raw data is loaded first and transformed only when an analytical need arises, because organizations wanted to retain as much raw data as possible and preserve optionality rather than discard what might later matter. Raw-data hoarding is not itself a strategy, though, since a frequently cited reason big-data initiatives fail is not a shortage of new data but an inability to manage the data already held, and research on elite data organizations found their two most consistent practices to be getting governance genuinely right and treating information as a valuable asset in practice, not just in mission statements. <span class="src">Modern Data Strategy</span> <span class="src">2. Big Data and Data Management</span>

Whether data should be formally valued and carried on the balance sheet remains unresolved. Most organizations do not estimate data value with the cost, market-value or revenue approaches applied to other intangibles, and research cited in the book suggests part of the reason is financial, since treating data costs as an expense rather than a capitalized asset avoids showing data on the balance sheet, with its tax implications. The book still sees practical reasons to value data internally, namely greater accountability for quality and use, a basis for measuring IT effectiveness and a stronger case for information-systems spending, and proposes beginning with an internal data balance sheet that tracks the value of data assets without resolving the formal accounting question. Candidate approaches include cost, for example for third-party data, fair market value, future revenue and indirect measures such as the cost of poor quality, regulatory non-compliance or failure to adapt to demand. <span class="src">Modern Data Strategy</span> <span class="src">3. Valuing Data as an Asset</span> <span class="src">4. Physical Asset Management vs. Data Management</span>

The inventory analogy extends beyond valuation. Fleckenstein and Fellows map inventory management onto data management across four dimensions, which are cost, where ordering and holding costs correspond to the cost of obtaining, loading, licensing and administering data and losses from poor quality correspond to wastage, inventory types, where raw data resembles raw material, cleansed data finished goods, partially cleansed data work in progress and profiling, cleansing and metadata spare parts, stewardship, and obsolescence, where reclaiming space corresponds to moving data among online storage, near-line storage and archives, which makes search, integration and reporting more efficient. A data steward is accountable for a data domain across its life cycle and across business processes rather than for one application, acts as the go-to resource for data questions and escalates issues to a governance council, as inventory managers do for stock. The analogy does not give data an explicit value but approximates it through the resources needed to manage it. <span class="src">4. Physical Asset Management vs. Data Management</span>

Execution follows a maturity path. Organizations typically progress through grassroots and IT-led efforts until business and IT must collaborate under an overarching data strategy that advances data maturity, coordinates existing projects and supports business goals, ideally driven by an executive who is a data person and a team that includes data people as well as process and technology people. Implementation should begin with a small coordinated subset of domains focused on the most critical business needs and should use existing infrastructure, formalizing informal initiatives such as stewardship, since an all-inclusive strategy is costly, hard to orient and prone to failure compared with an incremental approach. <span class="src">5. Leading a Data Strategy</span> <span class="src">6. Implementing a Data Strategy</span>

Data quality is the foundation, and MIT Sloan Management Review's research argues that most organizations get stuck in one of two dysfunctional modes. In the unmanaged mode data moves like a daisy chain, each department using data for its own job and generating new data for the next group, with people seeing themselves through functional lenses such as salesperson or market researcher rather than as participants in a shared ecosystem, so errors are corrected individually, with difficulty, or missed and compounded downstream. Organized cleanup follows, with a central team and tooling that find and fix errors faster and more cheaply, but gains are modest because fixing errors correctly needs business context a central team lacks, which traps the organization in endless reactive repair. The breakthrough is proactive prevention, a mindset shift in which people see themselves as both data creators and data customers, customers clarifying requirements, both sides measuring data against them, creators running improvement projects and controls that stop errors at the source, and the arrangement evolving with the business. <span class="src">How to Get Proactive About Data Quality</span>

At scale this needs a core team that coordinates the program, trains creators and customers, maintains shared dashboards and informs senior management, plus connector roles that recur under titles such as embedded data manager, data product manager, data ambassador or quality champion. HelloFresh's journey was neither quick nor linear. It stayed in unmanaged mode too long as a fast-growing startup, then moved to cleanup under a central engineering team that lacked understanding of how teams used the data, which frustrated the international marketing analytics team and the International Operations Business Intelligence team, whose weekly global report to the C-suite suffered from inconsistent data from many geographies. That team broke through first, building a quality framework around data contracts, which are quality service-level agreements, and local business intelligence teams took on creator responsibility, with clear communication between the two, but gains stayed within that team. <span class="src">How to Get Proactive About Data Quality</span>

A 2020 companywide survey then made the scale visible, with 61% of respondents reporting unreliable data, 53% reporting incorrect data and nearly 84% relying on manual workarounds, which triggered CEO intervention. HelloFresh formed a companywide data quality working group to formalize creator and customer roles and standard processes, launched a data literacy program, moved to a data mesh architecture that formalized creators' quality responsibility, hired its first vice president of data and later embedded data product owners and engineers in business domains. Its self-service platform now ingests data in minutes rather than months, with quality, governance and observability built in, though decentralizing data product teams and exploring AI-driven self-healing data remain unfinished. The lesson is that better tools or a dedicated team are not enough, since the change was organizational, centered on the creator and customer relationship, with accountability for quality residing in the business rather than in a specialist function. <span class="src">How to Get Proactive About Data Quality</span>

Monetization is the downstream question of turning proprietary data into revenue, a strategy gaining momentum as AI makes analysis easier and examples accumulate, with Amazon's advertising business built on customer-interest data bringing in USD 56 billion last year, Walmart's online advertising business at roughly USD 4 billion, much of LinkedIn's USD 16 billion in revenue tied to user data sold to recruiters, Mastercard and Visa advisory divisions selling transaction insights, and Reddit's stock rising 12% on news of its 2024 data-licensing deal with OpenAI. HBR's study of more than 30 organizations and 12 senior executives across retail, media, tech, manufacturing and marketing finds most companies struggle to choose an approach, often building infrastructure for a year or two before clarifying what to build or who would buy it. The most successful strategies start close to home with existing partners, suppliers and customers, who understand the business context that makes the data valuable, with whom relationships make use cases and deals easier, who can buy a new offering as a line-item add-on that scales quickly, and who avoid the privacy and custodianship risk of selling to unaffiliated brokers, since sharing agreements often bar such sales. Quick broker deals look simple but create less value and expose customers and suppliers to leak risk without their involvement. <span class="src">How to Monetize Your Data</span>

Whatever the path, privacy, regulatory, reputational and security risk must be managed from day one, with benchmark data aggregated and anonymized and legal and risk teams involved before launch, and monetizing poorly organized, low-quality or incomplete data reliably backfires, which makes data quality a prerequisite. Two structural choices follow. Direct monetization charges customers or partners for data or data-based offerings, typically by subscription, as with Tesco's Dunnhumby, which grew from point-of-sale data into a standalone analytics business, while indirect monetization embeds data in existing offerings at no extra charge and suits high-margin businesses where differentiation matters more, as when consulting firms bundle proprietary insights or Sony Interactive Entertainment gives PlayStation creators rich gameplay and marketing data free, so that better-informed creators build better games that increase platform engagement. Indirect monetization lacks P&L visibility but can raise the return on data investment, as one technology provider found its highest retention among customers using its data offerings, a training company found skills-demand benchmarks improved satisfaction and retention, and a manufacturer sharing service and inventory scorecards with suppliers reduced bottlenecks and improved margins. <span class="src">How to Monetize Your Data</span>

The second choice is packaging, with three tiers of rising pricing potential and required investment. Raw data is simplest for the seller but leaves extracting value to the buyer and suits buyers with strong analytics capability, such as data brokers and tech companies. Insight services give custom analysis that spares the customer the work and protects privacy because sensitive raw detail never leaves the seller, as with Mastercard Advisors' anonymized transaction-network insights. Commercially ready solutions package dashboards, workflows, machine-learning models and insights as standalone products or inside existing digital products and command the highest prices because they demand least of the buyer, as with ADP's compensation-benchmarking product, Instacart's ad-targeting platform, which generated an estimated USD 958 million in 2024, and FordDirect's dealer analytics platform, which combined dealer websites, CRM and back-office data into one customer-journey view and drove a 40% sales increase and 22% lower cost per lead for dealers in targeted segments. <span class="src">How to Monetize Your Data</span>

Forrester's research adds cross-functional coordination, examined through the B2C chief marketing officer, whose position shows both the temptation and the danger of a single-function data strategy. Four forces of data deprecation, namely tightening privacy laws, browser and operating-system restrictions, consumer adoption of privacy tools and walled gardens such as Amazon, Facebook and Google, constrain third-party data and push 76% of surveyed B2C marketing executives to collect more first-party data, but acquiring more data does not yield better targeting or insight. Companies are already awash in customer data yet remain fragmented, reactive and myopic, an industry norm in which, as a CVS Health executive put it, suggesting less data risks looking unsophisticated, useful data sits dormant in silos, with a quarter of marketing decision-makers saying silos degrade their insights, data quality decays so that stale data undermines personalization, and hoarding is a standing risk because cheap cloud storage has removed the old cost incentive to minimize footprint, as T-Mobile's 2021 breach showed when it was found to have needlessly retained sensitive information on more than 40 million prospective and former customers, leading to a USD 500 million class-action settlement. <span class="src">The CMO’s Guide To An Enterprise Data Strategy</span>

Forrester's prescription is that CMOs must formalize a seat in enterprise data strategy rather than pursue a marketing-only agenda that would entrench silos, working with the CDO, CIO and finance and with an orchestra of IT, data science, risk and compliance, marketing, sales and service, and CX, as PepsiCo did over seven months in building its enterprise strategy with data scientists, strategists, stewards and engineers. CMOs hold the keys to customer understanding, which gives them four responsibilities, surfacing consumer insights beyond marketing's own uses, as when a financial services firm uses marketing interaction data to anticipate credit card applications and adjust onboarding, turning the strategy into market actions with measurable value, since marketing owns CRM, loyalty and campaign data and usually operationalizes what the CDO or CTO designs, bridging strategy to in-market execution and feeding results back, and aligning marketing's data needs with privacy policy, where only 15% of privacy decision-makers report that their privacy team collaborates with marketing. Practical advice is to tie data needs to concrete customer-journey moments, assess collection risk including ethical and operational dimensions with legal and security involved from the start, and inventory data across the enterprise, including call-center, inventory, pricing, credit, fraud and logistics data that affects customer experience though it originates outside marketing. <span class="src">The CMO’s Guide To An Enterprise Data Strategy</span>

Caterpillar shows what CEO-level ownership looks like. When CEO Jim Umpleby set out in 2017 to grow services and parts revenue through digital offerings, he found customer data too siloed and incomplete to support the strategy, which is a mix of unmanaged-mode quality problems and departmental silos. He did not delegate the fix to IT but committed three years of runway to an enterprise data platform, Helios, set a public USD 28 billion services-revenue target for 2026 to focus the work, and made data ownership a senior business responsibility, recruiting roughly a dozen vice presidents to own 14 enterprise data domains and evaluating them monthly on domain data quality, while a new data product owner role, the connector role seen at HelloFresh under another title, tracked reuse and value payoff for data products such as the customer fleet list. The platform consolidated eight legacy systems and about 200 dealer-integration interfaces, cut data-management complexity by a factor of 30 by the company's estimate, and services revenue grew from USD 14 billion in 2016 to USD 24 billion in 2024. A Dealer Digital Council gave international dealers quarterly input to the roadmap and an internal Demand Review Board ranked business-unit proposals against it, preventing the re-emergence of silos. <span class="src">Data Transformation Is the CEO’s Business</span>

Once the foundation matured, Caterpillar layered AI on it rather than launching a separate initiative, with machine-learning models correcting data-entry anomalies such as mistyped serial numbers at ingestion, vector stores making equipment manuals and service records searchable by AI and an AI Digital Product Council prioritizing use cases by the same business-value discipline as the platform, which shows that a genuine data foundation is what lets AI investments compound instead of stalling on poor inputs. <span class="src">Data Transformation Is the CEO’s Business</span>

The same prevention logic applies to the data behind AI models. Hoerl and Redman observe that data-science training concentrates on the mechanics of machine learning rather than its limitations, leaving data scientists, who like model developers tend to fall in love with their models while data gets little attention, poorly equipped to prevent or diagnose failures, and because senior managers decide whether and how widely to deploy a model, closing the gap is a management responsibility even though most managers lack technical backgrounds. Their Right Data framework has five elements, namely the problem and population of interest, defined clearly before data work begins, the right data, a concept rather than a dataset, judged against six criteria of relevancy and completeness, comprehensiveness and adequate representation, freedom from bias, timeliness, clear definition of terms and units, and appropriate exclusions for legal, regulatory, ethical or intellectual-property reasons, the training data actually used, the resulting model, and the future data the model will meet after deployment, which often differs from the training data and erodes performance, with large gaps between the right data and actual data at each stage signaling risk. <span class="src">What Managers Should Ask About AI Models and Data Sets</span>

Managers can walk developers through six questions across three phases. At problem definition they ask how and where the model will be used and how the developers will acquire data that meets the right-data criteria, during development they ask what gaps exist between the training data obtained and those criteria and how future data will be checked against them, and around deployment they ask what controls will detect data or model drift and what the top three failure modes are, with mitigation plans. The authors criticize validating a model on a holdout set carved from the same training data, standard on platforms such as Kaggle, as no substitute for evaluating real future data, since holdout data resembles training data in every way that matters, and Amazon's facial recognition system, trained on data from one geographic area but deployed far more widely, is the canonical example of the resulting poor calibration. <span class="src">What Managers Should Ask About AI Models and Data Sets</span>

Data risk completes the picture, and PwC opens with an image of viewing the organization's data like the night sky through a straw, piece by piece but never whole. The fragmentation is structural, since the chief data officer focuses on governance and quality, the chief financial officer on the reliability of data for planning, the chief risk officer on integrity for risk reporting, the chief information security officer on classification, encryption and loss prevention, and the chief compliance officer on privacy and coordinating compliance across departments, each a legitimate lens but none giving enterprise-wide visibility. The result is data at a dead end, which can quietly undermine legacy migrations, slow AI adoption, constrain portfolio expansion and degrade reporting and innovation while eroding regulator and consumer trust. In PwC's 2025 Global Digital Trust Insights survey 48% of executives said they would prioritize data protection and trust over technology modernization in the coming year, yet many still treat data risk as an IT problem. <span class="src">Data risk is everywhere 5 steps to manage it</span>

Three forces raise the cost of the gap. Regulation keeps tightening through laws such as the Consumer Data Privacy Act, the EU Data Act, California's DELETE Act and the Protecting Americans' Data Act, cloud transformation keeps accelerating, with 72% of top-performing organizations in PwC's 2024 Cloud and AI survey prioritizing data modernization, and AI compounds both, since 69% of respondents implemented cloud data modernization to power AI, so data must be both more accessible and more tightly controlled, a tension that IT-centric governance alone cannot resolve. PwC defines data risk as exposure to financial or reputational harm from loss, quality limits or related problems that affect the ability to acquire, store, transform, move, protect and use data, in five categories. Data quality risk, from entry errors, missing or misclassified records and poor integration, brings compliance problems, poor decisions and financial loss, data protection risk, from mishandled data, third-party breaches, weak encryption and unsecured APIs, brings privacy and security vulnerabilities and lost trust, and data loss risk, from cyberattacks and weak backup and recovery, brings outages and response costs. Data compliance risk, from failing to identify, classify or retain data for disclosure and audit, invites scrutiny and fines, and data exposure risk, from intellectual-property theft, insider threats and shadow IT, erodes access control and competitive advantage. <span class="src">Data risk is everywhere 5 steps to manage it</span>

PwC proposes five steps to move from fragmented, IT-owned risk to an enterprise discipline. First, know what data risk is in these terms. Second, pursue overall rather than partial visibility, a digital paper trail across the life cycle, since IT manages technical aspects, compliance manages regulatory aspects and each function keeps its own tools and controls, so logs are interpreted differently and business leaders must help resolve the redundancy and exposure. Third, rally privacy, data, security, risk and technology teams to identify, document and measure risk together, with role-specific upskilling, certifications in governance, security and privacy, and external validation. Fourth, keep asking role-specific questions, such as whether departments keep data siloed and the organization is ready for exposure as data becomes accessible for the CEO, whether financial-decision data is reliable and how its quality is measured for the CFO, whether data location and sensitivity are known across on-premises and cloud environments for the CISO, whether data can be tracked from collection to disposal including legacy data for the COO, what the major risks are and whether regulatory-change management exists for the CRO and whether policies are defined and data is complete and accurate enough for insight for the CDO. Fifth, stop treating data risk as an afterthought to innovation, since discovery, cataloging and lineage are prerequisites for both deletion obligations and newer unstructured-data challenges, which requires cross-department accountability extended to third parties and moves data risk from a back-office concern to a business priority. <span class="src">Data risk is everywhere 5 steps to manage it</span>
