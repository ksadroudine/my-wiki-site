## Summary

Two ways generative AI's productivity gains carry hidden costs to human capability. A mathematical model shows heavy reliance on unmodified AI output risks homogenizing ideas and content over time, while a controlled workplace experiment finds gen AI narrows the novice-expert gap for conceptualizing work but not for executing it — an "AI wall" set by how far a task sits from a person's own expertise. An MIT SMR synthesis of four studies adds that AI raises individual creativity while narrowing collective diversity, depending on where in the workflow it enters.

## Concept

Two pieces of research, one theoretical and one experimental, converge on a shared warning: generative AI's productivity gains are real, but they come with genuine, under-examined costs to human capability that don't show up if you only measure output speed. The first addresses creativity and originality directly. As employees increasingly lean on tools like ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot to speed up writing and coding, they face what the research frames as a genuine trade-off between originality and effort — AI-generated content convincingly mimics the fluency of human writing or code, but lacks the specific stylistic choices and original thinking a person would naturally bring to the same task unaided, and getting an AI's output to actually reflect that individual voice and expertise requires iterative, time-consuming prompt refinement that many users simply skip once the first draft looks "good enough." A team of software engineers illustrates the stakes concretely: just as prose writers aim for elegant turns of phrase, engineers strive for elegant, original solutions to coding problems reflecting their own experience with object architecture, naming conventions, and testing choices — but tools like GitHub Copilot make it easy to generate or autocomplete large blocks of code quickly, and an engineer under deadline pressure who accepts that first draft without refining it may save time now while quietly increasing future costs, since research has found people often struggle to review and adapt AI-generated code well enough to fix it later, sometimes making a full rewrite more efficient than incremental correction — with a separate 2023 study finding LLM-based coding tools measurably diminished code quality and long-term maintainability. A mathematical model of human-AI interaction, developed specifically to capture the dynamics of this trade-off, formalizes the underlying risk: as people balance getting genuinely optimal, personalized output against working efficiently, diversity of thought and creativity both tend to erode, because defaulting to a tool's unmodified output produces more homogeneous content than individual humans working independently would produce — if everyone's emails were written by the same AI assistant, they'd predictably start to sound alike, at real cost to the originality and diversity of ideas that drive organizational growth and innovation. This homogenization risk compounds through a specific feedback mechanism: as AI-generated content increasingly enters the training data used for future AI models — both proprietary organizational content and the broader public internet — some researchers warn of a "homogenization death spiral," where AI-generated content increasingly displaces human-generated content in training data, potentially degrading the resulting models' quality or, in more extreme framings, contributing to model collapse over time. Because gen AI can competently produce so much routine content so quickly, the cost of losing some diversity of thought can feel trivial against the scale of efficiency gains on offer — but the research argues this understates the risk specifically because generative AI, unlike prior labor-saving shifts like automation or offshoring, operates directly in the realm of ideas, content, and innovation rather than merely routine physical or administrative tasks, meaning the habit of defaulting to AI output carries implications for organizational creativity that earlier productivity technologies simply didn't. The proposed mitigations center on management practice rather than technology alone: rethinking productivity expectations task by task, giving employees more time specifically where a task genuinely calls for original human thought or judgment rather than applying a uniform "faster is better" standard everywhere; making it genuinely easier for users to guide, amend, and correct AI output — retrieval-augmented generation to improve factual accuracy, and comprehensive prompt-engineering training so employees can actually shape outputs toward their own ideas rather than accepting whatever a model defaults to; and having managers actively encourage employees to authentically express their distinct perspectives and contribute their own creativity, positioning AI as an assistant that enriches work rather than a substitute that quietly erodes the individuality and diversity of thought an organization actually depends on. <span class="src">Does GenAI Impose a Creativity Tax</span>

The second piece of research tests a related but distinct question empirically: not whether AI erodes originality, but whether it can actually close the performance gap between novices and experts — the premise many organizations are implicitly betting on when they deploy gen AI to help less-experienced employees take on more advanced work. Researchers from Stanford and Harvard Business School's Digital Data Design Institute ran a controlled experiment with 78 employees at IG Group, a UK-based fintech firm, dividing them into three groups by their "expertise distance" from a writing task: experts (professional writers who regularly drafted articles for the company's website), adjacent outsiders (marketing specialists with no direct article-writing experience but general familiarity with what the writers did), and distant outsiders (developers and data scientists with no marketing or writing background at all). Each group completed two distinct stages — conceptualizing an article and then actually writing it — both with and without gen AI assistance, with IG executives blind-scoring the results on a 1-to-5 scale. Without AI, the results showed the expected skill hierarchy at conceptualization: writers scored highest (3.82 on average), followed by marketing specialists (3.04) and technologists (3.02). With gen AI assistance, however, that gap nearly disappeared: writers' concept scores rose to 4.12, marketing specialists' to 4.18 — slightly outperforming AI-assisted writers — and technologists' to 4.05, with all three AI-assisted groups outperforming unassisted writers entirely. This result on its own would seem to validate the promise of AI-driven skill-hierarchy flattening, since conceptualization requires relatively less specialized expertise — mainly the judgment to recognize whether a proposed topic idea is good enough, something one participant likened to imagining running a marathon rather than actually running it. But the writing stage told a categorically different story. Without AI, writers again performed best. With AI, writers still led (3.96 on average) and marketing specialists came close behind (3.92) — but technologists' scores with and without AI assistance were essentially unchanged (3.42 and 3.38 respectively), meaning gen AI provided them functionally no measurable benefit for the execution phase of the task, in sharp contrast to the conceptualization phase where it had helped them the most. Interviews with participants revealed why: the further a worker's own expertise sat from the knowledge genuinely required for a task, the less gen AI assistance actually helped them close the gap with true experts — marketing specialists understood the writers' general professional language and had just enough domain knowledge to meaningfully refine AI-generated content, while technologists, whose work had nothing to do with writing, lacked the intuition to judge what language to keep, cut, or rework, and were observed in the study simply copying and pasting AI's suggestions directly into articles rather than editing them. The researchers named this limit "the AI wall": the point at which gen AI cannot substitute for genuine domain expertise, because it depends fundamentally on the user's own capacity to assess, refine, and integrate its suggestions rather than functioning as an automatic skill-equalizer regardless of user background — directly challenging the common assumption that gen AI enables "universal task fluidity" for any worker on any task. The researchers argue this AI-wall dynamic generalizes well beyond the specific writers-versus-technologists case studied, offering two practical recommendations for organizations deploying gen AI across employees with varying expertise. First, employees applying AI to a given domain need at least enough general understanding and hands-on experience with that area to meaningfully assess and improve AI-generated output — not necessarily deep expertise, but enough working knowledge to exercise genuine judgment over what a model's suggestions get right and wrong, since (in study lead Luca Vendraminelli's words) "AI isn't a magic fix for everything at work if it is not able to fully automate tasks" and "expertise is irreplicable. No technology can substitute for it." Second, organizations need to expect real structural and cultural change once employees start using gen AI effectively — altering processes, decision-making approaches, and how teams collaborate, since gen AI can meaningfully blur job titles and role boundaries within closely related fields (an SEO specialist and a content strategist, for instance) but struggles far more to bridge genuinely large divides between functions tied to fundamentally different expertise, budgets, and organizational power structures, such as marketing, sales, and product — a gap that broader, more flexible job design can help address, but only through deliberate structural and cultural investment, not through deploying the technology alone. <span class="src">Gen AI Won’t Make Your Employees Experts</span>

<span class="src">The Hidden Cost of AI-Assisted Creativity</span> (MIT SMR, July 2026) provides empirical support for this concept's homogenization argument. It defines group-level creativity through diversity, measured as the semantic similarity of outputs, and shows AI shifts distributions toward higher similarity across all four studies. In the circular-economy study a single human iterating with AI beat a crowd on quality and viability, but the crowd generated more novel ideas, and prompting the model to tackle a different problem each time restored novelty comparable to the crowd's. The authors also cite evidence that students with unrestricted AI access did worse once it was removed while guardrails eliminated the penalty, and that consultants who accepted AI recommendations uncritically underperformed. Ubisoft's Ghostwriter, where writers define character and context before selecting from AI-generated lines, is offered as a sequencing example. <span class="src">The Hidden Cost of AI-Assisted Creativity</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses whether the homogenization and AI-wall effects interact — whether, for instance, an organization that successfully mitigates homogenization risk through active editing and prompt refinement (as the creativity-tax research recommends) would also see its non-expert employees perform better at execution tasks, closing the AI wall gap as a side effect.
- One of the four studies is an unpublished 2026 working paper, and the authors of the synthesis conducted all four studies themselves.
