## Summary

Building an AI-first leadership team means redefining the mandate of each senior role, including people, marketing, transformation, finance, operations and technology, rather than adding AI tools to old job descriptions. Only about 5 percent of companies capture significant bottom-line AI value, and BCG attributes success roughly 70 percent to people and process, 20 percent to technology and 10 percent to algorithms.

## Highlights

- BCG's C-suite survey of 1,250+ companies found nearly every company is experimenting with AI but 60% have generated no value from it; of the 40% seeing returns, only the top 5% — "future-built" companies — unlock significant bottom-line value, with 1.7x greater revenue growth, 3.6x greater total shareholder return, and 1.6x greater EBIT margin than laggards, and these top performers are 50% more likely to have shared business-IT ownership of AI operating models.
- The AI-first Chief People Officer becomes a "chief capabilities architect" who decides what work is best done by people versus AI and redesigns workflows accordingly — CEOs should ask whether their CPO is AI-curious (actually using AI, not just discussing it), has led any large-scale transformation before, can bring structure to ambiguity, can draft a new "social contract" with anxious employees, and can claim a genuine seat at the table.
- The AI-first Chief Marketing Officer becomes a "chief growth architect" who owns the data inputs, growth platforms, and measurement models of a unified martech stack, working with the CTO to replace fragmented, siloed optimization logic with enterprise-wide logic that feeds real-time demand signals into product, supply chain, and finance decisions.
- The AI-first Chief Transformation Officer's role expands from driving execution discipline to distinguishing genuine AI-driven results from mere "AI activity" — appointing a CTrO at the start of a growth transformation raises the odds of success by 22 percentage points, but the role demands low ego, high accountability, and a willingness to work toward one's own obsolescence by embedding capability into the business rather than accumulating authority.
- The AI-first Chief Financial Officer flips the finance function's time allocation from roughly 90% producing numbers and 10% advising the business to the reverse, building a "no surprises" real-time heads-up display of leading indicators; CEOs should set three time-bound goals — a 5-year AI value forecast within 6 months, a single-portfolio view of all AI spend within 6 months, and a live real-time finance command center within 12 months.
- Two BCG deep-dive companion pieces extend the CFO thesis: finance AI fails not from weak models but from ungoverned data and inconsistent semantic definitions (the same "margin" meaning different things in Europe versus Asia-Pacific breaks an AI agent's reasoning), and governance must be designed as architecture — data lineage, escalation rules, inspectable agent-generated models — rather than bolted on as compliance; separately, a fully agent-powered finance function could cut staffing needs for today's workflows in half within 2-5 years, moving general accounting to real time, reporting to 100% automation, and planning to continuous, agent-driven reforecasting.
- The AI-first Chief Operating Officer keeps the same core mandate (cost, delivery, quality, innovation, time-to-market) but must reinvent processes from first principles rather than automate existing ones, shift from reactive to predictive operations, and build in-house technical capability instead of relying on vendors — BCG estimates AI could increase industrial-operations productivity by more than 30% within 2-3 years and potentially triple it within 5, with costs down 60% or more.
- The CIO's traditional monopoly over technology delivery is eroding as AI makes build capacity abundant (business teams can create their own agents), but the role becomes more necessary, not less — someone must still decide what should be built, govern the digital-labor lifecycle, curate machine-readable enterprise context, and link AI spend to value; BCG frames this shift as moving from "service provider" to "orchestrator of enterprise intelligence."

## Concept

BCG's multi-part series on building an AI-first senior leadership team opens from a shared diagnosis, repeated across every installment: a C-suite survey of more than 1,250 companies found that while nearly every company is experimenting with AI, 60% have yet to generate any value from their investments, and of the 40% that are seeing returns, only a small top tier — the top 5%, which BCG calls "future-built" companies — are unlocking significant bottom-line value, with 1.7 times greater revenue growth, 3.6 times greater total shareholder return, and 1.6 times greater EBIT margin than lagging peers. These future-built companies share a structural trait: they are 50% more likely to have shared business-IT ownership of their AI operating models, with clear decision rights and accountability split between technical and business leaders rather than concentrated in either alone. This finding anchors BCG's central argument across the entire series: becoming AI-first is not primarily a technology problem to be solved by procuring better tools, but a leadership problem requiring each C-suite function to be fundamentally re-mandated, not merely AI-augmented. <span class="src">Strategy and Soft Skills What CEOs Should Look For in an AI-First Chief People Officer</span>

Article one of the series argues CEOs most commonly underestimate their **Chief People Officer**'s role, casting the CPO as a supporting actor who implements AI strategy rather than the lead actor who should shape it from the outset. The AI-first CPO becomes a "chief capabilities architect," deciding what work is best done by people versus AI and then building the processes and reskilling programs that integrate AI tools without breaking worker trust — a genuinely large-scale undertaking, since large enterprises are looking at upskilling entire divisions of tens of thousands of employees at once, and BCG's data shows future-built companies are four times more likely than laggards to have structured AI learning programs with protected employee learning time. Five questions help a CEO evaluate a CPO's readiness: is the CPO AI-curious, actually running pilots and defining productivity-based KPIs rather than performative engagement metrics; has the CPO led any prior large-scale transformation, technological or otherwise; can the CPO bring structure to ambiguity by prioritizing which questions can be answered now versus monitored for later; can the CPO draft and manage a new "social contract" with employees that explains transparently why and how AI will be used; and can the CPO claim a genuine seat at the transformation table and challenge the CEO's own assumptions. <span class="src">Strategy and Soft Skills What CEOs Should Look For in an AI-First Chief People Officer</span>

Article two turns to the **Chief Marketing Officer**, arguing marketing is usually a CEO's first port of call for AI experimentation because it is data-rich, customer-facing, and heavily digital — yet few CEOs grasp how AI positions the CMO to drive enterprise-wide growth rather than just marketing efficiency. BCG frames the AI-first CMO as a "chief growth architect" who, working alongside the CTO, owns the data inputs, commercial growth platforms, and measurement models of a unified martech stack, tearing down the historical silos between paid, owned, earned, and commerce media that were never designed to work together. As those systems align, marketing becomes a source of real-time customer-behavior signals that inform branding, pricing, product innovation, and resource-allocation decisions well beyond marketing's traditional remit — a shift BCG predicts will inevitably "ruffle some C-suite egos" as the CMO's role expands into adjacent functions' territory, requiring explicit CEO backing. Five diagnostic questions parallel the CPO's: can the CMO partner with IT to build the AI/data backbone; is the CMO as passionate about developing people as building platforms; can the CMO go deep on execution details rather than speak only in abstractions; is the CEO prepared to back the CMO through inevitable turf friction; and can the CMO scale AI-driven growth while still protecting genuinely human-driven creativity, since AI can personalize and optimize but cannot itself feel or originate emotion. <span class="src">Art and Algorithms What CEOs Should Look For in an AI-First Chief Marketing Officer</span>

Article three addresses the **Chief Transformation Officer**, a role BCG's research shows raises a growth transformation's odds of success by 22 percentage points when appointed at the outset, against a baseline where general business transformations succeed only 30% of the time and just 5% of companies report significant bottom-line AI value. The AI-first CTrO's core job shifts from generically driving execution discipline to four more specific capabilities: ensuring AI effort concentrates on a few high-value, trackable areas rather than diffusing broadly; distinguishing "AI activity" (tool adoption, pilot counts) from real financial and operational impact; having enough technical fluency to broker effectively between technology leaders who own platforms and business leaders who own the processes being reshaped, without needing deep AI expertise; and "owning nothing but being accountable for everything" — working through influence rather than direct authority. Four questions test this: can the CTrO wield sustained influence without formal control over budgets or teams; can they relentlessly focus on outcomes while staying out of the spotlight, attributing success to business leaders; can they move fluidly between the enterprise view and two levels down into daily operations to catch problems before they escalate; and — the role's defining trait — are they genuinely willing to work toward their own obsolescence, embedding capability into the organization rather than accumulating personal scope. <span class="src">Soft Power, Hard Results What CEOs Should Look For in an AI-First Chief Transformation Officer</span>

Article four, and two substantial BCG companion deep-dives, address the **Chief Financial Officer** — the series' most extensively covered role. The core CEO mandate reframes the CFO from protector of controls and accurate reporting into an architect of AI value who determines where AI investment is and isn't generating returns; executing this requires flipping the finance function's time allocation from roughly 90% producing numbers and 10% advising the business toward the reverse, building an AI-enabled "heads-up display" of live operating inputs (sales activity, pricing, conversion, cost trends) linked directly to revenue, margin, and earnings — what BCG calls a "no surprises" finance function that spots emerging misses early enough to still change course. BCG sets three time-bound CEO goals for CFOs: within six months, a five-year forecast of where AI can move revenue, margin, productivity, and resilience (not a list of pilots); also within six months, bringing all AI spending into a single portfolio view with justified ROI expectations; and within twelve months, a live, real-time finance command center replacing after-the-quarter reporting. A joint BCG-and-SAP deep dive on "The CFO's AI Agenda" extends this by diagnosing why most finance AI pilots stall: not model capability but ungoverned, semantically inconsistent data — an AI agent asked to explain a margin decline across regions produces an unreliable answer if "margin" is defined differently in Europe versus Asia-Pacific, since a human analyst can silently adjust for such inconsistencies but an agent cannot. The fix runs in two layers: harmonizing underlying data (a unified chart of accounts, consistent cost/profit-center hierarchies) and building an explicit semantic layer that encodes driver relationships (revenue to pricing/volume/channel, costs to production/headcount/supplier terms) so an agent can trace a variance from the general ledger into its actual business drivers; this work should proceed incrementally, domain by domain, rather than as a single monolithic data migration, since organizations seeing early AI returns in finance are consistently those that didn't wait for perfect data. The same piece argues finance AI governance must be designed as architecture from day one — data lineage tracing outputs back to source transactions, explicit escalation rules for when agents act versus defer to a human, and agent-generated models expressed as inspectable, readable structure rather than opaque code — rather than treated as a compliance exercise retrofitted after the first audit; the operating posture starts as "AI proposes, human disposes," with autonomy expanding only as reliability is demonstrated. A second BCG deep dive, "The AI-First Finance Function," projects that within two to five years leading organizations will run agent-powered finance functions capable of real-time close, fully automated reporting, and dynamic scenario modeling in seconds, potentially cutting the staff needed to handle today's workflows in half — with general accounting moving to continuous reconciliation, board decks assembling automatically with interactive drill-down, planning becoming continuously reforecast rather than periodic, and transactional operations (procure-to-pay, order-to-cash, payroll) running fully autonomously with humans handling only flagged exceptions; in this future, the CFO's core tasks shift to designing the underlying data fabric, building the agent ecosystem, setting the intervention thresholds that trigger human referral, and defining explicit AI "no-go zones," becoming, in BCG's phrase, "custodian of the enterprise's central nervous system." <span class="src">From Hindsight to Foresight The CEO Mandate for an AI-First Chief Financial Officer</span>

Article five addresses the **Chief Operating Officer**, arguing the COO's core mandate — cost, delivery, quality, innovation, time-to-market — does not change, but how it is delivered changes considerably, with BCG research estimating AI could increase industrial-operations productivity by more than 30% within the first two to three years and potentially triple it within five years while cutting costs 60% or more, contingent on whether the COO develops a genuinely AI-first operating model rather than simply automating the existing one. Enduring COO strengths (pattern recognition, operational discipline, resilience, people leadership) remain valuable and are amplified by AI's ability to stress-test scenarios and surface early warning signals, but five new capabilities are required: thinking and deciding differently by challenging long-held assumptions about what's possible, since the constraint shifts from what technology can do to whether the COO knows what they want it to do; reinventing operations from first principles rather than incrementally improving them, starting from the desired outcome and working backward through procurement, planning, and logistics; moving from reactive to predictive operations using AI's early-warning capabilities while preserving human judgment for what AI cannot simulate; becoming AI-fluent enough to specify what operational systems should do rather than ceding that to vendors, while never ceding human judgment itself to the machine; and building deeper technical capability across the operations team rather than centralizing it, embedding AI expertise into everyday work. Three questions test COO readiness: is the COO reinventing operations or simply running them with AI layered on top; is the COO building organizational capability or just deploying tools; and is the COO rethinking familiar operational problems (what systems are actually needed, what automation level is realistic) based on what AI newly makes possible, rather than fitting AI into old problem framings. <span class="src">Reinvention and Rigor What CEOs Should Look for in an AI-First Chief Operating Officer</span>

A related BCG piece — not formally numbered within the CPO-through-COO series but published on the same "AI-first enterprise" theme — argues the **CIO**'s traditional monopoly over technology delivery is eroding just as the role becomes structurally more necessary. AI is making "build capacity" abundant: autonomous agents increasingly plan, build, test, deploy, and operate software directly, business teams can create their own agents and applications, and AI consumption shifts part of IT economics from fixed investment to usage-based operating expense — developments that could make the CIO role, which first took shape in the early 1980s, look outdated. But those same forces make a CIO-equivalent function more essential, not less: when everyone can build, someone must decide what should be built; when agents act across workflows, someone must manage the digital-labor lifecycle; when enterprise knowledge becomes machine-readable context, someone must ensure data, rules, and system dependencies are accurate and governed; and when AI spend scales with every prompt, token, and agent, someone must link that cost to value. BCG frames this as a shift from "service provider" to "orchestrator of enterprise intelligence," with the mandate — regardless of whether it sits with a CIO, CIDO, CDO, or CTO — spanning C-suite alignment on AI as a business agenda, redesigning end-to-end workflows rather than automating isolated tasks, building scalable AI platforms and data governance, managing the digital-labor ecosystem (visibility over agents, models, permissions, and lifecycle decisions), embedding cybersecurity and compliance directly into production controls, rewiring IT talent toward new roles like context engineers and harness engineers, and continuously renewing the technology estate rather than treating legacy modernization as an occasional remediation program. <span class="src">Why We Still Need a CIO in the AI-First Era</span>

Across all six roles, BCG returns repeatedly to the same underlying ratio, sometimes stated as "10-20-70" and elsewhere as "70/20/10": only about 10% of AI transformation success traces to the algorithms themselves, roughly 20% to technology and data, and the remaining 70% to people, process, and the hard organizational work of changing how decisions actually get made — meaning every role-specific mandate in the series, however different its technical surface, converges on the same underlying claim that AI-first leadership is fundamentally a people and organizational-design challenge rather than a procurement decision. <span class="src">The AI-First Finance Function</span>

## Open Questions

- BCG's series treats each C-suite role independently, but does not address how the newly overlapping mandates it describes — the CMO's data/martech ownership, the CIO's context governance, the CFO's data-fabric design, the COO's technology-stack ownership — should be reconciled or arbitrated when two AI-first executives claim the same territory, beyond generically noting that friction "should be greeted as a sign of progress."
- The finance-specific pieces project agent-powered finance functions cutting required staff in half within two to five years, but none of the three CFO-focused articles address the workforce-transition, retention, or morale implications of that reduction, despite the CPO article's emphasis on trust and a "new social contract" with anxious employees elsewhere in the same series.
- The CIO piece does not resolve whether the "CIO, CIDO, CDO, or CTO" mandate it describes should ultimately consolidate into one title or remain distributed — a question the vault's data-leadership-roles-evolution concept addresses from the opposite direction (arguing the CDO title specifically is proving unstable).
