## Summary

Telecom operators are restructuring both their IT architecture and their organizational operating models to escape legacy complexity and deliver value faster. A business-architecture framework, an empirical IT-excellence benchmark, a concrete CIO account, and six operators' agile transformations converge on one conclusion — holistic, cross-dimension change outperforms isolated cost-cutting or single-capability fixes.

## Highlights

- Telecom operators face a "complexity cost trap" from managing sprawling legacy technology stacks, rising labor costs, and unpredictable external spend; a dedicated business-architecture discipline — mapping value-creation opportunities, business capabilities, and underlying technology into a shared blueprint — is proposed as the missing link between strategic plans and financial budgets.
- McKinsey's IT Excellence Diagnostic, benchmarking more than 20 telecom operators across six capability dimensions (business functionality, operating model, engineering excellence, IT architecture, cloud, and data/AI), found top-quartile operators achieve nearly 30% lower IT cost-efficiency ratios than peers — but only by improving consistently across all dimensions rather than excelling in just one.
- Most telecom operators' cloud migrations follow a simplistic "lift and shift" approach that fails to capture cloud's cost benefits; operators with the highest cloud-adoption rates in the benchmark study actually had the worst IT cost-efficiency ratios, while genuine leaders build cloud-native systems or substantially re-architect existing applications.
- More than 60% of telecom operators have adopted agile methodologies, but most fail to realize the full benefit — leading operators industrialize a proof of concept into production in three to six months versus 1.5 years for peers, a gap attributed largely to superior IT architecture and engineering-excellence practices such as automated deployment pipelines and integrated product catalogs.
- Six telcos' (Cogeco, Entel, Magyar Telekom, Masmovil, Safaricom, Tesco Mobile) agile transformations reveal a consistent pattern: durable change requires simultaneously reworking four elements — structure (cross-functional "squads" and "tribes"), workflows (unified quarterly business reviews linking strategy to team-level OKRs), talent (shifting from role-based to skill-based models), and culture (leaders shifting from directive control to coaching) — rather than adopting agile practices in isolation.
- Deutsche Telekom's CIO Peter Leukert grounds these abstract frameworks in a concrete case: the company built a custom operational support system for its German fiber rollout from scratch in nine months, delivered a 50% productivity increase in customer service through better digital tooling, and expects GenAI to deliver a further 50% productivity gain, attributing this to disciplined integration, architecture, and data governance rather than to the AI models themselves.
- Leukert's account illustrates the abstract business-architecture and IT-excellence frameworks concretely: Deutsche Telekom uses TM Forum's Open Digital Architecture to build shared software once and deploy it across all eleven of its European operating countries, demonstrating the "modularity, re-use, and scalability" business architecture is meant to enable at the strategic level.
- A recurring theme across all four sources is that AI adoption amplifies, rather than substitutes for, IT fundamentals — clean data, disciplined integration, sound governance, and architectural simplification determine whether an operator can scale AI use cases into measurable EBITDA impact, not the choice of AI model itself.

## Concept

<span class="src">Calling All Telecom Business Architects</span> (Strategy&, November 2023) introduces the foundational discipline the later sources build on. Facing simultaneous pressure from cost transformation, evolving customer expectations, multi-technology re-platforming, and next-generation infrastructure investment, telecom operators fall into what the article calls a "complexity cost trap" — the accumulated burden of legacy technology maintenance, rising labor costs, and volatile external spend. Business architecture is proposed as the remedy: a blueprint spanning three layers (value-creation opportunities, business capabilities, and underlying technology) that gives leadership a shared taxonomy for aligning investment decisions with strategic direction. The article recommends telecom leaders build a capability map, embed business architecture into strategic and financial planning as a distinct discipline, and establish a "legacy relief factory" to systematically retire outdated technology in agile increments.

<span class="src">Deutsche Telekom’s CIO on how new approaches to IT can drive growth</span> (TM Forum, December 2024) grounds this abstract framework in a single operator's lived experience. CIO Peter Leukert describes how Deutsche Telekom's IT organization supports the company's aggressive fiber rollout (targeting roughly 17.5 million homes passed in Germany by 2027) and improving customer experience against an "OTT player" benchmark, emphasizing time to market as his team's key value metric. Concretely, the IT team built a custom operational support system for fiber services from scratch in nine months rather than buying an off-the-shelf product, later expanding its scope and licensing it to external partners. Leukert reports a 50 percent productivity increase in customer service from better digital tooling, with GenAI expected to deliver a further 50 percent gain — but insists that "the key is not building or training the models. The key is integration," pointing to the same architectural and data-governance disciplines the business-architecture framework describes in the abstract. Deutsche Telekom's use of TM Forum's Open Digital Architecture to build shared software once and deploy it across all eleven of its European operating countries is a direct, working illustration of business architecture's promised modularity and reuse.

<span class="src">Transforming telecom tech How IT excellence drives innovation and cost efficiency</span> (McKinsey, February 2025) supplies the empirical backbone connecting the framework to measurable outcomes. Benchmarking more than 20 telecom operators worldwide across six capability dimensions — business functionality, operating model, engineering excellence, IT architecture, cloud, and data/AI — McKinsey's IT Excellence Diagnostic finds that operators in the top quartile of tech capability spend nearly 30 percent less on IT relative to revenue, but only when they improve consistently across all dimensions rather than in isolation; one operator that transformed IT programmatically across all capabilities cut IT spending by as much as 40 percent. The diagnostic surfaces the same lift-and-shift cloud problem in the data: operators with the highest cloud-adoption rates actually post the worst cost-efficiency ratios, because moving workloads to the cloud without re-architecting them limits the value captured, echoing the "how" behind Deutsche Telekom's decision to build cloud-native rather than simply migrate. The study also finds that although more than 60 percent of operators have adopted agile methodologies, most fail to realize the full benefit — leading operators industrialize proofs of concept into production in three to six months versus 1.5 years for peers, a gap the diagnostic attributes to superior IT architecture and engineering-excellence practices specifically.

<span class="src">Inside agile telcos Organizing to value and what it takes to succeed</span> (McKinsey, September 2025) picks up precisely the "operating model" dimension the IT-excellence diagnostic identifies as one of six success factors and examines it in depth through interviews with executives at six telcos — Cogeco, Entel, Magyar Telekom, Masmovil, Safaricom, and Tesco Mobile — two to five years into their agile journeys. All six pursued deep, parallel transformation across four elements: structure (reorganizing around value-creating cross-functional "squads" and "tribes" rather than functional silos), workflows (a unified quarterly business review cycle linking company strategy to team-level OKRs), talent (shifting from role-based to skill-based career models), and culture (leaders moving from directive control to coaching). The article's central finding echoes the IT-excellence diagnostic directly: companies that tried to limit change to one element, such as adopting agile workflows without altering structure, needed to eventually touch all four to see real impact. Notably, several interviewees connect their agile operating model directly to their ability to deploy AI effectively — Masmovil's cross-functional teams found and scaled AI use cases area by area, and the article argues that only end-to-end-owned, cross-functional units (rather than siloed, function-specific pilots) can deploy AI and AI agents to the processes with the greatest potential.

Read together, these four sources trace one coherent argument from framework to evidence to lived practice: a telecom operator's business-architecture blueprint (the "why" and "what") requires an aligned operating model overhaul (the "how," detailed through six operators' agile journeys) to actually deliver the cost and speed benefits McKinsey's cross-operator benchmark measures — and Deutsche Telekom's CIO account shows what disciplined execution of both looks like inside a single company, right down to why GenAI's promised productivity gains depend on the same architectural fundamentals rather than on the AI technology itself.

## Open Questions

- None of the four sources reconcile how a telco should sequence business-architecture design (Strategy&'s framework) against an agile operating-model transformation (the six-telco interviews) when starting from scratch — whether one should precede the other, or whether they are meant to proceed in parallel, is not addressed.
- The IT Excellence Diagnostic does not disclose which specific operators fall into its top and bottom quartiles, so it is not possible to cross-reference its findings against Deutsche Telekom's or the six agile-transformation telcos' individual performance.
