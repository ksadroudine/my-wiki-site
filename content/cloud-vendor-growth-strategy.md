## Summary

How technology vendors and incumbents, not enterprise buyers, should respond to the cloud market's growth. Bain's "five faces of the cloud" segments customers by adoption behavior to guide vendor targeting, while a companion piece sizes the market shift and identifies three moves — harvesting legacy franchises, inorganic investment, and sales-channel evolution — that let non-cloud-native incumbents capture cloud growth rather than lose to it.

## Highlights

- Cloud's growth was projected to represent 60% of all growth in the global IT industry through 2021, with public cloud infrastructure and enabling services, IaaS/PaaS, and SaaS all growing in the double digits annually — a tailwind concentrated heavily in the leading cloud providers (Azure grew 76% in a single 2018 quarter; AWS delivered more than half of Amazon's total operating income that same period).
- From 2013 to 2017, all but three categories of legacy (non-cloud-native) technology underperformed growth expectations — high-end enterprise servers saw an actual 11% decline against a predicted 3% decline — showing cloud's growth was substantially cannibalizing rather than merely supplementing incumbent technology categories.
- The one bright spot among legacy categories, midrange and volume servers, offers a transferable playbook for non-cloud-native incumbents: harvest legacy product-line cash flow to fund new hybrid-IT and multicloud offerings, pursue acquisitions to acquire fast-growing capability in cloud markets where the incumbent is underexposed, and evolve the sales and channel model to actually match how customers now buy cloud.
- Enterprise cloud adoption reached the levels predicted years earlier (33% of workload in the cloud by 2017, matching a 2013 estimate) and customers subsequently raised their own expectations further (50% of workload in the cloud by 2022) — but adoption rates vary enormously by customer type, meaning a one-size-fits-all vendor go-to-market approach leaves real segments underserved.
- Bain's "five faces of the cloud" customer-segmentation framework, first identified in 2011 and revisited in 2019: Transformational (early adopters prioritizing performance and scalability over cost, some already hitting an on-premise-favoring economic ceiling for steady, high-utilization workloads); Heterogeneous (mature businesses deliberately segmenting workloads across SaaS, IaaS/PaaS, and on-premise based on fit rather than pursuing full migration); Safety Conscious (demand exists but private-cloud offerings have been too costly or complex to meet these customers' dedicated-environment requirements); Price Conscious (the segment most sophisticated at capturing real savings, since companies that rightsize workloads during migration — rather than simply "lifting and shifting" — can cut costs 30-60%); and Slow and Steady (the largest segment by spending by 2022 and the primary source of future cloud-market growth, having waited out the early-adopter phase before committing).
- The two central vendor lessons converge: providers chasing growth need to explicitly choose and design for a target customer segment rather than pursuing the whole market uniformly, and non-cloud-native incumbents specifically need an active, multi-pronged strategy (legacy-funded reinvestment, acquisition, channel evolution) rather than assuming steady legacy demand will persist through the shift.

## Concept

<span class="src">How Technology Incumbents Can Realize Their Cloud Growth Ambitions</span> (Bain & Company, January 2019) opens a series on the enterprise cloud market by sizing just how large and fast the shift toward cloud was, and what it meant for technology vendors that weren't leading cloud providers. Cloud's growth was projected to represent 60 percent of all global IT industry growth through 2021, split across public cloud infrastructure and enabling services (9% annual growth, $127 billion by 2021), IaaS/PaaS (29% annual growth, $117 billion by 2021), and SaaS (16% annual growth, $159 billion by 2021) — a tailwind captured overwhelmingly by the leading cloud providers themselves, with AWS growing more than 40% per quarter and delivering over half of Amazon's total operating income by Q3 2018, and Azure growing 76% in the same quarter. The flip side of this concentration was a difficult period for technology industry incumbents outside the leading cloud vendors: from 2013 to 2017, every legacy technology category except three underperformed growth expectations, with high-end enterprise servers experiencing an actual 11 percent decline against a forecast 3 percent decline. The lone genuine bright spot — midrange and volume servers, buoyed partly by white-box manufacturers and partly by a resurgence in branded OEM gear from Dell, HP, and Lenovo — offered a transferable playbook for other struggling incumbents: harvest cash flow from legacy product franchises specifically to fund new hybrid-IT and multicloud offerings rather than simply defending the legacy business; pursue inorganic investment (acquisitions) to obtain fast-growing capability in cloud market segments where the incumbent lacks organic exposure; and evolve the sales and channel model to actually match how customers now buy and consume cloud services, rather than continuing to sell cloud-adjacent products through a sales motion built for on-premise deals. The article's closing warning is directed at incumbents who might read short-term stability as safety: the cloud shift was structural, not cyclical, and incumbents who assumed "the storm has passed" based on recent performance were setting themselves up for a later reckoning. <span class="src">How Technology Incumbents Can Realize Their Cloud Growth Ambitions</span>

<span class="src">Revisiting the Five Faces of the Cloud</span> (Bain & Company, March 2019), the explicit second installment in the same series, narrows from market-level sizing to customer-level segmentation, addressing the tactical question the first article leaves open: given that incumbents need to pursue cloud growth deliberately rather than passively, which customers should they actually target, and how? Bain's answer is a five-segment taxonomy first developed in 2011 and revisited here against updated 2019 data. Transformational customers, the early-adopter segment that set the market's pace, prioritize performance and scalability over cost savings and have driven much of cloud's visible momentum — but some are already discovering a natural ceiling, since a workload that is highly predictable and runs consistently at 80 percent utilization increasingly favors on-premise economics over cloud's pay-as-you-go model. Heterogeneous customers, typically mature businesses with forward-looking but risk-aware IT leadership, deliberately segment their existing workloads across SaaS, IaaS, PaaS, and retained on-premise infrastructure based on genuine fit rather than pursuing wholesale migration. Safety Conscious customers have real, demonstrated demand for cloud but have been held back specifically by private-cloud offerings that proved too costly or too complex to meet their requirement for a dedicated, non-shared cloud environment — representing a segment vendors could unlock significantly by improving private-cloud/public-cloud interoperability. Price Conscious customers have been the segment most sophisticated at actually capturing cloud's promised savings, specifically because they treat migration as an opportunity to audit and rightsize computing and storage practices across the company (uncovering substantial fragmentation and duplication in the process) rather than simply "lifting and shifting" existing workloads as-is — Bain's own client work found rightsizing during migration can cut costs 30 to 60 percent, a gain "lift and shift" migrations reliably fail to capture. Slow and Steady customers, who waited out the early-adopter phase before committing over the following three to five years, were projected to become the largest segment in total cloud spending by 2022, accounting for half of all spending growth — making them, in Bain's assessment, the single most important segment for a cloud vendor seeking growth to focus on. The article's closing observation ties back to the growth-sizing piece: because different segments are moving at very different paces and for very different reasons (some already near a ceiling, others just beginning to ramp), "a rising tide lifts all boats, but in this case it won't lift them all evenly" — reinforcing that vendor success depends on deliberately choosing which face of the cloud to serve, not on riding overall market growth passively. <span class="src">Revisiting the Five Faces of the Cloud</span>

Read together, the two articles supply the vendor-side counterpart to a question this vault's other cloud concepts address almost entirely from the enterprise-buyer's perspective: not how a company should adopt cloud, but how a technology provider or incumbent should compete for and win cloud-driven growth. The market-sizing piece establishes why the stakes are structural rather than cyclical for non-cloud-native incumbents, and the segmentation piece supplies the customer-targeting discipline needed to actually execute the incumbent playbook the first piece proposes.

## Open Questions

- Both sources are from 2019, predating the accelerated cloud adoption driven by the 2020-2021 pandemic-era digital transformation wave; neither source is updated here against how the five-segment taxonomy or the growth-sizing figures held up through that acceleration.
- Neither source addresses how a single technology vendor should allocate go-to-market investment across multiple customer segments simultaneously, versus committing primarily to one — the "focus on one segment" recommendation is made in the abstract without discussing the tradeoffs of a multi-segment portfolio approach.
