## Summary

Research showing that observable language, not private intentions, determines whether a workplace disagreement stays constructive. Covers the intention-behavior and behavior-perception gaps that undermine good intentions, five specific linguistic techniques, and how organizations can train, measure, and hire for this skill.

## Highlights

- A 1,113-person study found that people instructed on the specific words and phrases to use when expressing receptiveness to an opposing view were rated by their disagreement counterparts as more objective, intelligent, and trustworthy — and counterparts were more willing to work with them in future — than people who received either no guidance or the classic "consider the other side's perspective and be empathetic" advice, since only concrete language actually produced observable, judgeable behavior. <span class="src">A Smarter Way to Disagree</span>
- The intention-behavior gap explains why good intentions alone fail: even people who genuinely intend to be curious and respectful often can't sustain it under the emotional pressure of active disagreement — in the researchers' studies, participants who agreed that expressing curiosity was a good idea nonetheless defaulted to disguised argument-making, such as asking "How can you believe that?" as if it were a genuine question. <span class="src">A Smarter Way to Disagree</span>
- The behavior-perception gap means that even successfully executed intended behaviors aren't always read as intended by the other person — a question meant to signal curiosity ("Why would you think that?") can land as a sarcastic taunt, and the researchers found a consistent disconnect between how curious/receptive speakers rated their own messages and how curious/receptive readers actually rated the same messages. <span class="src">A Smarter Way to Disagree</span>
- Five specific linguistic techniques, developed and tested across a decade of experiments, close both gaps: state curiosity directly ("I'm curious how you think about XYZ") rather than implying it through rhetorical questions; acknowledge the other side by restating the core of their position before responding, proving you were listening even without agreeing; make common ground explicit ("we both want…") since some shared basis almost always exists even amid sharp disagreement; hedge claims ("from my view…") rather than asserting certainty, since the average person is wrong at least half the time in factual disputes and hedging reads as more thoughtful, not weaker; and share a personal story behind a belief, since vulnerability built trust more effectively than citing research or data in a controlled bonus-allocation experiment. <span class="src">A Smarter Way to Disagree</span>
- Language, not nonverbal behavior, is the right lever for organizational intervention, since most professional communication today happens in writing (email, Slack, text) where nonverbal cues don't even apply, and language is inherently easier to control and less prone to misinterpretation than tone or body language, even though words themselves can still be misread. <span class="src">A Smarter Way to Disagree</span>
- Organizations can operationalize this in four ways: teach explicit verbal skills rather than abstract mindset advice (train "express curiosity verbally" instead of just "be curious"); have leaders model the specific behaviors in team meetings, one-on-ones, and town halls, since conversational receptiveness is contagious — people treated respectfully during disagreement tend to respond in kind; use technology (NLP toxicity monitors, post-conversation feedback dashboards, AI chatbot practice partners) to measure and reinforce the right language at scale, noting employees are often more willing to practice disagreement with an AI than with a real colleague or boss; and factor constructive-disagreement skill into hiring and promotion the same way other soft skills are assessed, following Harvard's approach of asking applicants to describe how they handled a real disagreement. <span class="src">A Smarter Way to Disagree</span>

## Concept

Disagreement is both valuable and risky in organizational life: divergent perspectives spark creativity, catch costly errors, and improve decisions, but disagreement handled poorly carries real interpersonal and financial costs — and despite decades of advice from academics and consultants, conflict in organizations persists. Much of that advice has historically targeted internal mental states: "put yourself in their shoes," "have compassion and empathy," "try to understand rather than judge." A decade of experimental research finds this approach has a fundamental limitation — people cannot read each other's minds, so unless an internal mental state gets translated into something observable, it has essentially no effect on how a disagreement actually unfolds. This was demonstrated directly in a study of 1,113 people writing messages to someone who strongly disagreed with them about a hiring policy: participants who received no guidance, participants who received classic perspective-taking and empathy guidance, and participants given specific words and phrases to signal receptiveness were compared, and only the third group — trained on concrete language — was rated by their disagreement counterparts as more objective, intelligent, and trustworthy, and as someone they'd be more willing to work with again. The implication is that organizations should focus training on observable linguistic behavior specifically, not general mindset coaching, because language (unlike thoughts and feelings) can actually be seen, measured, and improved by others — counterparts, trainers, mentors, or technology. <span class="src">A Smarter Way to Disagree</span>

Two specific gaps explain why good intentions so often fail to produce constructive disagreement in practice. The intention-behavior gap is the same phenomenon behind failed New Year's resolutions: even a genuine intention to stay curious and respectful collapses under the real pressure of active conflict, especially when someone is directly contradicting a deeply held belief. In the researchers' studies, participants overwhelmingly agreed in principle that expressing curiosity toward an opposing view was the right approach, yet when actually asked to do it, they frequently failed — getting pulled back into making their own argument, or disguising an argument as a rhetorical question ("Can you honestly say you think that is a good idea?") rather than genuinely signaling openness. The behavior-perception gap is a second, independent failure mode: even when someone successfully executes their intended behavior, there's no guarantee the other party interprets it as intended, since ultimately the listener — not the speaker — determines how a message comes across. A question meant to convey genuine curiosity ("Why would you think that?") can easily be heard as a sarcastic jab instead, and the research documented a consistent disconnect between how curious and receptive message-writers rated their own communications and how curious and receptive the actual readers rated those same messages. Closing both gaps together requires organizations to help people learn behaviors that will actually be interpreted as intended, and then to help them successfully execute those specific behaviors under real conflict pressure — a two-part problem that vague mindset advice alone cannot solve. <span class="src">A Smarter Way to Disagree</span>

Five specific, research-tested linguistic techniques address this directly, all sharing a common thread: they use language to escape the competitive spiral that disagreements tend to fall into, and each requires the speaker to express a degree of vulnerability that instinct usually resists. Signaling curiosity directly and explicitly ("Hey, it seems we're seeing this differently — I'm curious how you think about XYZ") works better than implying interest through indirect or rhetorical questions, and doesn't require abandoning one's own position in the process ("I believe XYZ, but I'd love to understand where you're coming from"). Acknowledging the other side by restating the core of their statement — even without agreeing with it — proves genuine listening and gives the other person confidence their message actually registered ("I hear you — the team has been working really long hours, and this is a demanding client. But here's why we can't add more staff..."). Finding common ground by explicitly naming shared beliefs, values, or goals ("I agree with some of what you're saying…," "we both want…") counters the tendency for a specific point of disagreement to consume all attention even when broader alignment exists. Hedging claims — using language like "from my view…" or "sometimes it is the case…" rather than asserting flat certainty — is grounded in the finding that in factual disagreements (like a project's cost or a technical glitch's cause), the average person is wrong at least half the time, and research has shown that people who acknowledge a complex issue has multiple sides are perceived as more thoughtful and knowledgeable than people who make dogmatic claims that later turn out false. And sharing a personal story behind a belief builds trust more effectively than leaning on data or research alone, since people's strongest convictions typically trace back to lived experience rather than a slide deck — in one controlled experiment, participants entrusted with dividing a shared bonus after hearing others' positions trusted more those who shared a vulnerable personal story than those who cited research results. <span class="src">A Smarter Way to Disagree</span>

The article specifically recommends focusing organizational effort on language rather than nonverbal behavior like eye contact or posture, for two practical reasons: a large share of today's professional communication already happens in writing (email, Slack, text), where nonverbal signals don't apply at all, and even in verbal exchanges, language is both easier to deliberately control and less prone to misinterpretation than nonverbal cues, which can inflect meaning in ways speakers don't intend. This doesn't mean mindset coaching is worthless — any successful organizational intervention still needs employee buy-in, and someone who genuinely doesn't want to show empathy or curiosity will resist behavioral coaching regardless — but mindset advice should always be paired with concrete guidance on how to actually express that mindset in words, not left as an abstract aspiration. Concretely, organizations have four levers available. They can teach specific verbal skills directly, replacing vague instructions ("be curious," "be open-minded") with trained behaviors (expressing curiosity verbally, practicing conversational receptiveness, learning effective storytelling). They can have leaders model these exact behaviors in the organization's regular rhythms — team meetings, one-on-ones, performance evaluations, town halls — since the research finds conversational receptiveness is contagious, meaning people treated with respect during disagreement tend to respond in kind, reinforcing the behavior across the organization over time. They can use technology to reinforce the change at scale: many organizations already run NLP-based toxicity monitoring on digital conversations, but the more valuable application is proactive rather than reactive — preconversation reminders of useful phrases, dashboards giving employees direct feedback on their own conversational behavior immediately after an interaction, and generative-AI-powered chatbots that let employees practice disagreement language and receive specific feedback, which is especially useful since people are typically more willing to disagree openly with an AI than with a real colleague or manager, given the lower relationship risk. And they can factor constructive-disagreement skill directly into hiring and promotion decisions, the same way organizations already weigh other soft skills like teamwork — Harvard and several other top universities now require applicants to describe a strong disagreement they navigated and how they handled it, a model that translates directly into hiring interviews and performance evaluations for constructive-disagreement ability specifically. <span class="src">A Smarter Way to Disagree</span>
