## Summary

## Concept

Generative AI offers organizations transformative opportunities across learning, work, creativity, and decision-making, but its risks — trust and accuracy problems, hallucinations, biases inherited from underlying foundation models — leave many companies caught in a genuine conundrum: how to harness gen AI's power without falling prey to its failure modes. Some organizations respond to this fear by severely restricting or outright banning gen AI use, an approach the article argues is more dangerous than it appears, since it drives predictable workarounds (employees quietly using personal accounts to circumvent restrictions) while forfeiting the technology's real benefits entirely rather than actually eliminating the underlying risk. Organizations more willing to engage with gen AI typically build a first layer of risk mitigation around policy — usage guidelines, codes of conduct, restricting activity to approved tools, and technical guardrails — which the article treats as necessary and advisable but insufficient on its own, since no policy document can anticipate every unknown, future, or hard-to-define scenario a fast-evolving technology will produce. A second, increasingly common layer adds individual judgment: encouraging employees to bring critical thinking to their use of AI tools, which goes beyond simply vetting AI-generated output to include framing the right collaboration mode with the AI, setting clear context and boundaries, and giving the system continuous feedback. Individual judgment genuinely improves on policy alone, since it lets employees interpret abstract guidelines in the specific context of use — sanitizing sensitive elements out of a prompt to protect confidentiality, for instance, in a way no generic policy document could specify in advance. But individual judgment has a real failure mode of its own: when an AI hallucinates or produces inaccurate output, an individual can fall into a "trust trap," swayed by the model's confident, authoritative tone into accepting content they would otherwise have questioned. <span class="src">To Mitigate Gen AI’s Risks, Draw on Your Team’s Collective Judgment</span>

The article's proposed response to this gap is a third layer of risk mitigation — team-based judgment — that sits alongside policy and individual judgment rather than replacing either. Discussions with colleagues bring genuinely diverse perspectives to bear on an AI's output, challenge conclusions that an individual working alone might accept uncritically, and can surface hidden biases that only become visible when multiple people examine the same content from different angles; reaching out to domain experts specifically helps validate data or resolve areas of doubt where a team lacks the confidence to judge on its own. This team-level judgment takes three specific forms. Collective judgment means teams engage in structured discussion — reviewing AI-generated output together, in shared reflection and discussion routines — specifically to check accuracy, identify risk, and evaluate ethical or legal implications before that output gets accepted and used, catching problems that an individual reviewing the same content alone might miss. Domain judgment means that when unexpected results or anomalies appear in AI output, decision-making authority is explicitly delegated to whoever has the most relevant expertise or is closest to the actual work — not automatically to whoever holds the highest rank on the team — so that identifying and consulting the right subject-matter expert becomes a routine part of the workflow rather than an afterthought, letting their insight guide the team before a problem has a chance to propagate further. Reflective judgment means teams meet on a regular cadence specifically to share and discuss their experiences using AI, including the experiences of others beyond their immediate team, functioning as ongoing organizational learning that keeps pace with how quickly the technology itself keeps changing — team retrospectives focused specifically on gen AI lessons are the concrete mechanism the article points to for this component. <span class="src">To Mitigate Gen AI’s Risks, Draw on Your Team’s Collective Judgment</span>

A constructed example makes the difference between having and lacking this third layer concrete. A three-person team — Gaby, Trent, and Xi — is preparing a management report on digital trends and supply-chain impact, each responsible for one section, using gen AI for research. In the version without team-based judgment, the AI performs well overall but hallucinates on one specific point, fabricating a believable case study and supporting sources for a particular country in Gaby's section; because there's no team review step, Trent and Xi build the rest of the report on top of this fabricated case, amplifying rather than catching the error. In the version with team-based judgment, the same team agrees at the outset to meet weekly to collectively review each section for errors or inconsistencies — practicing collective judgment. When the team reaches Gaby's section in week two, Trent flags the country-specific example as worth double-checking and the team reaches out to Jane, a genuine supply-chain expert from that country — practicing domain judgment — who confirms the example isn't plausible given local regulation, revealing the hallucination before it spreads further into the report. Afterward, the team runs a workshop specifically to share what they learned about recognizing and handling hallucinations with other colleagues — practicing reflective judgment. The three components work in concert in this example exactly as the framework intends: collective judgment provides the thorough review and cross-checking that catches the error, domain judgment supplies expert validation of the specific detail in question, and reflective judgment turns a single team's near-miss into organizational learning that helps other teams avoid the same trap. A real-world parallel comes from Ferrari's automotive engineering practice, described by Powertrain Project Lead Valentin Marguet: engineering teams diagnosing complex mechanical faults, such as oil leakage in a turbocharged engine (with candidate causes spanning gasket failure, seal deterioration, component fatigue, improper assembly, and inaccurate monitoring), use gen AI to augment their own root-cause analysis but stay explicitly "in control" by critically assessing AI outputs against their own domain experts, quickly recalibrating and refining the analysis process to keep AI contributions "technically grounded" rather than accepted uncritically. <span class="src">To Mitigate Gen AI’s Risks, Draw on Your Team’s Collective Judgment</span>

Team-based judgment doesn't emerge automatically from simply telling a team to "collaborate more" — the article treats it as a capability that needs deliberate cultivation and ongoing reinforcement, offering concrete building blocks for leaders. Creating explicit routines for reflection and discussion within project timelines — short checkpoint meetings specifically designed for collective sensemaking, structured around concrete challenge questions like "is this plausible?", "are the sources cited correctly?", and "is this recommendation prone to potential risks or biases?" — turns collective judgment from an aspiration into a repeatable practice, and doing this in a group is more powerful than doing it alone precisely because different perspectives and expertise combine to make the group collectively less susceptible to the trust trap than any individual member. Establishing mechanisms for identifying and reaching domain experts quickly — expert maps or subject-matter-expert lists, internal communities of practice, and incentives for the experts who take time to support other teams — operationalizes domain judgment so that finding the right person to consult doesn't itself become a bottleneck when time is short. And building in dedicated time and infrastructure for continuous learning — retrospective sessions built directly into project plans, experience-sharing sessions, and a shared knowledge repository or dedicated gen AI academy for consolidating what's learned — operationalizes reflective judgment across projects and over time, rather than letting each team relearn the same lessons independently. Cultivated at scale, this three-part capability is positioned as a genuine strategic asset for navigating an AI-driven future: it equips organizations to adapt rapidly to a fast-evolving technology landscape and to face risks that aren't yet widespread or even known, turning collective organizational wisdom into a source of confidence for continued AI adoption rather than a reason for defensive retreat. <span class="src">To Mitigate Gen AI’s Risks, Draw on Your Team’s Collective Judgment</span>
