## Summary

Large language models generate text by repeatedly predicting the next token, which makes hallucination an inherent consequence of the design and produces limits in reasoning, knowledge, understanding and planning that no prompt or retrieval technique removes entirely. Practical use therefore depends on human review, AI judges, retrieval and complementary technologies, as the finding that consultants using GPT-4 erred more often on a reasoning task shows. Scaling laws reliably predict better next-word prediction but not new capabilities, and data limits, the possibility of sudden plateaus, the narrow role of synthetic data and a market shift toward smaller, longer-trained models suggest that capability gains from scale alone may slow.

## Concept

At the mechanical level an LLM does one thing repeatedly, predicting the most likely next token, a word or sub-word unit, given everything before it, appending that token and repeating. External software, not the model, runs the loop and decides when to stop, based on the model's own predictions, since it learns in training to emit a special end-of-sequence token where an example's answer was complete, and on external rules such as a maximum token limit or a user-defined stop sequence. The appearance of a model deciding that it is done is therefore an interaction between statistical prediction and configurable software logic, which a team building an application can and should tune deliberately. <span class="src">How LLMs Work — Top 10 Executive-Level Questions</span>

Three technical factors shape what a model can do. Parameters are the trainable weights that encode relationships extracted from training data, and more of them generally means more nuanced, knowledgeable output, as GPT-3's 175 billion do, training data volume, measured in tokens, matters because a larger and more diverse corpus generally improves generalization, as GPT-3's roughly half a trillion tokens did, and context window size sets how much prompt text the model considers at once, 2,000 tokens for GPT-3, with a larger, more detailed context generally improving accuracy for the task at hand. A model does not learn from a correction in real time, since a user's correction might inform a training run weeks or months later but the running model does not update, and apparent memory of earlier conversations is a separate feature that stores facts such as a name, preferences and recurring topics and silently re-injects them into a new prompt, so the model receives reminders as input rather than recalling a past interaction. <span class="src">How LLMs Work — Top 10 Executive-Level Questions</span> <span class="src">What Are LLMs and Generative AI</span> <span class="src">The Working Limitations of Large Language Models</span>

The same mechanism explains why retrieval-augmented generation remains valuable even with context windows in the million-token range. Overloading a prompt with excess or irrelevant material degrades output, models do not weigh all parts of a long prompt equally and attend more to the beginning and end, missing details buried in the middle, and longer prompts cost more and run slower in production, all of which argue for retrieving and including only the most relevant material instead of stuffing everything into a larger window. <span class="src">How LLMs Work — Top 10 Executive-Level Questions</span>

Hallucination, confident and plausible output that is fabricated or wrong, is a structural consequence of token prediction rather than a bug that engineering will eliminate, because the model generates statistically likely sequences rather than verifying claims against a ground-truth source. Prompt engineering, retrieval, domain-specific fine-tuning and rule-based post-processing checks can each reduce hallucination for a use case but none can guarantee its absence, and this extends to the citations a model provides, which can be as readily fabricated or misapplied as any other content, so a citation should never be trusted without independent verification that the source exists and supports the claim. <span class="src">How LLMs Work — Top 10 Executive-Level Questions</span>

Four broader limitations follow from the same mechanism. Reasoning limits mean LLMs simulate individual logical rules convincingly but do not reliably chain them into valid multi-step conclusions, as a cabbage-classification puzzle that a model confidently answers wrongly shows, each step carries a nonzero chance of error, and the model often cannot explain the chain that produced its answer, so reviewers struggle to catch errors. Knowledge and expertise limits mean the model is bounded by, and can reproduce errors in, its training data, producing fabrication such as invented citations and case law and inconsistency, since the same question can receive materially different answers depending on phrasing or timing. Understanding limits mean it can misread the logical structure of a prompt, as in a nested-boxes puzzle it gets backwards, which is hardest to catch when the user does not know the domain well enough to notice or to write a better prompt. Planning limits mean plans that look step by step can be practically naive, since the model does not question whether a stated goal is realistic or interrogate the user's assumptions as a competent advisor would. <span class="src">The Working Limitations of Large Language Models</span>

The stakes appeared in a field experiment with more than 750 Boston Consulting Group consultants, in which participants using GPT-4 on a business reasoning task got the answer wrong 23% more often than a no-AI control group, not mainly because the model erred more often alone but because its confident, persuasive rationale led users to accept the wrong answer without independent verification. <span class="src">The Working Limitations of Large Language Models</span>

Since the limitations cannot be engineered away entirely, applications should be designed around them. Human review remains the most reliable safeguard for open-ended tasks, with sampling or risk-based triage used to manage the cost of reviewing at scale. For structured or high-volume tasks a second AI judge, typically another LLM that evaluates the first model's output, scales better, but the judge can also hallucinate or fail to match human judgment on complex cases, so its feedback should be combined with retrieval-based fact-checking or escalation of low-confidence outputs to a person. Complementary technologies address specific limits, namely knowledge graphs and dedicated reasoning engines for multi-step inference and domain-specific fine-tuned models, such as models trained on clinical literature to medical-licensing-exam caliber, for expertise beyond a general model's broad training. <span class="src">The Working Limitations of Large Language Models</span>

McKinsey's introductory explainer places these limits in a business-risk frame, noting the reputational and legal exposure from publishing biased, offensive or copyrighted output and estimating that generative AI could add up to USD 4.4 trillion a year to the global economy. It distinguishes predictive machine learning, which classifies patterns, from generative AI, which creates content, describes supervised and self-supervised training and cites GPT-3's roughly 45 terabytes of training text to explain why only well-resourced firms build such models. Its mitigations are careful selection of training data, smaller specialized models, customization on proprietary data, keeping a human in the loop and avoiding generative AI for critical decisions about significant resources or human welfare, and its examples of counting errors, basic algebra and jailbreak-style manipulation reflect early models. <span class="src">What Is Generative AI</span>

Scaling laws describe a highly predictable relationship, since as model size, training compute and dataset size rise together, language models reliably achieve lower perplexity, meaning better accuracy at predicting the next word. This predictability underlies the popular expectation that scaling will continue for many more orders of magnitude and eventually yield artificial general intelligence, but the expectation misreads the laws. Perplexity is largely irrelevant to end users, who care about emergent abilities, the new capabilities that appear with scale, and although emergence has accompanied scaling so far, it follows no comparably law-like regularity, so past emergence guarantees nothing about further scaling. On benchmarks designed to test extrapolation to genuinely unseen task types, LLMs tend to perform poorly, which suggests, without proving, that their gains come more from interpolating within patterns in training data than from a general capacity to extrapolate, and if so capability gains should plateau once the space of tasks represented in available data is effectively exhausted. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>

Data availability is a more immediate constraint. Developers already draw on essentially all readily available high-quality text, and proposed new sources are smaller than they appear, since transcribing all of YouTube's roughly 150 billion minutes of video would, after removing music and non-speech content and applying deduplication and quality filtering, likely add substantially less usable data than Llama 3's training set of about 15 trillion tokens. Acquiring more is also getting costlier, as copyright holders increasingly expect compensation and reputational and regulatory pushback against large-scale collection grows. Precedent, in CPU clock speeds through the 2000s and civil aircraft speeds since the 1970s, shows that exponential trends can plateau suddenly and that a trendline alone gives no warning, so it is impossible to know how many further orders of magnitude of useful scaling remain. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>

Synthetic data is often proposed to get around the ceiling, but the evidence suggests it works mainly for closing specific capability gaps, in math, code, low-resource languages and alignment, rather than for increasing overall training volume. The clearest successes, such as AlphaGo's self-play, depend on a fully self-contained, simulatable environment in which a model can generate unlimited high-quality training signal by playing itself, a System 2 to System 1 distillation pattern expected to extend to bounded domains such as code generation but not to open-ended tasks such as translation, where no equivalent self-play loop exists. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>

Industry behavior is shifting from maximizing model size to maximizing capability per dollar. Dataset size, model size and training compute once scaled together as the jointly optimal strategy, but with high-quality data a growing bottleneck developers push toward smaller models trained for longer. Using API pricing as a rough size proxy, GPT-4o costs about 25% of GPT-4 while matching or exceeding its capability, Claude 3.5 Sonnet is about five times cheaper than Claude 3 Opus yet more capable, and Gemini 1.5 Pro is cheaper and more capable than Gemini 1.0 Ultra, so in each family the largest model is no longer the most capable. Smaller models need proportionally more training compute to reach a given level, so the shift trades training cost for lower inference cost, a deliberate rebalancing because inference cost over a model's lifetime is thought to dominate total cost, and earlier models such as GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 are considered under-trained on this measure, with Llama 3's 8-billion-parameter model using roughly 20 times the training compute of the original similarly sized Llama. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>

Narayanan frames the trend as a ladder of generality, a long-running progression in which computers need less effort to be directed at a new task, from special-purpose machines through Turing machines to today's instruction-tuned LLMs, and expects that the current step is unlikely to be the last, but notes that the AI research community has been poor at predicting how many steps remain, what triggers the next or when, a difficulty expected to persist however the scaling debate resolves. <span class="src">AI scaling myths</span>
