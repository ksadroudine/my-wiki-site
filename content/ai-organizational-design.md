## Summary

The arrival of capable, human-scale AI is pressuring organizations to redesign work processes and structures built for an earlier, pre-AI era. Empirical research on GitHub Copilot use and, separately, physical robot adoption both show AI/automation flattening hierarchy in practice, a companion piece frames the underlying choice organizations face as coaching workers with AI versus algorithmic control, and BBVA's bank-wide rollout supplies a concrete playbook for turning informal shadow AI use into a managed organizational asset.

## Concept

Organizational structures have historically formed around the constraints of the dominant technology of their era. The modern org chart originated with the New York and Erie Railroad in 1855, when executive Daniel McCallum used the telegraph to coordinate work across a large workforce spread over a wide geographic area, producing what is considered the first organizational chart. Henry Ford's assembly line exploited mechanical clocks and standardized parts; Agile software development, formalized in 2001, exploited new software tooling and internet-based communication. Each of these structures persisted for decades because they were built around genuinely fixed human limits — finite attention, the need for people to physically be present, the fact that adding capability to a project meant adding more people or making existing people work harder. <span class="src">Reshaping the tree rebuilding organizations for AI</span>

AI breaks that constraint: for the first time, "intelligence" can be added to a project without adding headcount. This is already happening informally and largely unsanctioned — a 2023 Salesforce survey found over half of employees using AI at work were doing so without organizational approval, and 64% had passed off AI-assisted output as their own unaided work. This "shadow AI" use is possible because LLMs operate at human scale: they can read documents, draft emails, and adapt to context without requiring specialized training or bespoke software integration, so an individual with access to a capable model can simply start delegating tasks to it, independent of any top-down rollout. <span class="src">Reshaping the tree rebuilding organizations for AI</span>

Redesigning a process around AI, rather than merely automating one step of an unchanged process, means treating AI as an added participant throughout the workflow. A described case study from a small educational-software team illustrates this at the level of a single feature-design process: AI personas were used to generate first-pass simulated user feedback in place of (or ahead of) human testers; AI was used to synthesize scattered team feedback into a single agenda ahead of a meeting, shortening the meeting itself; and AI-driven screenshot-to-code tooling let the team produce and iterate on working prototypes live during the meeting rather than only planning changes to be built later. In that account, a process that previously took roughly a week was reduced to one or two days. The same account anticipates a further step in which autonomous AI agents carry concepts through to deployed code with minimal human intervention, with humans shifting toward a supervisory role. <span class="src">Reshaping the tree rebuilding organizations for AI</span>

From this, three principles are proposed for organizations trying to adapt deliberately rather than reactively. First, let teams develop and share their own methods for using AI, since AI performs more like an additional team member than an external IT system, and top-down mandates are likely to fit individual team contexts poorly; this requires incentives and culture that make employees comfortable experimenting and disclosing what they're doing. Second, build processes for the more capable models expected in the near future, not just for what today's models can do, since organizational change itself takes time to implement. Third, move quickly: if the efficiency gains observed in early AI experiments continue, organizations that delay experimentation risk falling behind rapidly, which argues for lightweight, bottom-up experimentation guidelines rather than slow, centrally-designed rollouts. <span class="src">Reshaping the tree rebuilding organizations for AI</span>

Empirical research on one specific role bears out the structural shift Mollick describes in the abstract: <span class="src">How AI Is Redefining Managerial Roles</span> reports a Harvard Business School study, led by Manuel Hoffmann, of 50,032 software developers observed from 2022 to 2024, half of whom used GitHub Copilot. The researchers tracked more than 2.4 million platform actions, sorting them into core work (coding) and managerial work (coordination and project management), and found that Copilot access shifted developers' task allocation toward core work (+5%) and away from project management (-10%). Two mechanisms drove this: developers using gen AI worked more autonomously and needed less coordination with others, and faster coding freed up time for exploration and learning. The authors argue this lets companies flatten their reporting structure — not by eliminating middle managers outright, but by reducing how many are needed and redirecting the ones who remain away from routine coordination, scheduling, and quality-checking toward higher-value hands-on work and process improvement. The effect is uneven across skill levels: because less-skilled workers are more likely to trust an AI system's guidance than their own judgment, they benefit disproportionately from gen AI's availability, which frees managers from a significant share of the burden of directly upskilling low performers. A companion interview with KPMG's head of Workforce Innovation, Edwige Sacco, corroborates this from inside a real organization: gen AI is blurring the line between middle-manager and associate-level work, since associates who use it to compress a five-hour client-discovery task into three hours are reinvesting the freed time in more strategic work once reserved for managers, while managers who remain increasingly focus on the human-centric client conversations — reading between the lines, articulating a client's real problem — that gen AI still cannot handle. <span class="src">How AI Is Redefining Managerial Roles</span>

<span class="src">Reinventing the Organization for GenAI and LLMs</span> (MIT SMR, Ethan Mollick, April 2024) is a companion piece by the same researcher, published five months after "Reshaping the Tree," that reuses much of the same underlying material — the 1855 railroad org-chart origin story, the Wharton Interactive case study, and the Salesforce shadow-AI survey statistics — while extending the argument in a genuinely new direction: the question of whether AI-enabled organizational change tends toward worker control or worker coaching. Prior algorithmic management (Uber's driver ratings, UPS's route-efficiency tracking) already gave companies comprehensive, real-time, and largely opaque visibility into worker behavior, a shift MIT Sloan's Katherine Kellogg and coauthors characterized as categorically different from traditional supervision. The article argues LLMs could supercharge this into a more totalizing form of control — an AI system that tracks, targets, evaluates, and rewards workers while also delivering feedback in a way that feels warm and personal, potentially making comprehensive surveillance more palatable without changing who is actually in charge. But the article treats this trajectory as a genuine choice rather than an inevitability: organizations that instead let AI remove the most tedious parts of a job, leaving employees with more interesting work, see workers respond positively in ways that support engagement and retention. Translating this into practice requires the same shadow-AI-surfacing challenge "Reshaping the Tree" identifies, but the article is more specific about the incentive design required to solve it — not just permission to use AI openly, but concrete rewards (explicitly, cash prizes, promotions, and flexible work arrangements) for employees who surface substantial AI-driven process improvements, on the logic that such incentives are a small price relative to the productivity gains at stake. <span class="src">Reinventing the Organization for GenAI and LLMs</span>

<span class="src">The Hidden Demand for AI Inside Your Company</span> (HBR, April 2026) supplies the concrete, enterprise-scale answer to a question both Mollick pieces raise but don't resolve: what should an organization actually do once it recognizes its own shadow AI economy? Using BBVA, a 125,000-employee, 25-country bank, as its central case, the article argues that most corporate gen AI programs fail by treating employees' unauthorized consumer-AI use as a compliance problem to be restricted rather than a demand signal to be redirected — a mistake, since a recent industry report found that although only 40% of companies had purchased official LLM subscriptions, employees at more than 90% of those companies reported regular personal AI use for work regardless. BBVA's response rested on three principles matching this concept's own thesis almost point for point: treat AI as an assistant rather than a replacement, give employees autonomy with clear accountability for results, and build a peer-to-peer network (BBVA's "Champions" and "Wizards") to spread knowledge rather than relying on top-down IT mandates. The bank moved fast (compressing legal and GDPR review into two months) and scaled through engineered scarcity rather than blanket rollout — an initial 3,000 licenses, allocated competitively and revoked from low users, made access a privilege rather than an obligation and created organic demand that grew usage to 11,000 active users within a year, over 4,800 employee-built custom GPTs, and reported time savings of 2-5 hours per week (3-4 hours per week among the 600-person internal audit team, which reached 99% adoption). Governance stayed centralized on the dimension that mattered most — a hard rule that AI outputs could never write directly to core systems without human validation, backed by mandatory training and an automated "Quality Score" evaluating the guardrails of thousands of employee-created GPTs, since manual case-by-case risk review at that scale was infeasible. The article closes with five transferable lessons for other organizations: secure the environment quickly rather than studying the problem at length, make access attractive through scarcity rather than mandate, designate power users to diffuse tacit knowledge, train managers concretely rather than abstractly before asking them to sponsor change, and enforce human ownership of AI-assisted work as a non-negotiable structural constraint. <span class="src">The Hidden Demand for AI Inside Your Company</span>

Read together with the two Mollick pieces, BBVA's experience is the empirical proof of concept for "Reshaping the Tree"'s central claim that shadow AI use signals genuine organizational demand rather than a risk to be suppressed, and for "Reinventing the Organization"'s argument that surfacing that demand requires concrete incentives rather than mere permission — while adding operational detail (engineered scarcity, a formalized Champions/Wizards network, an automated Quality Score) neither theoretical piece specifies.

<span class="src">Why Robots Will Displace Managers — and Create Other Jobs</span> (MIT SMR, June 2025) extends this concept's hierarchy-flattening thesis beyond generative AI to physical robots, drawing on the authors' peer-reviewed Management Science study of firms adopting industrial and collaborative robots in production. The mechanism differs from GitHub Copilot's — robots don't make individual employees more autonomous the way a coding copilot does — but the organizational effect converges on the same outcome. Robots dramatically improve how precisely individual employee performance can be measured and compared, since a worker's productivity working alongside a consistent, transparent robot is far easier to isolate from teammates' contributions than performance in a purely human team; this lets one manager effectively supervise a larger group, with the study finding each $1 million in robot investment associated with roughly a 5-person increase in average span of control, directly reducing how many middle managers a robot-adopting organization needs. Critically, the effect isn't simple job destruction: middle-skilled roles (machinists, welders, assembly-line workers) decline as robots take over their tasks with greater consistency and falling cost, but companies that invest in robots hire significantly more high-skilled workers (to program, design, and manage the robots themselves) and more low-skilled workers (for tasks robots still can't reliably perform, illustrated by Amazon's continued reliance on human labor to load and operate delivery vans) — with the combined increase in high- and low-skilled hiring outweighing the middle-skilled losses in net employment terms. The article's closing case study, Stanley Black & Decker's robot-based tape-inspection system, illustrates a role middle managers retain even as their ranks thin: robot-collected inspection data revealed upstream production-quality patterns that neither C-suite executives nor frontline workers were positioned to see, and it took middle managers' cross-functional vantage point to both discover the analytics opportunity and drive the resulting process fix — a concrete instance of the "role redefinition rather than elimination" pattern this concept's other sources describe for genAI-driven hierarchy flattening, here demonstrated for physical automation instead. <span class="src">Why Robots Will Displace Managers — and Create Other Jobs</span>

## Open Questions

- The source's process-redesign example is drawn from a single small internal team (Wharton Interactive); it does not address how the same redesign approach scales to larger organizations with more formal approval chains, compliance requirements, or unionized workforces.
