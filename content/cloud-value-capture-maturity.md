## Summary

Three consultancies' maturity models for why some companies capture dramatically more cloud value than others — McKinsey's seven value drivers behind a projected $1 trillion prize, Bain's four states of adoption (Avoiders, Migrators, Explorers, Strategic Value Creators), and PwC's four behaviors distinguishing AI-and-cloud "Top Performers." All converge on the same finding — business-IT co-ownership, not technology maturity alone, is what separates leaders from laggards.

## Concept

Three consultancies, working independently and at different points across roughly a decade of cloud adoption research, arrive at strikingly convergent conclusions about why some companies capture dramatically more value from cloud (and now AI) than others — and none of them attribute the gap primarily to technology sophistication. McKinsey's contribution quantifies the scale of what's actually achievable: a detailed review of cloud cost-optimization levers and value-oriented business use cases estimates more than $1 trillion in run-rate EBITDA is up for grabs across Fortune 500 companies by 2030, organized across three dimensions of increasing ambition. Rejuvenate describes using cloud to break from legacy approaches and lower cost and risk across IT and core operations — effective cloud usage measurably improves application development and maintenance productivity by 38 percent and infrastructure cost efficiency by 29 percent for migrated applications, and could cut downtime roughly 57 percent for migrated applications against a backdrop of $650 billion in projected 2030 losses from downtime and security breaches, though McKinsey is explicit that a careless "lift and shift" migration executed without optimization or remediation can actually increase costs rather than reduce them. Innovate describes harnessing cloud to accelerate or enable innovation — new and enhanced use cases enabled by on-demand access to nearly unlimited infrastructure, accelerated product development through cloud-native tooling (containers, microservices, DevOps, CI/CD, serverless architecture), and rapid scaling to new customer segments and geographies leveraging a cloud provider's own global infrastructure. Pioneer describes the leading edge, where sufficiently mature cloud adopters begin experimenting with genuinely emerging technology — quantum computing, blockchain, augmented and virtual reality, 3D printing — that McKinsey couldn't precisely quantify as of this research but flags as the next stage of value evolution worth starting to experiment with now. Moderna, whose cloud-built mRNA "Drug Design Studio" let it deliver the first clinical batch of its COVID-19 vaccine candidate to the NIH just 42 days after initial viral sequencing, illustrates the kind of speed this combination of dimensions can unlock in practice — CEO Stéphane Bancel's own framing was that cloud let the company move fast "because you don't have to reinvent everything, you just fly." McKinsey's prescription for capturing this value rests on four actions: setting an ambitious, urgent business aspiration rather than a conservative target that undersells what cloud actually makes possible; pursuing a hard-headed economic case that captures both rejuvenate-style cost savings and innovate-style business acceleration, adjusted realistically for transformation risk and sequenced by business domain; adopting agile, cloud-native ways of working through what McKinsey calls a "DevSecFinOps" approach — small cross-functional teams working within a well-defined architecture to deliver business cases (not merely applications) in rapid iterative cycles, with security embedded into development and end-to-end process automation built in from the start; and building a standardized, automated cloud platform that gives developers, cloud's primary day-to-day consumers, a genuinely strong self-service experience through automated, API-based provisioning. <span class="src">Cloud’s trillion-dollar prize is up for grabs</span>

Bain's contribution turns McKinsey's value-potential framing into a maturity model describing where companies actually sit relative to that potential, identifying four states most companies fall into, with the majority clustered in the first two. Avoiders still operate entirely on their own data centers, often citing perceived regulatory requirements, risk concerns, or simple underappreciation of cloud's potential — and pay a heavy long-term price for it, missing out on faster innovation speed and access to entirely new cloud-enabled profit pools that accrue disproportionately to earlier movers. Migrators, the largest single group, have delegated cloud to the IT department without any comprehensive, value-based cloud strategy, pursuing it from a narrow infrastructure-cost-and-flexibility lens; within this group, roughly 60 percent take a pure "lift-and-shift" approach that moves workloads to the cloud without reengineering applications to actually optimize cost or unlock cloud-native capability — a choice frequently made in anticipation of cost savings that may never materialize, and that can even raise total costs if the company ends up paying for both cloud usage and a lingering on-premises footprint simultaneously — while the other 40 percent do take time to modernize as they migrate. Even Migrators executing the harder, right path commonly hit real friction: Bain's research finds companies reporting that roughly 40 percent of their cloud execution capabilities are insufficient, producing delayed cloud programs that underdeliver on targeted results (often traceable to underinvestment), and shadow IT structures that emerge when central IT gets sidestepped for individual, decentralized migration efforts — a dynamic that can trap organizations in an unproductive cycle where business leaders disengage from cloud topics because they've only ever seen cost overruns and delay, making it progressively harder for IT to reopen the strategic conversation cloud actually deserves. As complexity compounds at this stage, companies commonly lose control of cloud total cost of ownership (TCO) entirely — but Bain's research finds that deliberately focusing on cost and value optimization can improve cloud TCO by 20 to 30 percent, freeing funds that can then be redirected into genuinely strategic cloud opportunities rather than simply absorbed by uncontrolled sprawl. Explorers represent an attempted break from this cycle, typically driven by individual leaders (a CIO or CDO partnering with a specific business leader) rather than a robust, company-wide effort; these pairings understand cloud's real value — faster speed to market through quicker feature deployment, for instance — but tend to produce isolated success stories reached through lengthy trial and error rather than a repeatable engine for driving high business value organization-wide, even though these efforts and their successes are genuinely worth encouraging and celebrating as the organization continues progressing. Strategic Value Creators, the rarest state (Bain research finds only 16 percent of companies believe they have an effective cloud roadmap actually focused on strategic priorities, a proxy for how few reach this stage), have developed a genuinely deep understanding of how cloud creates business value — new revenue streams, new customer-facing services, broad technology adoption — and treat cloud initiatives as a company-wide effort rather than an IT department responsibility. Moderna again illustrates this state concretely, having started building genuine strategic cloud capability at its 2010 founding — its clinical-trials team and IT department jointly built a "world-class digital lab" living on the cloud years before COVID hit, meaning the accelerated vaccine-development timeline the world eventually saw was the product of a decade of deliberate strategic investment, not a lucky pandemic-era pivot. Capital One offers a second example: the first bank to announce an all-in cloud commitment, in 2015, it has since used cloud-enabled AI and machine learning for real-time fraud detection, speech-recognition-driven customer experience improvements, automated call-center validation, and real-time availability of previously siloed data sources for new business use cases. What these Strategic Value Creators share, per Bain, comes down to three critical success factors: genuine business engagement, with committed top leadership actively developing and communicating an understanding of cloud's business value rather than delegating that understanding entirely to IT; deep collaboration between business and technology specifically aimed at both accelerating existing initiatives and identifying genuinely new high-value ones, rather than IT and business simply coexisting; and building the actual execution capabilities and investment behind cloud initiatives, since Bain's experience finds many companies fail here even when their strategic ideas are sound — having the right ambition is necessary but insufficient without matching capability-building behind it. <span class="src">Harnessing the Value of Cloud</span>

PwC's contribution extends this same "leaders versus laggards" framing directly into the AI era, based on a survey of 1,030 US executives (308 in business roles, 722 in technology roles, all from companies with at least $500 million in revenue) conducted in June-July 2024. PwC identifies "Top Performers" — 12 percent of the surveyed sample, 124 companies — as those scoring at the top range of both a cloud-maturity performance index and a separate GenAI-maturity performance index, and finds these Top Performers are roughly twice as likely as other surveyed companies to have already achieved measurable value across each of ten surveyed value categories. Four specific behaviors distinguish them. First, Top Performers prioritize AI-powered cloud architecture as genuinely integrated business strategy rather than a disconnected technology initiative: two-thirds (67 percent) report having a formalized strategy for identifying, implementing, and tracking AI use across the whole organization, versus just 37 percent of other companies, and the starkest single behavioral gap in the entire survey is that 72 percent of Top Performers report "all-in cloud adoption" for data modernization specifically, compared to just 33 percent of other companies — with similarly large maturity gaps (generally more than double) across other cloud-engineering disciplines including security, AI, migration, app modernization, and native app development. Second, Top Performers double down on data modernization specifically because GenAI raises the competitive stakes of good data: as GenAI itself becomes a commodity built on the same handful of underlying models, an organization's own proprietary data and specialty knowledge becomes the actual differentiator, requiring a genuinely AI-ready data platform and governance model to exploit — 98 percent of Top Performers agree their data architecture and governance are well-equipped for GenAI, and 69 percent have already implemented GenAI-oriented data modernization versus just 31 percent of other companies, with correspondingly higher shares of their front-, middle-, and back-office data actually living in the cloud (82, 74, and 79 percent respectively, versus 63, 52, and 61 percent for other companies). Third, Top Performers evolve IT itself into a strategic powerhouse by embracing AI-powered, agentic workflows within the technology function specifically, treating it as a natural starting point for the broader shift toward AI working in concert with humans across every business function — 65 percent report holistic AI adoption across app development and management, compared to just 28 percent of other companies, with the average adoption gap across 15 distinct IT categories running 23 percentage points in Top Performers' favor, and software-development-specific GenAI adoption showing efficiency gains upward of 30 percent along with better quality and faster time to market. Fourth, and least mature even among Top Performers, is resetting cloud provider relationships and expectations to match this new reality: Top Performers rate their primary cloud service providers more favorably than other companies across several dimensions (KPI/performance-monitoring transparency, technical support, and engagement around evolving capabilities), and are somewhat more engaged than other companies in newer areas like assessing CSP carbon-footprint impact — but strikingly, even Top Performers lag on the most direct lever available to them: only 35 percent report actively renegotiating CSP pricing, contracts, or SLAs (versus 38 percent of other companies), despite AI having already driven service-delivery efficiencies of 20 to 40 percent that would obviously justify a fresh look at existing vendor terms — a genuine gap between recognizing AI's cost implications and actually acting on them commercially, even at the most sophisticated companies in the survey. <span class="src">2024 Cloud and AI Business Survey</span>

## Open Questions

- None of the three sources addresses whether the maturity gap they document is closing or widening over time — whether "laggards" are catching up to where "leaders" were a few years earlier, or whether leaders are pulling further ahead as the underlying technology itself keeps advancing.
