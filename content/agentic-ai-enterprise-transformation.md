## Summary

McKinsey's account of how AI agents resolve the "gen AI paradox" — widespread adoption with minimal bottom-line impact — by automating whole business processes rather than isolated tasks. Covers the "agentic AI mesh" architecture paradigm, the CEO-led "agent factory" operating model, and a two-to-three-year roadmap from cost-cutting pilots to P&L-level, agent-first business transformation. A companion EY framework identifies the same root cause from a different angle — up to 75% of enterprise value trapped between functional silos — and proposes a seven-layer "Agentic Enterprise" operating model to recover it. BCG also describes an agentic transformation office that automates programme tracking while keeping business owners accountable.

## Concept

Two sequential McKinsey pieces from the same research team trace a single argument across two acts: first diagnosing why generative AI adoption hasn't translated into bottom-line impact, then prescribing exactly how CEOs should lead the transformation that finally closes that gap. The starting diagnosis is what McKinsey calls the "gen AI paradox": more than 78 percent of companies now use gen AI in at least one business function, up sharply from 55 percent a year earlier, yet more than 80 percent report no material contribution to earnings, and only 1 percent of surveyed enterprises consider their gen AI strategy genuinely mature. The paradox traces to a structural imbalance between two categories of deployment. Horizontal solutions — enterprise-wide copilots and chatbots, with nearly 70 percent of Fortune 500 companies already using Microsoft 365 Copilot — scale quickly precisely because they're accessible, off-the-shelf, and require no workflow redesign (activating Copilot can be as simple as flipping on an Office 365 extension), and much of their initial adoption was driven by risk mitigation as organizations raced to offer secure internal alternatives to external tools employees were already experimenting with. But their productivity gains, while real, spread thinly across individual employees and stay largely invisible in top- or bottom-line results. Vertical use cases — embedded into specific business functions and processes, with far higher potential for direct economic impact — see the opposite problem: fewer than 10 percent of deployed use cases ever escape pilot mode, and even fully deployed ones typically support only isolated steps of a larger process, operating reactively when a human prompts them rather than proactively or autonomously. Six factors explain this limited vertical scaling: fragmented, bottom-up initiatives identified function-by-function rather than enterprise-wide (fewer than 30 percent of companies report direct CEO sponsorship of their AI agenda, producing a proliferation of disconnected micro-initiatives); a lack of mature, packaged solutions that forces teams to build custom, often without the MLOps engineering talent needed to industrialize and maintain models in production; genuine technological limitations of first-generation LLMs (inaccurate outputs undermining trust in precision-critical environments; fundamental passivity, since LLMs don't act unless prompted and can't independently drive workflows; weak handling of complex, multi-step, branching-logic workflows; and limited persistent memory, making coherent operation across extended interactions difficult); AI centers of excellence that accelerated early experimentation but often operated in silos, disconnected from core IT, data, and business functions in ways that later made scaling difficult; persistent data accessibility and quality gaps, especially for largely ungoverned unstructured data; and cultural apprehension and organizational inertia rooted in fear of disruption and unfamiliarity with the technology. <span class="src">Seizing the agentic AI advantage</span>

AI agents are positioned as the resolution to this paradox precisely because they extend LLMs with memory, planning, orchestration, and integration capabilities that let them understand goals, decompose them into subtasks, interact with both humans and systems, execute actions, and adapt in real time — shifting gen AI from reactive content generation to autonomous, goal-driven execution capable of automating entire complex, multistep business workflows that first-generation tools simply couldn't reach. Beyond raw efficiency, agents transform operations across five dimensions: accelerating execution through parallel rather than strictly sequential task processing; bringing adaptability by continuously ingesting data to reshuffle priorities and flag anomalies before they cascade; enabling personalization by tailoring decisions to individual customer context in real time; bringing elasticity, since digital execution capacity can expand and contract with workload in ways fixed human staffing can't match; and improving resilience by monitoring disruptions and rerouting operations autonomously. Beyond operations, agents can amplify existing revenue (proactively surfacing real-time upsell and cross-sell offers based on behavior and context) or create genuinely new revenue streams (industrial companies embedding agents in connected products to enable pay-per-use or performance-based monetization; service organizations packaging internal expertise — legal reasoning, tax interpretation, procurement best practices — as agent-based SaaS or API products for clients and partners). Real deployments already demonstrate this range concretely: a large bank's hybrid AI-human "digital factory," where human workers supervise squads of agents documenting legacy code, writing new code, reviewing each other's work, and integrating features for testing, cut time and effort more than 50 percent for early-adopter teams against a $600 million, 400-application legacy-modernization project; a market research firm's multiagent system autonomously identifying data anomalies and explaining market-share shifts (replacing a 500-person manual process where 80 percent of errors were caught only by clients) showed more than 60 percent potential productivity gains and expected annual savings above $3 million; and a retail bank's AI-assisted credit-risk-memo workflow, extracting data across at least ten sources and drafting nuanced, interdependent memo sections, showed a 20-to-60-percent productivity increase including a 30-percent faster credit turnaround. The critical lesson from these deployments is that maximizing value requires reinventing processes around agents, not merely inserting agents into legacy workflows — illustrated by a hypothetical call center moving through three levels of transformation: gen AI assisting human agents within an otherwise unchanged, fully manual process yields a modest 5-to-10-percent productivity improvement; adding agents at specific steps (classifying tickets, proposing resolutions, autonomously resolving simple cases like password resets) without reconfiguring end-to-end routing yields 20-to-40-percent time savings and 30-to-50-percent backlog reduction, but coordination friction still caps the gains; only the third level — reimagining the process around genuine agent autonomy, with agents proactively detecting issues, initiating resolution, and communicating directly with customers while humans shift to escalation-management and quality-oversight roles — unlocks transformative impact, with up to 80 percent of common incidents resolved autonomously and 60-to-90-percent faster resolution times. Full reinvention isn't warranted everywhere: simple, standardized, repetitive workflows with limited variability (payroll, travel-expense approval, password resets) do fine with straightforward task automation, while complex, cross-functional, exception-prone processes tightly linked to business performance are where redesign delivers dramatically more value than merely speeding up what already exists. <span class="src">Seizing the agentic AI advantage</span>

Scaling this kind of transformation requires a genuinely new architecture paradigm McKinsey calls the "agentic AI mesh" — a composable, distributed, vendor-agnostic pattern built on five mutually reinforcing design principles: composability (any agent, tool, or LLM can plug into the mesh without system rework); distributed intelligence (tasks get decomposed and resolved across networks of cooperating agents rather than one monolithic system); layered decoupling (logic, memory, orchestration, and interface functions are kept separately modular to maximize flexibility); vendor neutrality (favoring open standards like MCP and Agent2Agent over proprietary protocols, so components can be independently updated or replaced as the technology matures without lock-in); and governed autonomy (agent behavior actively controlled through embedded policies, permissions, and escalation mechanisms, ensuring safe and transparent operation rather than uncontrolled sprawl). This architectural shift needs to be paired with a broader reset of the AI transformation approach itself, across four dimensions: strategy shifts from scattered, bottom-up tactical initiatives to strategic programs directly aligned with the organization's most critical priorities, with executives challenged to look beyond today's operating model toward entirely new revenue streams and competitive moats; the unit of transformation shifts from individual use cases to full business processes, reframing the guiding question from "where can I use AI in this function?" to "what would this function look like if agents ran 60 percent of it?"; the delivery model shifts from siloed, specialized AI centers of excellence — which reach real limits once agents are deeply embedded across complex, data-hungry business processes — to durable, cross-functional transformation squads combining business domain experts, process designers, AI/MLOps engineers, IT architects, and data engineers; and the implementation process shifts from experimentation to industrialized, scalable delivery designed for both technical robustness and economic sustainability from the outset, since unlike traditional IT systems (where annual run costs typically represent just 10 to 20 percent of initial build costs), gen AI solutions at scale can incur recurring costs that actually exceed the initial build investment if cost discipline isn't designed in from the start. Four enablers support this reset across people (fostering a "human + agent" mindset through training and internal champions, while introducing new roles like prompt engineers, agent orchestrators, and human-in-the-loop designers), governance (formal frameworks defining agent autonomy levels, decision boundaries, behavior monitoring, and audit mechanisms, with agents classified by function and matched to an appropriate oversight model), technology architecture (evolving from LLM-centric setups toward the agentic mesh, with an eye toward eventually reshuffling enterprise systems entirely around agents), and data (transitioning from bespoke, use-case-specific pipelines toward reusable data products, with governance extended to unstructured data). Because this pivot cannot be delegated, McKinsey frames it as squarely a CEO-led mandate built on three actions: formally concluding the experimentation phase (reviewing lessons learned, retiring unscalable pilots, refocusing on high-impact strategic domains); redesigning AI governance and the operating model around a strategic AI council spanning business, HR, data, and IT leadership with real value-tracking mechanisms tied to business KPIs; and launching a first "lighthouse" transformation project in parallel with building the underlying agentic technology foundation (infrastructure, data quality, governance frameworks, workforce readiness) needed to scale beyond it. <span class="src">Seizing the agentic AI advantage</span>

The companion piece translates this strategic argument into a concrete, phased CEO roadmap, opening by acknowledging real market skepticism — Gartner has predicted more than 40 percent of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027, and the current period has been characterized as a "trough of disillusionment" — while arguing this very disillusionment is precisely the opportunity for decisive leaders to jump ahead of hesitant competitors. Early implementations already show measurable value at real scale: harnessing agents for IT modernization work can accelerate project timelines 40 to 50 percent and cut costs more than 40 percent while improving output quality; a universal bank facing a large IT-project backlog and a shortage of skilled developers started with just three engineers and built a tech-modernization "agent factory" of 100 agents supervised by only five humans, executing the entire modernization lifecycle from reverse engineering through building new applications and cutting time and labor costs more than 50 percent; and McKinsey's broader experience suggests agents supporting people and automating tasks can drive 3 to 5 percent in annual company-level productivity gains initially, rising to 10 percent or more as agent teams take on genuinely complex workflows. Understanding what specific agents can actually do requires moving past loose, human-like framing toward a concrete capability spectrum. "Agentic labor" covers agents functioning as tools that support existing work: individual-augmentation tools (drafting, summarizing, coding, research) that are fast becoming a routine "cost of doing business" much like email or spreadsheets, delivering 20-to-30-percent higher personal throughput on single tasks, though broad horizontal deployment rarely translates into major business impact and usage tends to taper off beyond lead users without deliberate change management (embedding tools into standard procedures, integrating expected usage into performance management, proper training, visible leadership role-modeling); and task/workflow automation, where an agentic execution layer sits atop existing processes with only minor changes to them, delivering 20-to-40-percent faster cycle times or lower handling costs for repetitive transactional work (near-full automation of simple contact-center transactions like balance checks), though these domain-specific deployments remain constrained by operating largely in isolation, requiring significant human intervention to actually scale at volume and quality. "Agentic engine" describes the higher-value tier, made possible by breakthroughs in multi-agent teamwork: functional agentic workflows redesign a single domain (financial planning and reporting, for instance) fully around teams of AI agents — rethinking task order, merging steps, incorporating new data sources, and eliminating the fragmented handoffs that hamper current processes — with vendors already building full-stack, agent-native applications across customer care, finance, and supply-chain planning, and estimated call-center impact of automating 60 to 80 percent of incoming requests with comparable or better satisfaction than current systems; and cross-functional agentic systems tackle complex, high-level-decisioning workflows spanning entire customer journeys — 24/7 field-service dispatch, autonomous insurance claim adjudication, seconds-fast mortgage underwriting — with early pilots showing 70-to-80-percent cost-per-transaction reductions for labor-heavy processes, though at this level the binding constraints shift from technology to organization and operating model, requiring direct CEO and board involvement to rearchitect leadership and team responsibilities that have historically lived inside siloed corporate functions, since incremental change won't suffice here. McKinsey's hypothetical two-to-three-year roadmap sequences this progression concretely. Years one and two focus on building understanding, momentum, and foundational capability: agent "fluency" spreading to 25-to-50-percent-plus of employees regularly using enterprise agents (with a goal that all employees interrogate data conversationally rather than simply reading static reports); first-generation tools automating a broad range of existing processes (targeted cases like structured data-quality fixes reaching 90-to-95-percent resolution); early agentic integration into core systems, shifting interfaces from static commands toward prompt-based querying; a front-runner team launching a "lighthouse" project to redesign one complete end-to-end process from the ground up (with bold targets, such as automating more than 70 percent of an order-to-cash process); and softening demand for certain roles as productivity rises, particularly in coding, where the latest agents can boost productivity 50 to 100 percent. Supporting these markers requires CEOs to architect the transformation for genuine value (moving teams from isolated use cases to cross-functional priority workflows, with enterprise-wide learning centrally captured and codified into a reusable agent-first redesign playbook) led by a dedicated central "agent factory" team responsible for identifying, redesigning, and scaling priority workflows; to scale the transformation through an organizational mandate for composable rather than monolithic agents (echoing Amazon's 2002 API-exposure mandate as the template for enforced design discipline), with the agent factory building reusable blueprints, guardrails, performance-evaluation systems, and standardized KPIs while exercising strong governance to prevent uncontrolled sprawl, and CEOs prioritizing vendor-agnostic architecture (potentially an agentic AI mesh for higher-investment-appetite companies), a platform for automating agent management and observability, and systems that build genuine user trust in how agents make decisions; and to shift talent and operating models toward a human-agent hybrid where every employee — not a specialized few — develops the skills to build, train, task, and supervise agents, with performance management and KPIs recalibrated around agent-management effectiveness specifically. Years two and three shift focus from activity measures to P&L impact: the first lighthouse process hits a tipping point at greater than 90-percent automation; agentic automation scales across roughly 90 percent of key value streams, becoming the standard operating model rather than a system upgrade; traditional siloed operating models begin visibly eroding as cross-functional human-agent teams (and even agent-to-agent interactions crossing organizational boundaries) manage entire customer journeys; agent adoption exceeds 75 percent across all key functions, with most professionals managing several agents and advanced users juggling dozens; and the ratio of agents to full-time employees shifts substantially, with software-development-lifecycle headcount needs potentially dropping 30 to 40 percent and financial-planning-and-reporting workload potentially reduced by 75 percent. At this stage CEOs must reimagine the business model itself — questioning what remains genuinely defensible in an agentic world, using agents operating in digital-twin environments to test new processes and offers cheaply and at scale, and reworking budgets and workforce-capability models jointly with the CFO and CHRO as investment shifts meaningfully from labor toward technology — while reorganizing around outcome-based value streams rather than legacy functional structures, and working closely with HR to redesign career structures, incentives, and reporting systems (introducing new role archetypes like "agent orchestrator" and "agent trainer") for a workforce McKinsey research suggests could see up to 30 percent of current worked hours automated by 2030. <span class="src">The change agent Goals, decisions, and implications for CEOs in the agentic age</span>

EY's companion diagnosis, opening its own "Agentic Enterprise" thought-leadership series, arrives at the same underlying conclusion as McKinsey's gen AI paradox through a different lens: enterprise architecture rather than technology maturity. EY finds up to 75% of enterprise AI value creation potential is trapped not within individual functions but between them, in the coordination friction of decision-making delay, approval latency, analysis duplication, and fragmented accountability — because ERP, CRM, HRIS, and PLM systems were designed decades ago to optimize vertical functional execution and control, while value itself flows horizontally through cross-functional processes like concept-to-launch, order-to-cash, and service incident-to-resolution. A recent automotive OEM transformation illustrates the scale: of more than $2.4 billion in total AI- and digital-enabled value creation potential identified, nearly $2 billion came specifically from enterprise value streams rather than functional optimization, and the OEM's incident-to-resolution process, spanning eight functions, spent 15-25% of its total effort on internal coordination rather than solving the customer's actual problem. Traditional AI initiatives fail to address this because they inherit the same functional boundaries as the organization itself; EY identifies five specific barriers — AI optimizing functions rather than enterprise value flow, AI wrapped around existing (rather than redesigned) processes, fragmented enterprise data and systems, the absence of a shared intelligence and organizational-context layer ("the most consequential barrier — and arguably the least understood"), and governance/operating models that haven't evolved to match AI's pace. EY's proposed remedy, the "Agentic Enterprise," layers seven interconnected components: business/customer outcomes at the top, key business decisions (where humans remain embedded for judgment and regulatory oversight), processes and workflows, an agent orchestration layer coordinating actions across functions, an intelligence/context layer built from semantic models, ontologies, and knowledge graphs that gives agents shared organizational meaning, enterprise systems of record (ERP/CRM/HRIS, whose role shifts from workflow logic toward pure record-keeping), and foundational infrastructure — with the intelligence layer positioned as the precondition for coherent cross-functional orchestration, since without shared context agents can analyze data in isolation but cannot coordinate decisions coherently across silos. For CIOs and CFOs, EY argues this shifts the technology investment thesis toward less customization and more flexibility as core enterprise systems stabilize into systems of record; for CEOs and boards, it introduces new leadership constructs — Value Stream Leaders, Agent Owners, and Enterprise Orchestrators — as governance shifts from siloed oversight toward continuous, real-time enterprise steering. <span class="src">How agentic AI can unlock enterprise value at scale</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither article addresses how the "agent factory" central team should be resourced or structured relative to the cross-functional transformation squads also recommended — whether the factory is a superset of those squads, a separate enabling function, or something else.
- The roadmap's markers (e.g., 90%+ automation of a lighthouse process by year two or three) are presented as aggressive but achievable targets; the articles acknowledge much will change over the timeline but don't address what a realistic range of outcomes looks like for organizations that fall short of these markers.
- The agentic transformation office is a forward-looking design for 2030, and the source gives no evidence from deployed implementations.
