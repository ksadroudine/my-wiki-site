## Summary

Enterprises are adopting generative AI and AI agents widely, yet most still report little earnings impact because deployments remain shallow, fragmented and layered on unchanged processes. Consulting research from McKinsey, EY, BCG and PwC argues that value comes from reinventing end-to-end processes around agents, supported by a composable architecture, an intelligence layer, an agent factory, CEO-led governance and a phased roadmap that moves from agent fluency to P&L impact. The main barriers are organizational rather than technical, including trust in high-stakes uses, change readiness, orchestration across vendors and agent sprawl, as Walmart's consolidation of dozens of agents into four super agents illustrates.

## Concept

Enterprise adoption of generative AI has reached a paradox. McKinsey reports that more than 78% of companies use gen AI in at least one function, up from 55% a year earlier, yet more than 80% report no material contribution to earnings and only 1% consider their strategy mature. The paradox traces to an imbalance between two kinds of deployment. Horizontal tools such as enterprise copilots, used by nearly 70% of the Fortune 500 through Microsoft 365 Copilot, scale quickly because they are off-the-shelf and need no workflow redesign, and much early adoption was driven by the wish to offer secure alternatives to external tools, but their gains spread thinly across individuals and stay invisible in financial results. Vertical use cases embedded in specific functions have far higher potential, yet fewer than 10% of deployed ones escape pilot mode, and even deployed ones usually support isolated steps and act only when a human prompts them. <span class="src">Seizing the agentic AI advantage</span>

Six factors explain the limited vertical scaling. Initiatives are fragmented and bottom-up, with fewer than 30% of companies reporting direct CEO sponsorship of their AI agenda. Mature packaged solutions are lacking, so teams build custom without the MLOps talent to industrialize them. First-generation LLMs produce inaccurate output, are passive unless prompted, handle multi-step branching logic poorly and have limited persistent memory. AI centers of excellence prototype fast but operate in silos disconnected from core IT, data and business functions. Unstructured data is poorly governed, and cultural apprehension and inertia rooted in fear of disruption slow adoption. <span class="src">Seizing the agentic AI advantage</span>

AI agents are positioned as the answer because they extend LLMs with memory, planning, orchestration and integration, so they can understand goals, decompose them into subtasks, interact with people and systems, act and adapt in real time, which moves gen AI from reactive content generation to autonomous execution of multistep workflows. They change operations in five ways, by accelerating execution through parallel processing, adapting as data arrives, personalizing decisions to individual context, flexing capacity with demand and increasing resilience by monitoring disruptions and rerouting autonomously. They can also amplify existing revenue through real-time upsell and cross-sell, or create new revenue, for example by embedding agents in connected products for pay-per-use monetization or packaging internal expertise in legal reasoning, tax interpretation or procurement as agent-based SaaS and API products. <span class="src">Seizing the agentic AI advantage</span>

Deployments already show the range of results. A large bank's hybrid digital factory, in which humans supervise squads of agents that document legacy code, write new code, review each other and integrate features, cut time and effort by more than 50% for early-adopter teams in a USD 600 million, 400-application modernization. A market research firm's multiagent system that finds data anomalies and explains market-share shifts, replacing a 500-person manual process in which clients caught 80% of errors, showed over 60% potential productivity gains and more than USD 3 million of annual savings, and a retail bank's AI-assisted credit-risk memo process drawing on at least ten sources showed 20-60% productivity gains including 30% faster credit turnaround. Agents for IT modernization can speed timelines 40-50% and cut costs more than 40%, and a universal bank that started with three engineers built an agent factory of 100 agents supervised by five humans, cutting time and labor costs by more than 50%. <span class="src">Seizing the agentic AI advantage</span> <span class="src">The change agent Goals, decisions, and implications for CEOs in the agentic age</span>

The central lesson is that value requires reinventing processes around agents rather than inserting agents into legacy workflows. In a hypothetical call center, gen AI assisting human agents inside an unchanged manual process yields 5-10% productivity gains, adding agents at specific steps such as classifying tickets, proposing resolutions and resolving simple cases yields 20-40% time savings and 30-50% backlog reduction but remains capped by coordination friction, and only reimagining the process around agent autonomy, with agents detecting issues, starting resolution and talking to customers while humans handle escalations and quality oversight, resolves up to 80% of incidents autonomously and speeds resolution by 60-90%. Full reinvention is not warranted everywhere, since simple, standardized, low-variability workflows such as payroll, travel-expense approval or password resets do well with straightforward automation, whereas complex, cross-functional, exception-prone processes tied to business performance gain dramatically from redesign. <span class="src">Seizing the agentic AI advantage</span>

McKinsey's second piece gives a capability spectrum for what agents can do. At the agentic labor end, individual-augmentation tools for drafting, summarizing, coding and research are becoming a routine cost of doing business and raise personal throughput by 20-30%, yet broad deployment rarely produces major business impact and usage tapers beyond lead users without change management such as embedding tools in procedures, building usage into performance management, training and visible leadership role-modeling. Task and workflow automation puts an agentic execution layer on top of existing processes and delivers 20-40% faster cycle times for repetitive transactional work, though isolated deployments need heavy human intervention to scale. The agentic engine tier is made possible by multi-agent teamwork, with functional agentic workflows that redesign an entire domain such as financial planning around teams of agents, and cross-functional systems spanning customer journeys such as field-service dispatch, insurance claim adjudication and mortgage underwriting, whose early pilots show 70-80% lower cost per transaction for labor-heavy processes. At that level the binding constraints are organizational and require direct CEO and board involvement to rearchitect responsibilities that have lived in siloed functions. <span class="src">The change agent Goals, decisions, and implications for CEOs in the agentic age</span>

Scaling requires a new architecture and a reset of the transformation approach. McKinsey's agentic AI mesh is composable, so any agent, tool or LLM can plug in without rework, distributed, so tasks are decomposed across cooperating agents, layered, so logic, memory, orchestration and interface stay modular, vendor-neutral through open standards such as the Model Context Protocol and Agent2Agent, and governed through embedded policies, permissions and escalation that prevent uncontrolled sprawl. The reset has four dimensions. Strategy moves from scattered bottom-up initiatives to programs aligned with the organization's critical priorities, the unit of transformation moves from use cases to end-to-end processes by asking what a function would look like if agents ran 60% of it, the delivery model moves from siloed centers of excellence to durable cross-functional squads of domain experts, process designers, MLOps and data engineers and architects, and implementation moves from experimentation to industrialized delivery with cost discipline built in, because gen AI run costs at scale can exceed build costs, unlike the 10-20% ratio of traditional IT. Enablers span people, with a human-plus-agent mindset and new roles such as prompt engineers, agent orchestrators and human-in-the-loop designers, governance that defines autonomy levels, decision boundaries, monitoring and audit, architecture that evolves from LLM-centric setups toward the mesh, and data that shifts from bespoke pipelines to reusable data products with governed unstructured data. <span class="src">Seizing the agentic AI advantage</span>

Because the pivot cannot be delegated, McKinsey frames it as CEO-led, with three actions, which are formally ending the experimentation phase and retiring unscalable pilots, redesigning AI governance and the operating model around a strategic AI council spanning business, HR, data and IT with value tracking tied to business KPIs, and launching a first lighthouse transformation alongside the technology foundation of infrastructure, data quality, governance and workforce readiness. The companion piece sets out a hypothetical two-to-three-year roadmap, noting that Gartner predicts over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027 and that the current trough of disillusionment is an opportunity for decisive leaders. Years one and two build agent fluency, with 25-50% or more of employees regularly using agents, automate existing processes with first-generation tools, integrate agents into core systems, launch a lighthouse project redesigning one end-to-end process such as order-to-cash with targets of over 70% automation, and stand up a central agent factory that identifies and scales priority workflows, builds reusable blueprints, guardrails, evaluation systems and standardized KPIs, and enforces composable design in the spirit of Amazon's 2002 API mandate. <span class="src">Seizing the agentic AI advantage</span> <span class="src">The change agent Goals, decisions, and implications for CEOs in the agentic age</span>

Years two and three shift from activity measures to P&L impact. The lighthouse process passes 90% automation, agentic automation spreads across roughly 90% of key value streams, siloed operating models begin to erode as cross-functional human-agent teams manage entire customer journeys, adoption exceeds 75% across key functions with most professionals managing several agents, and agent-to-FTE ratios shift enough that software-development headcount needs may fall 30-40% and financial-planning workload 75%. CEOs must then reimagine the business model, ask what remains defensible, test new processes in digital-twin environments, rework budgets and workforce models with the CFO and CHRO as spending shifts from labor to technology, reorganize around outcome-based value streams and redesign careers and incentives with new roles such as agent orchestrator and agent trainer, for a workforce in which up to 30% of current hours could be automated by 2030. <span class="src">The change agent Goals, decisions, and implications for CEOs in the agentic age</span>

EY reaches a similar conclusion through enterprise architecture. It finds up to 75% of enterprise AI value is trapped between functions in coordination friction, meaning decision delay, approval latency, duplicated analysis and fragmented accountability, because ERP, CRM, HRIS and PLM systems were built decades ago to optimize vertical execution and control while value flows horizontally through processes such as concept-to-launch, order-to-cash and incident-to-resolution. At one automotive manufacturer almost USD 2 billion of more than USD 2.4 billion in identified AI and digital value came from enterprise value streams rather than functions, and an eight-function incident-to-resolution process spent 15-25% of its effort on internal coordination. EY names five barriers, which are AI that optimizes functions rather than value flow, AI wrapped around existing processes, fragmented data and systems, the absence of a shared intelligence and context layer described as the most consequential and least understood, and governance that has not kept pace. <span class="src">How agentic AI can unlock enterprise value at scale</span>

EY's remedy is the Agentic Enterprise, which layers business and customer outcomes, key decisions where humans stay embedded for judgment and regulatory oversight, processes and workflows, an agent orchestration layer, an intelligence layer of semantic models, ontologies and knowledge graphs that gives agents shared organizational meaning, systems of record that shift toward pure record-keeping, and infrastructure. The intelligence layer is the precondition for coherent coordination, since without shared context agents can analyze data in isolation but cannot coordinate decisions across silos. For CIOs and CFOs the investment thesis shifts toward less customization and more flexibility as core systems stabilize, and for CEOs and boards it introduces Value Stream Leaders, Agent Owners and Enterprise Orchestrators as governance moves toward continuous, real-time steering. BCG applies agentic AI to the function that runs change itself, describing a transformation office, now constrained by tracking, report compilation and manual risk spotting, that by 2030 automates governance, updates and issue detection, forecasts value from portfolio and ERP data, and targets change management with real-time sentiment and role-specific coaching, with advice to start from a clear mandate and stretch targets, separate human-led from AI-augmented roles, and build learning loops. BCG warns that positioning AI as a substitute for human roles dilutes accountability and review quality and recommends minimum viable governance on what AI can draft, what needs human review and what stays a human decision. <span class="src">How agentic AI can unlock enterprise value at scale</span> <span class="src">The AI-Powered Transformation Office</span>

PwC's May 2025 survey of 300 senior US executives shows adoption that is accelerating on the surface but shallow underneath. Some 88% say their team plans to increase AI budgets because of agentic AI, with over a quarter planning rises of 26% or more, 79% say agents are already being adopted and two-thirds of adopters report measurable productivity value, with more than half also reporting savings, faster decisions or better customer experience. But much of the activity is employees using agentic features built into existing applications to speed routine tasks, and 68% say half or fewer of their employees interact with agents in everyday work, while only 17% report near-full adoption across almost all workflows, in contrast with Wyndham running coordinated agent teams across functions. About 46% worry that their company is losing ground to competitors, yet few are building operating models to orchestrate agents across vendors and functions, and only 45% of adopters are rethinking their operating model, 42% are redesigning processes around agents and 44% are developing new agentic products, even though half expect their operating model to be unrecognizable within two years. <span class="src">PwC’s AI Agent Survey</span>

PwC's central finding is that the barrier is human rather than technical. The challenges executives rank highest, cybersecurity and cost at 34% each, are in PwC's view largely solvable, since agents can be secured and good implementations pay for themselves within months, while the real obstacles are connecting agents across applications and workflows at 19%, organizational change readiness at 17% and employee adoption at 14%. Some 67% of executives expect agents to transform existing roles within 12 months and 48% expect headcount to rise rather than fall, so adoption is a redesign challenge to be led, not just resistance to overcome. Trust falls with stakes, from 38% for data analysis, 35% for performance improvement and 31% for daily collaboration with human teammates to 20% for financial transactions and 22% for autonomous employee interactions, which makes responsible AI a condition for high-stakes use. Among the 18% of companies not using agents, the top reason is no clear use case, which PwC calls a failure of vision, citing a retailer that began with software-development cycle times and halved production errors before expanding to HR, finance, supply chain and marketing. Its playbook is to get off the bench, rethink offense and defense since agents erode longstanding moats while opening new ones, focus on people by reimagining work and roles, orchestrate and integrate through something like an operating system that coordinates agents from multiple vendors, and design trust in from the start. <span class="src">PwC’s AI Agent Survey</span>

Walmart illustrates the orchestration problem that PwC finds beneath the more commonly cited excuses. It built dozens of agents, each reached through its own interface in a different system, until things became confusing, and its chief technology and development officer Suresh Kumar says users should not have to remember one agent for payroll and another for merchandising trends. Walmart consolidated the portfolio into four super agents, each fronted by one interface that taps many agents behind it, namely Sparky for customers, already live, Marty for suppliers, expected within months and eventually handling purchase analytics and advertising campaigns, and separate employee and engineering agents expected within the year, with the employee agent eventually able to act, for instance by verifying discount-card eligibility and issuing a replacement. The agents are connected through the Model Context Protocol, the open standard Anthropic introduced in late 2024, and older agents built before it was widely adopted are being retrofitted to conform. The logic is adoption, since scattered agents were unintuitive and a simplified interface is expected to raise usage, and it is backed by leadership hires, an Instacart executive as head of global AI acceleration reporting to the CEO and a dedicated AI platforms leader, with US division CEO John Furner framing the stakes as both top-line growth through more personalized shopping and bottom-line savings in supply chain and inventory. <span class="src">Exclusive Why Walmart Is Overhauling Its Approach to AI Agents</span>
