## Summary

The seven cloud migration strategies — rehost, relocate, replatform, refactor, repurchase, retire, retain. Traces their evolution from Gartner's original 5 Rs through AWS's additions, when each strategy actually fits a given workload, Bain's rightsizing methodology for avoiding the cost overruns a naive rehost commonly produces, and Microsoft's broader taxonomy of data migration types the 7 Rs sit within.

## Concept

The 7 Rs framework provides a menu of distinct migration strategies rather than a single universal migration path, on the premise that different workloads warrant fundamentally different approaches depending on their complexity, business criticality, and the organization's tolerance for cost, risk, and disruption. The framework itself has evolved through three stages as cloud computing matured: Gartner introduced the original 5 Rs early in the cloud computing era, when organizations were first grappling with how to think about moving legacy applications; AWS subsequently added a sixth strategy, Retire, formalizing the idea that migration planning should start by asking whether an existing application still delivers ongoing value at all, rather than assuming every workload deserves a migration path; and AWS's current 7 Rs model added Retain, explicitly acknowledging that not every application either can or should move to the cloud, giving organizations a legitimate way to keep certain systems on-premises as a deliberate strategic choice rather than an oversight. <span class="src">The 7 Rs of Cloud Migration 7 Strategies Explained</span>

The seven strategies span a spectrum from minimal to maximal change. Rehost ("lift and shift") redeploys an on-premises application and its dependencies onto cloud IaaS as-is, matching existing storage, networking, and compute characteristics without changing the underlying architecture — it is the easiest strategy to execute, requires the least in-house cloud-native expertise, and carries the lowest migration risk and disruption, at the cost of limited access to cloud-native capabilities like autoscaling once the workload is actually in the cloud. Relocate ("hypervisor-level lift and shift") is described as the newest addition to the model, migrating an entire collection of servers from an on-premises platform like VMware or Kubernetes directly to a managed cloud equivalent of that same platform (e.g. Amazon EKS or Google GKE), without rewriting source code, acquiring new hardware, or disrupting ongoing operations for connected clients — it's the fastest of all seven strategies and requires minimal staff retraining, but because the configuration and architecture carry over largely unchanged, it caps how much scalability and cloud-native benefit is actually unlocked, and makes it hard to scale resources back down for cost savings later. Replatform ("lift and reshape") sits between the two: it moves an application to the cloud while applying some degree of platform-level optimization to take advantage of specific cloud-native capabilities, while leaving the application's core source code and architecture untouched — this lets organizations selectively modernize particular components for maximum ROI without committing to a full rebuild, saving time and cost relative to a full refactor while still unlocking meaningfully more agility and automation than a pure rehost. Refactor ("re-architect") is the most complex and highest-investment of the seven, rebuilding a workload from the ground up specifically to support cloud-native capabilities like serverless computing, autoscaling, and distributed load balancing — often by breaking a monolithic application apart into microservices to achieve high availability and automation levels that are difficult to reach with more conservative strategies. It is considered the most future-proof option and typically yields substantially lower operating costs than the legacy architecture it replaces once complete, but it demands the largest upfront investment, extensive staff training and cloud expertise, continuous monitoring for cost optimization, and is explicitly not recommended for migrating a large number of applications simultaneously given its complexity. Repurchase ("drop and shop") swaps an internally managed system for an equivalent third-party managed service, typically a SaaS subscription — this converts fixed internal IT costs into a consumption-based, pay-as-you-go model tied more directly to actual usage and business revenue, reduces the operational burden of managing infrastructure in-house, and quickly unlocks cloud-native capabilities without the organization having to build them itself, but can become expensive for low-usage applications given SaaS baseline pricing, and cedes control over the pace of updates and feature releases to the vendor's own cadence rather than the organization's own priorities. Retire terminates or downsizes applications no longer delivering meaningful production value, typically as an early step in modernizing away from inefficient legacy frameworks — it requires the least investment of cost, time, and effort of any of the seven strategies and directly eliminates ongoing IT spend on idle resources, but carries a real risk if a workload is retired prematurely or without first confirming that no other part of the technology stack still depends on it, since that can create unplanned incompatibilities elsewhere. Retain keeps a workload in its current, non-cloud environment rather than migrating it at all, typically chosen for one of several concrete reasons: the workload depends on another application that must be migrated first and can't sensibly move ahead of it, there's no immediate business case for migrating it, the vendor is expected to eventually release a SaaS version that would make a custom migration unnecessary, or compliance and security requirements mandate the workload stay in a local data center — Retain is particularly relevant for organizations pursuing hybrid cloud specifically, and helps identify which workloads genuinely need immediate migration versus which can reasonably be revisited later, though it also risks becoming a permanent excuse to avoid otherwise beneficial modernization if not periodically reassessed. <span class="src">The 7 Rs of Cloud Migration 7 Strategies Explained</span>

Choosing among these seven strategies in practice means matching each specific workload's complexity, cost sensitivity, and tolerance for migration-phase disruption to the strategy best suited to it — an organization migrating a large application portfolio will typically apply several different Rs simultaneously across different parts of that portfolio, rather than committing to a single strategy company-wide, since the tradeoffs (investment required, resulting flexibility, resulting cost) differ so substantially between, for instance, a quick rehost of a stable legacy system and a full refactor of a strategically important, high-usage application. <span class="src">The 7 Rs of Cloud Migration 7 Strategies Explained</span>

A separate Bain analysis warns that the easiest of the seven strategies, rehost, is also the one most likely to disappoint on cost if executed carelessly. In a Bain survey of more than 350 IT decision makers, the single most common complaint about cloud migration was that expected cost savings simply hadn't materialized — and in some cases costs actually rose, with direct-match migration of an overprovisioned on-premises workload running 10 to 15 percent more expensive in the cloud than leaving it in place, since most existing workloads already carry substantial excess computing and storage capacity that a pure lift-and-shift simply relocates rather than eliminates. The fix is treating migration as an opportunity to rationalize workloads rather than replicate them as-is: assessing usage patterns and peak computing demand to make informed downsizing decisions, identifying storage patterns and proximity requirements, and rationalizing core-processor counts to reduce processor-tied software licensing costs — a process that commonly surfaces significant fragmentation, duplication, incomplete records, and even "zombie servers" (machines no longer needed that were never decommissioned). Executed well, this rightsizing can cut costs by 30 to 60 percent instead of increasing them; one analysis by TSO Logic (an AWS subsidiary) found that a company with 105,000 server instances and 20 terabytes of on-premises storage would have seen a 22 percent cost increase from a direct-match rehost, but a 36 percent cost reduction from rightsizing the same workloads before migrating. Savings are also uneven by workload type: nonproduction workloads benefit more from migration than production workloads, since their more volatile computing usage better matches the elasticity the cloud provides, and nonvirtualized workloads benefit more than workloads that are already virtualized, since virtualization has already captured some of the utilization gains a cloud migration would otherwise unlock. The practical implication for choosing among the 7 Rs is that "rehost" and "poorly executed rehost" are not the same strategy — the framework's easiest option still requires real workload analysis to actually deliver on its promised simplicity and cost benefit, rather than being a safe default that avoids the work altogether. <span class="src">Optimize, Don’t ‘Lift and Shift,’ When Migrating to the Cloud</span>

<span class="src">What Is Data Migration</span> (Microsoft, undated Azure glossary entry) situates the 7 Rs within a broader taxonomy of data migration generally, of which cloud migration is only one category. Microsoft defines data migration simply as moving digital information — to a different location, file format, environment, storage system, database, datacenter, or application — and breaks the practice into five broad, overlapping categories: storage migration (the most basic type, moving data between storage devices, including to the cloud), database migration (moving to an updated or different database engine, typically requiring data conversion since format usually changes too), application migration (moving an application to a new computing environment, itself often combining a database migration and a storage migration), cloud migration (the specific category this concept's 7 Rs framework addresses), and business process migration (moving data to better manage or operate the business itself, such as during a merger or new product launch). The source draws a definitional distinction directly relevant to any of the 7 Rs strategies that involve reformatting data along the way (most obviously Refactor and Repurchase): migration means moving data from one place to another, while conversion means transforming data from one format to another so a new system can actually read it — migration projects commonly require conversion as one step within them, but the two terms are not interchangeable, and a project can include both without the sequencing being obvious in advance. Microsoft also outlines a standard three-phase migration process — planning (discovering and mapping existing data, assessing organizational impact, and designing the migration approach and rate), implementing (building the migration solution and transferring data while monitoring for quality problems, duplicates, and anomalies), and validating (auditing the migrated data for accuracy before decommissioning the old system) — that generalizes the practical execution steps underlying any of the seven cloud-specific strategies this concept catalogs, regardless of which one is chosen for a given workload. <span class="src">What Is Data Migration</span>

## Open Questions

- The source does not provide guidance on how to sequence a large migration project across the 7 Rs when many applications qualify for more than one strategy, beyond the general framing that workload complexity and business case should drive the choice.
- The Microsoft glossary entry doesn't specify which of the five data-migration categories or which of the 7 Rs strategies most commonly require the highest degree of data conversion in practice, leaving the interaction between the two frameworks somewhat abstract rather than empirically grounded.
