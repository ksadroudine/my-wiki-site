## Summary

Findings from a large BCG/Harvard field experiment — AI dramatically improves consultant performance and levels skill differences, but also causes measurable overreliance on tasks outside the AI's "jagged frontier" of capability. Covers the Centaur/Cyborg framework for working with AI without falling into that trap.

## Highlights

- In a pre-registered field experiment with 758 BCG consultants (7% of the firm's consulting force) across 18 realistic tasks, consultants using GPT-4 completed 12.2% more tasks, finished 25.1% faster, and produced 40% higher-quality output than consultants without AI access — effects consistent across every dimension measured, by both human and AI graders who agreed with each other. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>
- AI access functioned as a skill leveler: the lowest-scoring consultants (per a pre-experiment baseline assessment) saw the largest performance jump, 43%, while top performers still improved but by less — a pattern the source compares to how the steam shovel eliminated the productivity gap between good and bad diggers. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>
- The "Jagged Frontier" is the metaphor for AI capability being unevenly distributed across tasks that look equally difficult to a human: AI excels at some unexpectedly hard-seeming tasks (idea generation) and fails at some unexpectedly easy-seeming ones (writing an exactly-50-word poem, because it operates on tokens rather than words) — and because the boundary is invisible, users can't tell which side of it a given task falls on without extensive hands-on experience. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>
- On a task specifically designed to fall outside the AI's frontier (using one of GPT-4's known blind spots to produce a wrong but convincing answer), unaided consultants got the answer right 84% of the time, but consultants using AI on that same task did *worse*, dropping to 60-70% accuracy — direct evidence that overreliance on an authoritative-sounding AI can degrade performance below the unaided baseline, not just fail to improve it. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>
- A separate cited study found recruiters using high-quality AI became measurably lazier and less careful in their own judgment — missing strong applicants and making worse hiring decisions than recruiters using low-quality or no AI — a phenomenon termed "falling asleep at the wheel," where sufficiently good AI removes the incentive for humans to stay attentive. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>
- Two navigable patterns for working across the jagged frontier are proposed: Centaur work (a clear division of labor, switching between wholly-human and wholly-AI tasks based on which side of the frontier each falls on) and Cyborg work (deep, interleaved integration, handing off partial tasks — like starting a sentence for the AI to complete — back and forth rather than dividing whole tasks). <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>
- AI-assisted output, while higher quality on average, was also found to be more homogeneous across consultants than unaided human output — a specific tradeoff the source argues Centaur and Cyborg approaches help offset, since actively combining human and AI work produces more varied results than either working alone. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>

## Concept

This concept summarizes a large, pre-registered field experiment — described as the largest such study of the future of professional work at the time — conducted jointly by a multidisciplinary team of social scientists (from Harvard, Warwick, MIT, and Wharton) and Boston Consulting Group, using 758 BCG consultants (7% of the firm's total consulting force) across 18 realistic consulting tasks: creative tasks (proposing shoe ideas for an underserved market), analytical tasks (market segmentation), writing/marketing tasks (press releases), and persuasiveness tasks (an inspirational internal memo). Consultants were randomly assigned access to plain GPT-4 (no special fine-tuning or prompting, the same version publicly available at the time) or no AI access, with performance measured on task completion rate, speed, and quality, graded by both human and AI raters who were found to agree closely with each other. The headline result was unambiguous and consistent across every measurement dimension: AI-assisted consultants completed 12.2% more tasks, finished 25.1% faster, and produced 40% higher-quality output than the control group. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>

A distinct and separately important finding was that AI access functions as a skill leveler: consultants who scored lowest on a pre-experiment baseline assessment saw the largest performance gains from AI access (a 43% jump), while already-top-performing consultants still improved, but by a smaller margin. The source frames the implications of this pattern as under-examined: a technology that compresses the performance gap between weaker and stronger workers changes what skill differentiation even means in a given field, drawing an analogy to how the steam shovel eliminated the practical importance of being a "good" versus "bad" digger by hand. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>

The paper's central conceptual contribution is the "Jagged Frontier" — a metaphor for the fact that AI capability is not smoothly distributed across tasks of apparently similar difficulty. Visualized as an irregular fortress wall (some capability "towers" jut far out, others fold back close to the center), tasks that look equally hard to a human observer can fall on opposite sides of this invisible boundary: GPT-4 handles open-ended idea generation — a task humans might expect to be hard for a machine — with ease, while it reliably fails at producing a poem of exactly 50 words, a seemingly trivial constraint, because the model operates over tokens rather than words and has no native concept of a precise word count. Because the boundary itself is invisible and doesn't track intuitive difficulty, the only way users can learn where it lies for their own work is through extensive hands-on use — there is no instruction manual, and the paper's authors state plainly that no one, including the model's own developers, knows the full range of a frontier model's capabilities in advance. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>

The experiment included one task specifically engineered to fall outside GPT-4's frontier, deliberately designed to exploit a known blind spot so the model would produce a wrong but superficially convincing answer — a task the researchers note was surprisingly difficult to construct precisely because GPT-4 proved capable enough to solve most attempted "outside the frontier" tasks correctly. Unaided consultants got this specific problem right 84% of the time; consultants who used AI on the same problem did *worse*, dropping to 60-70% accuracy, despite still writing up their (wrong) answers with higher-quality prose than the unaided group. This documents a direct overreliance mechanism, and the source connects it to a related study (by one of the same co-authors) finding that recruiters using high-quality AI became measurably lazier and less attentive in their own hiring judgment — missing genuinely strong candidates and making worse decisions overall than recruiters using either low-quality AI or no AI, a dynamic termed "falling asleep at the wheel": when the AI is good enough to seem consistently trustworthy, humans lose the practical incentive to stay critically engaged and start deferring to its authoritative-sounding output even on tasks where it's actually wrong. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>

Two navigable patterns are proposed for capturing AI's benefits (speed, quality, skill-leveling) while avoiding this overreliance failure mode. Centaur work maintains a clear division of labor between person and machine — analogous to the clean boundary between a mythical centaur's human torso and horse body — strategically routing entire tasks to whichever party (human or AI) is stronger at that specific task, based on where it falls relative to the jagged frontier; the source's own example is deciding on statistical technique personally while delegating graph production to the AI. Cyborg work instead blends human and machine effort at a much finer grain, interleaving contributions within a single task rather than dividing whole tasks between them — for example, having the AI complete a sentence a human started, moving back and forth across the frontier repeatedly within one piece of work, which the source describes as the pattern it recommends specifically for AI-assisted writing. Becoming either a Centaur or a Cyborg is described as neither difficult nor exotic: it emerges naturally from sustained hands-on use, since repeated exposure to a wide range of tasks is what lets a person start to perceive the actual shape of the jagged frontier for their own work, rather than guessing at it in the abstract. A further, separate finding motivating both approaches is that AI-generated output, while higher quality on average than unaided human output, was also found to be more homogeneous in aggregate across different consultants — actively combining human and AI effort (rather than relying on either alone) is presented as a way to recover variety alongside quality and correctness. <span class="src">Centaurs and Cyborgs on the Jagged Frontier</span>
