## Summary

Anthropic's Claude Code and Cowork have made vibe coding, which means building working software through natural-language conversation rather than hand-written code, accessible to non-engineers and small businesses. Small companies are already building custom CRMs to replace or avoid enterprise software, and Bain argues that the industry is shifting from AI-assisted to AI-led development, in which the traditional boundary between product and software development breaks down.

## Concept

Anthropic's Claude Code and its January 2026 successor, Cowork, mark what the Financial Times and Bain both independently treat as an inflection point in how software gets built — the shift from AI as a coding assistant to AI as the primary author of working systems. Claude Code, launched in 2025, quickly became the industry benchmark for AI-generated code and reached $1 billion in revenue within six months; Anthropic reports that between 70% and 90% of code written across the company today, including roughly 90% of Claude Code's own underlying codebase, is itself AI-generated. Two technical choices underlie this lead. First, Anthropic pioneered "reinforcement learning from AI feedback" (RLAIF), a technique originally built to make models safer by having the model itself rate and critique its own outputs against human-set guiding principles; the company found this approach also functions as a way to automate model improvement at a scale that traditional human-labeled reinforcement learning (RLHF, which relies on expensive, time-consuming human raters) cannot match, and it has contributed directly to Claude's lead on independent coding benchmarks. Second, Anthropic developed and open-sourced the model context protocol (MCP), which bridges AI models to applications, databases, and workflows in real time and lets organizations plug Claude into existing systems like Slack with minimal friction. Cowork extends Claude Code's capability from professional developers to non-technical knowledge workers entirely: Anthropic's head of digital native businesses, Guillaume Princen, describes it as doing for knowledge workers what Claude Code did for developers, and the company's newly released open-source plug-ins extend this into specific functions like legal contract review, sales, finance, marketing, and customer support — prompting former Dropbox CTO Aditya Agarwal to declare "we will never ever write code by hand again." The FT frames the disruption risk as running well beyond software itself: legal-AI vendors like Harvey and Legora, and advertising agencies including WPP, Omnicom, and Publicis, were named among Barclays' "AI losers" in a survey of buy-side investors, on the theory that enterprises adopting Claude directly can build their own lightweight tools rather than pay for a dedicated vertical AI product — though rival vendors point out their more specialized platforms still outperform a general-purpose plug-in on tasks tailored to an individual firm's needs. Experts interviewed by the FT flag two enduring risks in this shift regardless of model quality: AI models still frequently hallucinate, and a looming "comprehension debt" risk, in which junior developers grow too reliant on AI tools to retain the ability to understand and debug what has gone wrong when something breaks — a risk with outsized consequences in accuracy-critical, highly regulated fields like banking and law. <span class="src">How Anthropic achieved AI coding breakthroughs — and rattled business</span>

That same capability shift is already visible concretely in the small-and-midsize business software market, where "vibe coding" — building software through natural-language conversation with an AI tool rather than by writing code directly — is beginning to displace off-the-shelf enterprise software for companies whose needs don't fit a generic template well. The Wall Street Journal profiles several: water-treatment company CarboNet abandoned Salesforce after extensive customization still failed to deliver the exact workflow its sales team needed, and instead built a custom CRM with the help of startup OverAI (whose "AI Architect" tool builds systems in natural language while pulling in prebuilt components like permissioning and security alerts) for $15,000-$20,000 to build and roughly $5,000 a year to maintain; meat distributor Whetstone Distribution built a tool marrying its sales and operations data into a visual map for about $10,000 over a few weeks, explicitly to avoid a system that would consume a large share of its budget and lock it into a multiyear contract; and early-stage venture fund Crux Capital built a custom CRM to handle its genuinely unusual "customer" relationship — the startups it tracks as potential investments over multiyear horizons — because no off-the-shelf SaaS product fit that use case. Analysts are careful to distinguish the size of the threat by company scale: BNP Paribas's global head of software research, Stefan Slowinski, calls the market panic among software-stock investors overblown, since no complex multinational is likely to abandon an entrenched system like Salesforce anytime soon, but argues the more realistic risk to incumbents is indirect — a proliferation of new, nimble AI-powered vendors could hand customers negotiating leverage and put sustained pricing pressure on established players even without mass defection. Salesforce, for its part, reported 12% revenue growth to $11.20 billion in its most recent quarter and highlighted its own AI product, Agentforce, growing from $540 million to $800 million quarter over quarter, while a company spokesperson warned that "vibe coding CRM without enterprise-grade protections for business data is a high-stakes gamble," citing a 2025 MIT research report finding that internal builds fail twice as often as strategic vendor partnerships. Even builders who have gone the custom route acknowledge the tradeoff isn't purely about upfront cost — CarboNet's COO Bill Schonbrun wasn't sure whether the total cost of building and maintaining his custom system was actually lower than an enterprise SaaS license — and Gartner analyst Adnan Zijadic notes that many companies pursuing enterprise-grade custom builds still end up working with a dedicated "vibe coding vendor" offering customizable templates and prebuilt components, rather than building entirely from scratch, precisely because scaling, securing, and maintaining a vibe-coded application is harder than building the initial version. <span class="src">Meet the Companies Vibe Coding Their Own CRMs</span>

A first-person account from a self-described non-engineer illustrates just how far this capability now extends beyond professional software teams. Using Claude Cowork as a real-time coding companion, the author built and now operates a fully automated production system — a daily AI-news newsletter, a two-host narrated podcast, and the website that publishes both — entirely in spare time over several weeks, without ever sketching an architecture diagram first. The build proceeded as a sequence of six "graduations," each triggered by hitting a concrete limitation and asking Claude how to solve it: moving from unreliable Claude scheduled tasks to Google Cloud Run and Cloud Scheduler for dependable triggering; adding Firestore as a minimal two-document state store to make the pipeline idempotent (safe to retry without duplicating a day's newsletter); wiring the Anthropic API directly into the pipeline via authenticated HTTPS calls rather than manual chat interaction; using the Gmail API with OAuth to send real email (which incidentally created a natural human-review window); adding ElevenLabs text-to-speech to generate the two-host podcast audio, plus a Suno-generated jingle for polish; and finally building a public website on Cloudflare Pages, fed by a GitHub repository the pipeline writes to directly through GitHub's Contents API. The resulting system makes four sequential Claude API calls each morning — research (Claude Sonnet with web search), writing (Claude Opus, using a "golden copy" example newsletter as a style guide), self-review (the same model grading its own output against a quality threshold and rewriting if it falls short), and podcast-transcript generation — with Gemini used separately for image generation, since the author found its multimodal output currently stronger than Claude's for visuals. The author also discovered and solved a recurring "drift" problem: Claude Cowork would periodically forget prior architectural decisions and suggest alternatives (like Vercel hosting) already rejected weeks earlier, which the author fixed by building a persistent-memory "skill" documenting the system's actual architecture, invoked at the start of every relevant session. Most notably, the author deliberately trained himself out of a human-in-the-loop approval gate that had initially required him to review and approve each day's newsletter and transcript manually: once a self-review quality-check module proved reliable enough — grading each draft on structure and depth, auto-approving anything above a threshold, and emailing the author only when something failed the check — full automation became safe, illustrating a general pattern of starting with a human checkpoint and removing it only once the AI's own self-assessment has proven trustworthy in practice. The author's own framing of the underlying lesson: "the question isn't whether AI is good enough to run unsupervised. It's whether you've built the workflow that lets it run unsupervised consistently." <span class="src">How I Built a Production AI System Without Writing the Code</span>

Bain situates both of these ground-level shifts — professional developers working through Claude Code and non-engineers vibe-coding entire systems through Cowork — within what it calls the "Anthropic moment": a shift from point-solution AI coding assistance toward AI capable of executing full end-to-end workflows, marking the transition from AI-assisted to genuinely AI-led software development. Bain's own tracking shows executive expectations accelerating sharply: in its 2024 survey, leaders projected 20-30% productivity gains from AI in software development; by its 2026 survey, many now anticipate gains of 5x to 10x, and AI's role in the software development life cycle has moved from a meaningful contribution cited by roughly half of executives toward near-universal adoption. Bain's research nonetheless finds a persistent gap between these expectations and reality: most companies still report only single-digit efficiency improvements, even as 63% report higher output per engineer and 53% report faster release cycles. Bain identifies five recurring reasons transformation efforts stall short of their potential — optimizing a single activity like code generation rather than the full delivery chain (which just moves the bottleneck elsewhere); mistaking a proliferation of pilots for real adoption, since pilots don't automatically translate into sustained usage or business impact without new workflows, measurement, and guardrails; focusing too narrowly on code completion rather than combining tight-loop development assistance with a broader agent mode for multistep work; underestimating organizational resistance to a genuinely new way of working, which requires enablement and reassurance rather than being treated as a simple tooling swap; and failing to measure or prove value in a way that supports a credible ROI conversation, without which initiatives cannot scale past the pilot stage. Bain's central structural claim is that AI is dissolving the long-standing boundary between the product development life cycle (defining what to build, based on customer needs and strategy) and the software development life cycle (the actual work of building it), because AI-enabled teams can now define, build, test, and refine in a single continuous flow rather than in sequential handoffs between separate product and engineering functions — a shift that requires redesigning organizational structure, since developers increasingly move from being code executors to being "agent architects and orchestrators" who structure work for agents (with fresh context windows at each stage and human review at critical junctures) rather than writing every line themselves. Bain's roadmap for this transition runs through three phases — design, pilot, and scale, with too many companies jumping straight to pilots without first defining the problem and prioritizing investment — and rests on six durable principles regardless of which specific tools are in use: context is king (the best AI development platforms will be distinguished not by coding quality alone but by whether they supply full lifecycle context — product intent, architecture, code base conventions, telemetry, security posture, and customer signals — since without it agents remain simple task accelerators rather than genuine orchestrators); shift left, hard (clearer upfront intent, sharper acceptance criteria, and spec-driven workflows like research-plan-implement matter more as execution itself compresses, since garbage inputs still produce garbage outputs regardless of execution speed); close the loop (software development is a uniquely multistep AI use case, and the process only achieves its goals by transferring context between stages and feeding runtime results back into planning — a place where AI often exposes pre-existing product-engineering dysfunction rather than creating new problems); treat risk as a first-class design constraint rather than an afterthought (agents given real leverage over a product need a defined risk envelope: permissioning, policy-as-code guardrails, auditability, evaluation harnesses, secure secrets handling, and clear human checkpoints for high-risk changes); be intentional about buy versus build (organizations should buy commoditized tooling and orchestration wherever it accelerates learning, reserving genuine build effort for the truly differentiating context layer, domain ontology, and guardrails specific to how their own software actually ships); and measure outcomes, not just output (traditional DORA-style engineering metrics were designed for a world where only humans wrote code, and in an agentic world raw velocity can mislead, since teams can ship faster while quietly accumulating more defects, more technical debt, and more risk — Bain recommends segmenting metrics by human versus agent authorship, adding system-level measures like end-to-end cycle time and human intervention rate, and elevating risk and control health to first-class scorecard dimensions alongside speed). <span class="src">The Rise of the AI Development Life Cycle</span>
