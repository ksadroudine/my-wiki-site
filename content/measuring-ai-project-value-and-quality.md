## Summary

Predictive machine learning projects often fail because data scientists report technical metrics such as accuracy that mean nothing to business stakeholders, and generative AI application teams often underinvest in automated evaluations. Those evaluations would show whether an LLM-based application is good enough to ship, keeps working after launch and survives an underlying model upgrade.

## Concept

Eric Siegel's analysis of predictive machine learning project failure, adapted from his book *The AI Playbook*, identifies a specific and remarkably consistent structural cause behind why so many ML projects never reach deployment or fail to deliver value once they do: a near-total substitution of technical metrics for business metrics throughout the project lifecycle. Machine learning generates predictive models with genuine potential to improve targeted marketing, fraud mitigation, financial risk management, logistics, and countless other large-scale operational processes — work sometimes distinguished from generative AI by the label "predictive AI" — and one would reasonably expect the resulting business value to be front and center in how these projects get evaluated. In practice, Siegel argues, the opposite happens: data scientists overwhelmingly report on technical metrics like precision, recall, and lift (a measure of how many times better than random guessing a model performs), rather than on business metrics like revenue, profit, cost savings, or customers acquired that would directly convey the model's actual value to business stakeholders. This isn't a matter of data scientists not knowing better — the 2023 Rexer Analytics Data Science Survey found data scientists themselves rank business KPIs like ROI and revenue as the most important metrics, even while reporting technical metrics as what they most commonly and consistently measure in practice — a gap Siegel attributes largely to the fact that most ML software tooling is built to surface technical metrics by default, with business-metric translation requiring deliberate additional work most teams skip. Even the most commonly cited technical metric, accuracy, is "a blunt instrument" precisely because it treats every kind of prediction error as equally costly, when in the overwhelming majority of real business contexts it isn't: a false positive (the model wrongly flags something as positive) and a false negative (the model wrongly flags something as negative) typically carry very different real-world costs, and accuracy simply can't distinguish between them. Siegel's credit card fraud detection example makes this concrete: when a bank's model wrongly blocks a legitimate transaction (a false positive), the customer is inconvenienced and might shift future spending to another card — a cost Siegel estimates at roughly $100 on average; when the model wrongly approves a fraudulent transaction (a false negative), the bank is typically liable for the full loss, and Siegel estimates this cost at roughly $500 on average — a fivefold difference that a single "accuracy" number completely obscures. Working through a realistic worked example — a mid-sized regional bank with 100,000 issued cards, 100 million annual transactions, and a 0.1% fraud rate — Siegel shows the bank faces roughly $50 million in annual fraud losses with no detection model deployed at all, but that a properly cost-calibrated model (one explicitly built around the asymmetric $100-versus-$500 misclassification costs, rather than optimized for raw accuracy) can capture roughly $16 million of that loss as savings. Counterintuitively, the model that achieves this savings is actually slightly less accurate (99.8%) than a "dumb" baseline model that simply assumes every transaction is legitimate and takes no action at all (99.9% accurate, since fraud is rare) — demonstrating directly that pursuing accuracy and pursuing business value can point in genuinely different directions, and that a model sacrificing a small amount of accuracy specifically to reduce costlier false negatives is the better business outcome even though it looks "worse" on the naive technical metric. Siegel's broader argument is that this technical-metrics fixation sabotages ML projects in two compounding ways: during development, data scientists benchmarking against metrics that don't directly measure business value are, by construction, not optimizing for that value even if the underlying model architecture is sound; and at the deployment decision point, business stakeholders lacking visibility into a model's actual business value are forced to either authorize deployment on faith or cancel the project outright — and Siegel's data suggests cancellation dominates, consistent with the IBM Institute for Business Value finding that average enterprise-wide AI initiative ROI sat at just 5.9% as of late 2021, below the prevailing cost of capital, meaning the same capital would have performed better simply invested passively in the market. <span class="src">What Leaders Should Know About Measuring AI Project Value</span>

Where Siegel's research addresses predictive ML specifically, Rama Ramakrishnan's companion research addresses the parallel and increasingly urgent measurement problem facing generative AI application development: the systematic underinvestment in rigorous, iterative "evaluations" (evals) that most GenAI teams exhibit, and the concrete cost of that underinvestment in canceled projects and applications that fail to reach their intended business goal even when they do ship. Building an LLM-based application typically proceeds through three adaptation techniques of escalating complexity and effort, generally applied in sequence as simpler approaches prove insufficient: prompt engineering — directly instructing the LLM to perform a task, sufficient when the task is one an ordinary untrained human could reasonably accomplish, though small wording changes can still produce large output changes, requiring iterative tuning; retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), needed when the application must draw on proprietary company data not present in the base model's training, by extracting the most relevant facts and content for a given query and injecting them into the prompt before sending it to the LLM; and instruction fine-tuning, needed when the task involves specialized jargon or domain knowledge (medical notes, legal briefs) that prompt engineering and RAG alone can't reliably capture, achieved by training the model further on curated input-output examples drawn directly from the task domain. Once a team has built an application using some combination of these techniques, Ramakrishnan argues a rigorous evaluation process becomes the only reliable way to answer the questions that determine whether the project actually succeeds: How does the team know it's making genuine progress as it iterates? How does it decide prompt engineering alone isn't enough and fine-tuning is warranted? How does it decide the application is actually good enough to ship? After deployment, how does it keep the application aligned with evolving real-world user behavior, and how does it verify the application still performs well as underlying LLM vendors release new model versions? A robust evaluation process, per Ramakrishnan, has three interlocking components. First is gathering the questions: assembling a representative suite of inputs — ideally a few hundred well-chosen examples spanning both typical requests and deliberately included corner cases (incomplete, incorrect, or irrelevant input) — using synthetic-data techniques (prompting an LLM to generate realistic test inputs, using tools like LlamaIndex or Ragas) when real end-user data isn't yet available, with synthesized inputs requiring their own quality and relevance checks before being trusted as a test set. Second is conducting error analysis: running every question in the suite through the application, manually examining each output for correctness and for softer criteria like tone, style, and bias, and identifying and correcting every error found — genuinely time-consuming, labor-intensive work performed in the spirit of "data-centric AI" practices common in machine learning but comparatively rare in traditional software engineering, and consequently the step teams most often skip, despite Ramakrishnan calling it indispensable, since it's the only reliable way to build genuine understanding of what's actually going wrong and why. Third is automating that error analysis, since repeating painstaking manual review after every development change simply isn't practical at iteration speed: for structured or rule-governed outputs (checking whether a response contains a URL or email address it shouldn't, for instance), straightforward code-based checks suffice; for open-ended qualitative questions (is this LLM-generated summary actually faithful to the source article? does this response avoid slang?), the "LLM-as-a-judge" technique comes into play — feeding the original input, the LLM's output, and a verified ground-truth answer to a second, separate LLM and prompting it to judge whether the first LLM's output matches and satisfies stated criteria, with the judge LLM's own outputs periodically checked against human evaluation and iterated until the two are mostly in agreement, since a judge model can itself hallucinate or err just like any other LLM. Once this evaluation foundation exists, teams gain the ability to "push the button" after every application change and immediately see which quality metrics improved, which regressed due to unintended side effects elsewhere in the system, and which specific components most need further attention — but Ramakrishnan is explicit that the work doesn't end at launch: the team must instrument the live application to log every real user interaction, then regularly examine those logs, since actual end-user inputs reliably prove more varied than any pre-launch synthetic test suite, requiring the team to continually feed new inputs, new discovered error types, and updated error-checking code back into the evaluation suite to keep pace with real usage patterns and to catch quality degradation whenever an underlying LLM vendor ships a model update. As a secondary benefit, the same evaluation infrastructure that supports iterative development quality-checking also directly supports instruction fine-tuning efforts, since a mature evals process efficiently generates both realistic inputs (via synthetic data generation and captured real usage) and high-quality corresponding outputs (via automatic filtering of incorrect outputs), which together constitute exactly the input-output training data fine-tuning requires. <span class="src">The GenAI App Step You’re Skimping On Evaluations</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source directly addresses how the two measurement disciplines (business-metric-driven predictive ML evaluation, and evals-driven GenAI app quality assurance) should be combined for AI systems that blend both predictive and generative components, which is an increasingly common architecture — it's unclear whether a single unified measurement framework would apply, or whether the two disciplines genuinely need to stay separate given their different underlying model types.
- Ramakrishnan's evals process assumes the availability of specialized tooling (Promptfoo, Humanloop, DeepEval, LlamaIndex, Ragas) and organizational capacity to build and maintain the evaluation infrastructure over time — neither this source nor Siegel's addresses what a resource-constrained team (a startup, or a single business unit within a larger, less AI-mature company) should prioritize first if they can't build the full process described.
