## Summary

How skilled negotiators create leverage even without a clear BATNA — through partial alternatives, procedural moves, and reframing threats as warnings. Also covers why large-company negotiations structurally underperform due to agency and alignment problems, and how replacing deal review boards with proactive "deal value boards" fixes both.

## Highlights

- When a complete alternative to a deal doesn't exist, partial alternatives that only cover a fraction of the need can still shift negotiating dynamics: a tech company facing an intransigent sole electronics supplier found two smaller suppliers that together covered just 30% of its volume, and used that partial coverage to force better pricing from the incumbent rather than accepting an unaffordable increase. <span class="src">Negotiating When There Is No Plan B</span>
- Dependence between negotiating parties is usually more balanced than it feels: a company that relies heavily on a sole-source supplier is often just as important to that supplier's revenue and fixed-cost coverage, meaning even a party that seems to have "no alternative" often retains real leverage — genuine walk-away brinkmanship is rare (the authors saw it only once in 25 years of dealmaking). <span class="src">Negotiating When There Is No Plan B</span>
- Beyond a formal BATNA, negotiators can pursue "procedural alternatives" — unilateral actions that don't require the other side's agreement, such as paying invoices based on the lowest price a supplier charges anywhere globally (with a note explaining the "correction") rather than formally demanding renegotiation, using inertia and tacit (rather than active) consent to shift the relationship without a confrontation. <span class="src">Negotiating When There Is No Plan B</span>
- Reframing a walk-away possibility as a warning ("we can't afford this, so we'll be forced to find someone else") rather than a threat ("agree or we cut you off") avoids the coercion-counterthreat spiral, since warnings signal self-protection rather than an intent to harm the other side — and appealing to fairness ("why are you demanding this?") can extract concessions that raw power plays cannot, since people will sometimes concede rather than be shown to be acting unfairly. <span class="src">Negotiating When There Is No Plan B</span>
- Enterprise negotiations underperform not from negotiator skill gaps but from two structural problems: the agency problem (frontline negotiators are incentivized toward closing any deal — commission, time pressure — rather than the enterprise's actual interests, so organizations restrict their authority, turning them into "couriers" who must repeatedly seek approval) and the alignment problem (in complex, multi-stakeholder deals, forcing early pre-negotiation consensus on the most conservative acceptable terms leaves negotiators no room to explore creative trade-offs). <span class="src">Why Big Companies Struggle to Negotiate Great Deals</span>

- Organizations should first decide which deals and issues are actually worth negotiating hard, since most contract terms can be resolved at reasonable "market-standard" levels without real loss of value — the operative question is not how large an issue is but how much of that value is genuinely at stake in negotiating it versus accepting a market norm, and companies increasingly use generative AI agents to close standardized, low-variance deals automatically. <span class="src">Why Big Companies Struggle to Negotiate Great Deals</span>
- Traditional deal review boards (DRBs), which evolved from capital-project approval processes, should be reimagined as proactive "deal value boards" (DVBs) that give negotiating teams an enterprise-wide view of related deals and current/future leverage, and that broker internal "side deals" compensating business units that would otherwise block a proposal that benefits the company overall — evolving from expectation-setting early in a deal, to problem-solving support during negotiation, to a final go/no-go decision made by a small group once options are fully understood. <span class="src">Why Big Companies Struggle to Negotiate Great Deals</span>

## Concept

Two complementary pieces of negotiation research address the same underlying challenge — how organizations extract more value from high-stakes deals — from opposite ends: one focuses on individual negotiator tactics when a walk-away alternative seems unavailable, the other on the organizational structures that prevent even skilled negotiators from creating value at scale. On the individual-tactics side, the starting premise is that the absence of a clear BATNA (best alternative to a negotiated agreement, the term Roger Fisher and William Ury introduced in Getting to Yes) doesn't actually mean a negotiator is powerless — it usually means they haven't expanded their search enough. The first lever is the power of partial alternatives: when negotiators search for a BATNA, they typically look for one complete substitute that addresses every problem, but a breakthrough often comes instead from an option that only partially replaces the deal on the table. A high-tech company entirely dependent on one supplier for critical electronic components, facing repeated price increases and degrading service, identified two smaller, qualified suppliers that together could cover only 30 percent of its total volume — but that partial coverage was enough to credibly reduce its dependence on the incumbent and extract better pricing in exchange for a modest volume commitment, rather than accepting an unaffordable increase under the illusion that its only alternative was catastrophic supply disruption. <span class="src">Negotiating When There Is No Plan B</span>

A second lever is recognizing that dependence between negotiating parties is almost always more balanced than it feels from inside the weaker-seeming position: if a company relies heavily on a sole-source supplier, that supplier typically also depends heavily on the company's revenue, and the higher the switching costs for the customer, the more painful losing that customer usually is for the supplier too — meaning genuine walk-away brinkmanship is exceedingly rare in practice (the authors, who have advised on hundreds of negotiations across three decades, saw a deal actually blow up under firm-but-reasonable pressure only once). A global manufacturer facing a coatings supplier demanding a 10 percent price increase during contract renewal discovered that, although its business represented "only" 10 percent of the supplier's revenue, that revenue came from one of the supplier's most profitable and specialized product lines, meaning losing it would force costly retooling and strain the supplier's cash flow far more than the raw percentage suggested — illustrating that even a seemingly weak negotiating position can rest on hidden supplier vulnerabilities worth actively researching rather than assuming away. A third lever, for situations where a genuine BATNA search keeps coming up empty, is pursuing procedural alternatives: unilateral actions a negotiator can take without needing the other side's formal agreement, distinguishing between active consent (an explicit "yes") and tacit consent (proceeding as long as the counterpart doesn't actively object). A global microprocessor company discovered a supplier was charging wildly inconsistent prices for identical components shipped to different sites; rather than opening a formal negotiation it couldn't easily win (given it had no qualified alternative supplier), it simply began paying every invoice at the lowest price the supplier charged anywhere, with a note explaining the "correction" — and continued receiving shipments uninterrupted for nearly a year, saving significant money each month, until the supplier finally escalated in a notably non-aggressive tone once market conditions had shifted back toward better balance. Related to this is the principal-agent distinction: negotiators facing a large counterparty organization should distinguish the company from the specific individuals at the table, since a client's business may be small relative to the counterparty's total revenue but critical to a specific negotiating unit's targets or a specific negotiator's personal quota — leverage that headline revenue comparisons obscure entirely. Finally, communicating a walk-away possibility is a delicate art: framing it as a threat ("agree or we cut off shipments") triggers a coercive, defensive spiral, while framing the identical substance as a warning ("we can't afford this, so we'll be forced to look elsewhere") signals self-protection rather than an intent to harm, which is far less likely to provoke a counterthreat — and when power plays alone don't move a counterparty, appealing to fairness ("why are you demanding this?") can succeed where raw leverage cannot, since people sometimes concede simply because they can't justify their position once asked to explain it. <span class="src">Negotiating When There Is No Plan B</span>

The second, complementary piece of research addresses why large organizations underperform at negotiation systematically, even when individual negotiators are skilled. Two structural problems drive this: the agency problem, where negotiators act on the organization's behalf but their personal incentives (commission on closing a sale, time pressure to move on to the next of dozens of supplier relationships) don't perfectly match the enterprise's actual interests, leading organizations to restrict negotiator authority so tightly that dealmakers become, in effect, couriers who must repeatedly return to superiors for approval on every concession — a dynamic that slows deals, damages credibility, and strips away the room to solve problems creatively at the table. And the alignment problem, which compounds as deals grow more complex: in multiproduct, multiregion, or cross-functional negotiations, each internal stakeholder (sales wants pricing and revenue, engineering wants realistic deadlines, legal wants minimized risk) needs to be satisfied, and forcing early pre-negotiation consensus typically means agreeing on the most conservative minimums acceptable to everyone — after which negotiators have essentially no room left to explore genuine trade-offs, and if a counterparty won't accept those preapproved terms, the negotiator must reopen the internal debate from scratch, repeatedly, prolonging the process and eroding trust on both sides of the table. Common organizational fixes often make this worse rather than better: "guardrails" that preapprove specific concessions in advance tend to grow so long and restrictive that they almost never match the realities of a live negotiation (one lead negotiator described winning a deal within tight guardrails as something that "never happens"), and traditional deal review boards (DRBs) — which evolved out of capital-project approval, where a project can be approved or rejected on the basis of projected risk and return alone — simply replicate the authority-parceling problem with more senior people involved, since a negotiation additionally requires guesswork about what an external counterparty will actually accept. <span class="src">Why Big Companies Struggle to Negotiate Great Deals</span>

The prescribed fix works by attacking both problems simultaneously rather than separately. First, organizations should decide upfront which deals and issues are genuinely worth a full negotiation effort at all — not every deal merits it, and within any single complex deal, not every issue does either. The operative test isn't how large an issue is in absolute terms but how much of that value is actually at stake in negotiating it versus simply accepting a reasonable, market-standard term: in mature markets like Eurobonds, most terms already proceed on standardized forms with only a small number of genuinely negotiated adjustments, and companies increasingly use generative AI agents to close standardized, low-variance deals automatically within set parameters, freeing skilled human negotiators for the comparatively few issues where negotiation can genuinely change the outcome. Second, and most counterintuitively, the fix for the agency problem is not giving negotiators more authority but giving them essentially none: reframing them as problem-solvers tasked with exploring the counterparty's actual needs and constraints and bringing back a recommended business plan, rather than agents empowered to make binding commitments within a narrow, preapproved band. Removing commitment authority removes the incentive to make self-interested concessions purely to close a deal (directly addressing agency), and because the negotiator's job becomes exploration rather than defending fixed minimums, they can engage in genuinely creative problem-solving with a counterparty without triggering internal vetoes at every turn (addressing alignment). A midsize technology company renewing a critical $180 million partnership deal used exactly this approach — requesting authority to explore innovative structures with the incumbent partner and to open unauthorized, no-commitment talks with a disliked rival, which the board initially resisted but ultimately allowed; the rival partner turned out to be a genuinely viable option, and the resulting leverage helped the company close nearly $1 billion in improved terms with its original incumbent partner. Third, this requires replacing early, locked-in stakeholder sign-off with ongoing, two-way consultation throughout the negotiation — testing volume and delivery trade-offs with business-unit leaders, payment-term trade-offs with finance, service-capacity trade-offs with operations, as new information emerges from the actual talks — rather than freezing every internal position before external negotiation even begins, since forcing people to commit early to minimum acceptable terms predictably leads them to inflate those minimums defensively, hiding the organization's true fallback position even from its own negotiators. Finally, the traditional DRB should evolve into a "deal value board" (DVB): a proactive, cross-silo body whose job is not merely to approve deals but to give negotiators visibility into related deals and future opportunities elsewhere in the organization that could strengthen current leverage, and to broker internal "side deals" that compensate business units who would otherwise block a proposal that benefits the enterprise as a whole — evolving from managing stakeholder expectations early in a deal's life, to active problem-solving support as talks progress, to a final, well-informed go/no-go decision made by a small group only once every viable option has actually been surfaced and understood. <span class="src">Why Big Companies Struggle to Negotiate Great Deals</span>
