## Summary

Jeff Sutherland's Scrum is a framework for team-based, iterative work that replaces traditional project management's detailed upfront plans with short cycles of delivery and learning. Work happens in fixed-length sprints, commonly two weeks, in which the team commits to a set of backlog items and no one outside the team may add work. There are three roles, the team that does the work, the product owner who owns and orders the backlog and the scrum master who removes impediments, and there are routines such as a fifteen-minute daily stand-up and a retrospective at the end of each sprint. Teams estimate stories in relative points using Planning Poker, which makes their velocity, the points they reliably complete in a sprint, visible and improvable, and Scrum treats multitasking as a measurable source of waste.

## Highlights

- Scrum was built to replace traditional project management's pursuit of upfront control and predictability — expressed through detailed plans, Gantt charts, and status reports — with a framework that embraces the fact that every real project involves ongoing discovery, and instead structures regular checkpoints to assess whether work is heading in the right direction. <span class="src">Scrum</span>
- Work happens in Sprints — fixed-length, non-negotiable time boxes (commonly two weeks) during which the team commits to a set of backlog items and no new work can be added by anyone outside the team once the Sprint begins. <span class="src">Scrum</span>
- The Daily Stand-up is a strict fifteen-minute meeting held standing up at the same time every day, in which each team member answers only three questions: what did you do yesterday to help finish the Sprint, what will you do today, and what obstacles are in the team's way. <span class="src">Scrum</span>
- There are exactly three roles in Scrum: the team members who do the work, the Scrum Master who helps the team improve how it works and removes impediments, and the Product Owner who owns and prioritizes the Backlog and is accountable for translating team output into business value. <span class="src">Scrum</span>
- Story estimation uses relative sizing with the Fibonacci sequence (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13...) via "Planning Poker," where team members simultaneously reveal point estimates; if estimates cluster within two cards of each other they are averaged, and if they diverge more than three cards the highest and lowest estimators discuss before re-estimating. <span class="src">Scrum</span>
- The INVEST criteria (Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable) define whether a user story is genuinely "ready" to be pulled into a Sprint; a matching "Definition of Done" defines the conditions a story must meet to be considered complete, and teams that rigorously apply both are reported to roughly double their delivery speed. <span class="src">Scrum</span>
- A team's velocity — the number of story points it reliably completes per Sprint — is treated as the key operating metric, since knowing it lets a Product Owner forecast completion dates from the remaining Backlog and lets the team identify what is actually slowing them down. <span class="src">Scrum</span>
- The Sprint Retrospective closes each Sprint by asking what went right, what could go better, and what one specific, testable improvement (a "kaizen") the team will make next Sprint; a companion "Happiness Metric" survey (rating one's role and the company, and naming one thing that would improve next Sprint) is used to prioritize which improvement to pursue, on the premise that team happiness tracks autonomy, mastery, and purpose. <span class="src">Scrum</span>
- Multitasking is treated as a measurable source of waste, not a virtue: research cited in the source shows a team member splitting time across two simultaneous projects loses roughly 20 percent of total available time to context-switching, rising to 75 percent lost across five projects. <span class="src">Scrum</span>

## Concept

Scrum, as developed by Jeff Sutherland, is a framework for organizing team-based work around short, fixed-length iterations rather than around large upfront plans. It responds directly to a specific failure mode of traditional project management: the pursuit of control and predictability through detailed documentation, schedules, and status charts, which the source argues systematically fails because every substantial project involves ongoing discovery that no upfront plan can anticipate. Rather than trying to eliminate that uncertainty through more detailed planning, Scrum builds a structure around it — regularly checking in on whether the work being done is actually the right work, and whether the team's process itself could be improved. <span class="src">Scrum</span>

The operating rhythm centers on the Sprint, a fixed-length, non-negotiable time box (commonly two weeks) during which the team commits to a defined set of Backlog items and no new work can be inserted by anyone outside the team once the Sprint has started; this protects the team's focus and lets a stable measure of throughput — velocity, the number of story points reliably completed per Sprint — emerge over time. Within each Sprint, the Daily Stand-up enforces a strict fifteen-minute check-in, held standing up at the same time every day, structured around exactly three questions per person (what did you do yesterday to help finish the Sprint, what will you do today, what's blocking you), explicitly designed to surface obstacles and coordinate the team rather than to serve as individual status reporting to a manager. At the end of each Sprint, the team demos a genuinely complete increment of work directly to the people who will use it, and then holds a Sprint Retrospective that looks not at what was produced but at how the team produced it — asking what went right, what could improve, and committing to exactly one specific, testable improvement (a "kaizen," borrowed from Toyota's production system) to pursue in the next Sprint. A companion "Happiness Metric," in which each team member rates their satisfaction with their role and the company and names one thing that would make them happier next Sprint, is used to decide which improvement to prioritize, on the premise — drawn from research on intrinsic motivation — that the same factors that produce happy teams (autonomy, mastery, and purpose) also produce high-performing ones. <span class="src">Scrum</span>

Scrum defines exactly three roles, deliberately limiting the coordination overhead of larger organizational hierarchies: the team members who do the actual work; the Scrum Master, who is responsible for how fast the team is going and how it can go faster, chiefly by identifying and removing impediments (modeled explicitly on Taiichi Ohno's concept of eliminating waste in the Toyota Production System); and the Product Owner, modeled on Toyota's Chief Engineer role, who owns and continuously reprioritizes the Backlog and is held accountable not for team velocity but for translating that velocity into actual business value — a distinct accountability that requires deep domain knowledge, the authority to make binding prioritization decisions, ongoing availability to the team, and a track record measured in value delivered per point of team effort. Because a Product Owner needs both external market knowledge and constant availability to the team, the source explicitly advises against senior executives filling the role, since they typically lack the necessary day-to-day availability. <span class="src">Scrum</span>

Estimation is handled through relative sizing rather than absolute time estimates, using the Fibonacci sequence (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13...) as point values precisely because the growing gaps between successive numbers make size differences easy to perceive without demanding false precision. "Planning Poker" operationalizes this: team members privately choose a point value for a story and reveal them simultaneously, and if the spread is narrow the values are simply averaged, while a wide spread triggers a short discussion between the highest and lowest estimators before a second round — a design meant to avoid both the anchoring bias created by revealing estimates one at a time and the excessive time cost of a fully deliberative estimation process like Delphi. For a story to be considered ready to enter a Sprint, it must satisfy the INVEST criteria (Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable); for it to be considered complete, it must satisfy an explicit, agreed-upon Definition of Done. Teams that rigorously apply both disciplines are reported, in the source's telling, to roughly double their delivery speed on each dimension, compounding to a fourfold improvement when both are combined. Underlying much of this design is an explicit rejection of multitasking as a virtue: citing research on context-switching costs, the source treats working on multiple simultaneous projects as a quantifiable source of waste — losing roughly 20 percent of available time when splitting across two projects, rising to as much as 75 percent lost across five — which is part of the rationale for protecting the Sprint's fixed scope from outside interruption. <span class="src">Scrum</span>
