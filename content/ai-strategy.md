## Summary

Building an enterprise AI strategy is a staged process that runs from executive literacy through opportunity discovery to a prioritized roadmap, backed by mechanisms that carry the strategy into company-wide adoption. The sources add a framework for choosing among four strategic postures and McKinsey's Rewired thesis that operating-model redesign beats tool deployment. They also argue that AI is only partly platformed, so firms should invest in learning and durable complements.

## Concept

AI strategy refers to the deliberate process by which an organization decides where and how to deploy artificial intelligence in pursuit of its business goals, as distinct from ad hoc or opportunistic AI adoption. A commonly cited framework for building one runs through seven stages, beginning with developing executive AI fluency — a shared, non-technical understanding among leadership of what AI can and cannot do, the range of use cases relevant to the firm's industry, and how AI fits the company's broader transformation vision. Without this grounding, leadership cannot meaningfully evaluate or prioritize AI investments. <span class="src">7 Critical Factors for Developing an AI Strategy</span>

Most AI project failures are argued to trace back to exactly this gap in executive fluency, rather than to vendor overselling, messy data infrastructure, or poor team coordination — the reasoning being that a genuinely fluent executive would have caught each of those problems in advance (asking for client case studies, running a small data audit, building an accountable cross-functional team) rather than being blindsided by them mid-project. The specific expectation mismatch driving failure is that executives often unconsciously model an AI investment on how an IT project works — definable scope, boundable functionality, a predictable delivery timeline — when AI investment actually behaves more like R&D, requiring genuine iteration and exploration, with time-to-ROI that is far less predictable than a typical IT rollout. When that mismatch isn't caught early, leadership — unprepared for the data-cleaning investment or new data science talent the project turns out to need midway through — grows frustrated, imposes unrealistic deadlines the team can't meet, and cancels the project, and each visible cancellation makes internal buy-in for the *next* AI initiative harder to secure, compounding the problem across an organization's entire AI adoption trajectory. Executive AI Fluency is described as having three components that build on each other in sequence: first, understanding concretely how AI works and what a realistic AI deployment roadmap actually looks like (the team, data, and resource requirements involved); second, knowing a representative range of AI use cases specific to the company's own industry, including a realistic sense of where AI's current capability boundary actually falls — the given example is that a banking chatbot can reasonably be expected to answer simple, common customer questions, but not to handle a complex conversation drawing on the customer's prior history, since that's beyond what the technology reliably does today; and third, only once the first two are in place, building a genuine AI Transformation Vision that ties specific use cases to the company's 3-5 year strategic goals — attempting to build this vision without first grounding it in a real understanding of AI's mechanics and realistic use-case boundaries is described as leading almost inevitably to rash or ill-informed investment decisions. Educating executives to this level of fluency is credited with three concrete benefits: better project selection (funding goes to the projects genuinely likely to succeed, rather than whichever competitor's press release or vendor's slide deck was most persuasive), realistic expectations (leadership agrees up front to success measures the team can actually hit, rather than demanding an "IT-like" delivery timeline for what is really an R&D-like process), and well-resourced projects (the project gets the staffing, budget, and organizational context it needs, rather than being handed to an isolated team with no context to actually learn and adapt). This responsibility is framed as shared between enterprise leadership itself — who should actively consult peers, attend industry events, and build AI literacy as part of ordinary strategic planning rather than treating it as someone else's job — and the AI vendors and advisors serving them, whose own long-term commercial success depends on genuinely educating buyers about both AI's transformative potential and its real deployment challenges, rather than overselling a "plug-and-play" narrative that reliably collapses on delivery. <span class="src">Ending the Cycle of Failed AI Proof-of-Concept Projects</span>

From there, the process moves to clarifying the firm's existing business strategy and near/long-term priorities, then to articulating a full AI Transformation Vision: a written, often visual statement of where AI will contribute to competitive advantage, how the company will exploit its own proprietary data streams, and which areas — typically customer experience and core operations — stand to benefit soonest. This vision serves as a reference point that later-stage project discovery can be measured against, though it may itself be refined as individual project ideas surface. <span class="src">7 Critical Factors for Developing an AI Strategy</span>

The discovery phase collects a broad set of candidate AI initiatives, ideally sourced from multiple angles — use cases borrowed from adjacent industries, and lists of the firm's own near-term pain points — and is most productive when it draws on a genuinely cross-functional team. This is paired with an assessment of the organization's current AI maturity (sometimes called AI readiness), which establishes the starting point against which the roadmap is built. Candidate projects are then ranked using a combination of estimated cost, time to validate a pilot, strategic upside, and measurable financial upside, with the caveat that cost and time estimates for AI work are unusually hard to pin down and should be treated as order-of-magnitude figures rather than precise numbers. The output is a roadmap: a prioritized shortlist of initiatives with rough timelines, paired with the concretized Transformation Vision, meant to align business and technical leadership on a shared destination. <span class="src">7 Critical Factors for Developing an AI Strategy</span>

Industry-specific strategies follow the same shape at larger scale. Telecom operators such as SK Telecom structure their AI strategy as a pyramid separating foundational infrastructure investment, internal transformation of core business lines, and new AI-powered customer services — mirroring the generic maturity-assessment-to-roadmap sequence but applied across an entire industry vertical. <span class="src">Match Your AI Strategy to Your Organization’s Reality</span> (HBR, January 2026) addresses a question the other three sources in this concept largely take as given: once an organization has built executive fluency and moved through the assess/learn/experiment/build/scale adoption sequence, which *type* of AI strategy should it actually pursue? The article's diagnosis is that AI success depends less on algorithm quality than on the fit between a company's ambitions and its structural reality, illustrated by a paired 2018 example: GM's AI-generated, 40%-lighter latticelike seat bracket never reached production because its stamped-steel manufacturing system couldn't handle the geometry, while Apple's contemporaneous metalens work succeeded because Apple had the integrated technical system (materials science, semiconductor manufacturing, machine learning) to execute on it. From research across sectors, the authors identify two structural dimensions that determine which strategy fits a given company: value-chain control (how much influence the company has over the journey from idea to market) and technological breadth (how many fast-evolving, interdependent technologies it must integrate to compete). Crossing these dimensions yields four quadrant strategies, each with a characteristic logic, canonical examples, and a matching overreach failure mode. Companies with narrow value-chain control and low technological breadth pursue focused differentiation — going deep on one leverage point rather than chasing the frontier, as PepsiCo did with drone-based precision potato farming and McCormick did with its IBM-built SAGE flavor-development system — with Zillow's collapsed Zestimate-driven home-flipping business as the cautionary tale of overreaching this quadrant's ambition. Companies with broad control but low breadth pursue vertical integration — embedding AI across processes they already own, as JD.com did across its logistics network and Walmart did in hurricane-driven supply rerouting — with GE's $4-billion-plus Predix platform failure as the overreach case, undone by siloed data and organizational resistance rather than weak technology. Companies with narrow control but high breadth pursue a collaborative ecosystem strategy — partnering to share innovation risk and infrastructure, as Novartis did with Microsoft on drug discovery and Pfizer did with BioNTech on the COVID-19 vaccine — with IBM Watson's stalled MD Anderson oncology partnership as the overreach case, undone by integration and clinical-practice misalignment. And companies with both broad control and high breadth pursue platform leadership — setting standards other organizations build on, as Bloomberg did with BloombergGPT and Microsoft did with Azure OpenAI Service — with Google DeepMind Health's NHS partnership as the overreach case, collapsed not by weak technology but by a patient-data consent breach that broke public trust. The article treats these quadrants as dynamic rather than fixed (a company can sit in different quadrants across functions or regions) and profiles P&G as a company that deliberately operates in all four simultaneously — focused differentiation in its Oral-B iO toothbrush, vertical integration in its Azure IoT-driven manufacturing, a collaborative ecosystem through its Connect + Develop open-innovation network, and platform-adjacent reach through its Olay Skin Advisor and consumer-pulse data system. Finally, the article identifies employee resistance as a risk cutting across all four quadrants independent of which strategy a company pursues: in one enterprise survey, 31% of employees admitted actively pushing back on AI initiatives and 10% admitted sabotaging output quality or metrics, a risk Colgate-Palmolive and a company called Rent a Mac both addressed by making employees visible participants in AI rollout (internal AI Hubs, AI champions) rather than passive subjects of it. <span class="src">Match Your AI Strategy to Your Organization’s Reality</span>

Read together, the four sources describe the full arc of building and executing an AI strategy: the first two establish the executive-fluency foundation and staged process (fluency, vision, discovery, roadmap) needed before any strategy can be credibly chosen; the third supplies the organizational mechanisms (communities of practice, Centers of Excellence) that carry a chosen strategy through to company-wide adoption; and the fourth supplies the missing piece of *which* strategic posture to choose given a company's actual structural position, plus the quadrant-specific failure modes to watch for once that choice is made.

Two companion McKinsey pieces extend the Rewired manifesto's central claim — that durable advantage comes from organizational capability, not tools — into a specific argument about *why* operating-model redesign beats bolt-on AI deployment, and *what*, concretely, the companies that get it right actually do differently. "The Operating Model Advantage" opens from the same diagnostic McKinsey uses elsewhere: AI activity is everywhere, financial benefit is rare, and — echoing the manifesto's point that tools alone confer no advantage once broadly accessible — since 2023 hyperscalers have invested $1.3-1.6 trillion in AI infrastructure reaching 1.0-1.5 billion users, meaning the technology itself is rapidly commoditizing while outcomes diverge sharply across companies using the same tools. The article's central mechanism is what it calls the "complexity scissors": as companies scale, revenue growth follows a natural S-curve (rapid acceleration, then maturity), but coordination costs — review boards, management layers, cross-functional committees needed to manage a growing web of interdependencies (a 50-person team has 1,225 possible communication links against a 5-person team's 10) — rise continuously, opening a widening gap between the two curves; McKinsey's own organizational-health research finds fewer than one in ten large companies sustain above-cost-of-capital returns over a decade as a result. AI matters specifically because it is structurally different from prior technology waves: business process reengineering, ERP, and digital transformation all promised flatter organizations but left the underlying coordination structure (the same people, same approval chains) intact, whereas AI can route information and decisions through centralized orchestration layers based on nonpolitical, ROIC-oriented criteria, directly targeting the coordination layer itself rather than just accelerating individual tasks. Most companies still get this wrong: a McKinsey survey found only 21% have fundamentally redesigned their operating model around AI, while the remainder simply "bolt on" AI to unchanged structures, which fails for three reasons — complexity remains intact (adding AI often triggers more review layers rather than fewer, and McKinsey research shows agentic systems operating inside workflows not redesigned for them can propagate errors at machine speed), value stays trapped at the task level (individual activities speed up but broader workflows and bottlenecks don't move), and companies measure the wrong things (AI licenses and pilot counts rather than decision-cycle speed or ROIC). Top performers — those attributing 5%+ of EBIT to AI — were three times more likely to pursue broad operating-model redesign and twice as likely to redesign workflows before even selecting AI tools; concrete cases include Emirates Global Aluminium, which trained thousands of employees through a dedicated digital academy before deploying AI across smelting operations and documented $120 million in impact with a 170% ROI, and Toyota, whose AI-mediated resource-allocation workflow replaced a coordination-heavy process of dozens of spreadsheets and weeks of manual planning, shrinking the planning team more than 80% through redeployment (not layoffs) into higher-value work. McKinsey frames the resulting sequence as three questions in order: where to create strategic differentiation (concentrating investment in one to three high-value domains with proprietary data and workflow complexity, rather than a thin "peanut butter" spread — the article cites AI transformations led primarily as technology programs failing at rates above 80% because business and P&L leaders, not IT, must own domain selection), how to reorganize workflows (decomposing tasks and redesigning workflows before selecting talent models or technology — 79% of companies skip this step, yet McKinsey finds workflow redesign the factor most strongly correlated with enterprise EBIT impact), and how to rewire the operating model while keeping the business running (McKinsey's *Rewired* dual-operating-model approach: agile pods with different decision rights and metrics operating alongside the unchanged core business, demonstrating results through performance rather than mandate). <span class="src">The operating model advantage Why AI winners are rewiring their organizations</span>

"The Seven Operating Truths of AI-Native Companies" supplies the ground-level, empirically-derived companion to this operating-model argument: after interviewing leaders at 15 AI-centric companies spanning continents, industries, and company stages, McKinsey found this diverse group converged independently on the same seven insights, without having coordinated with each other — a convergence McKinsey treats as evidence these are genuine operating-system principles rather than one company's idiosyncratic playbook. The seven truths build on each other sequentially. AI is not a tool, it's a teammate: leading companies give agents names, Slack handles, and shared task boards, measuring success by what the business is newly willing to attempt (a Series A marketplace CEO's "virtual staff" boosted his capacity fivefold) rather than by hours saved, with the leadership move being to map how jobs are being reordered and redesign roles around the new human-agent allocation. Know what to build versus buy: build only what creates defensible advantage based on proprietary data or IP (an agtech venture builds its own crop-breeding models in-house), but buy everything else, with newer coding agents (Claude Code, Cursor) shifting where more technical companies draw that line — one CSO argues "SaaS is dead" because integrating a tool now takes longer than building one, though the leadership move cautions that custom tools are cheap to build but not to maintain. Your model isn't the bottleneck — accessing your tribal knowledge is: the ceiling on AI usefulness is set by an organization's knowledge hygiene, not model choice, since an agent can't answer a question that was never written down; the fix is architectural (build connectors to where activity already happens — meetings, Slack, working documents) rather than demanding a single unified data lake, since knowledge stays fresh only when humans keep touching and updating it in the tools they already use. Design for the swap, not the stack: winning architecture is a thin, governed layer connecting best-in-class, interchangeable components rather than a monolithic platform, with model agnosticism as a nonnegotiable design principle — "everything we build needs to be done in such a way that we can easily rip out a model here and put something else in," in one fintech COO's words — paired with tiered security (public data to commercial LLMs, sensitive data to zero-retention providers, core IP processed only on-premises). Trust precedes autonomy: autonomy is earned progressively — one venture automates a process only after running it manually long enough that "the pain forces automation" — and leading companies hold a firm quality ceiling for each domain (99.999% for healthcare, roughly 70-80% for software development and back-office work, beyond which human judgment is where value concentrates), since ungated autonomy backfires fast, as when one company's email-response agent nearly sent unauthorized contract terms to a customer before a human caught it. Centralize the platform, decentralize the tasks: no single centralized AI department or expert can drive transformation across business units they don't understand; instead, platform teams govern shared infrastructure, security, and architecture while business teams own their own problems and build directly on top of it, since McKinsey research found workers were three times more likely than leaders expected to already use AI for 30%+ of daily tasks — meaning the adoption barrier is leaders not enabling fast enough, not employee reluctance. And adoption is a flywheel, not a rollout: sustained adoption requires four reinforcing layers — role modeling (leaders visibly use AI themselves), sharebacks (social proof through AI guild talks and shared prompt libraries, not top-down mandates), measurement (adoption leaderboards by department), and hiring for AI-fluency as a screening criterion — with the flywheel stalling if any single layer is missing. McKinsey notes these seven truths mesh directly with its own *Rewired* playbook already covered in this concept: treating agents as teammates raises the build-versus-buy question, which depends on getting the knowledge layer right, which depends on composable governed architecture, safe operation requires earned trust, scaling requires the platform/task organizational split, and sustaining it all requires adoption as a genuine cultural flywheel rather than an IT rollout with a deadline. <span class="src">The seven operating truths of AI-native companies</span>

<span class="src">Building on AI’s Unfinished Foundation</span> (MIT SMR, August 2026) supplies a macro-level counterpart to this concept's firm-level frameworks, arguing that AI's failure to yet deliver general-purpose-technology-scale transformation is not primarily a model-capability or adoption-friction problem but an incomplete-platforming problem. Drawing on the historical precedent of electrification — technologically proven and commercially viable by 1882, yet not widely adopted for nearly four decades, until technological architecture stabilized around the 1895-96 Niagara Falls hydroelectric project, industrial architecture matured into a settled utilities/equipment-makers/financiers division of labor by 1907, and institutional architecture followed with the first state public-utility commissions that same year — the article argues that a general-purpose technology only becomes economically transformative once a technological, industrial, and institutional architecture stabilizes enough for decentralized organizations to confidently build on it. Applied to AI, the lower layers of the stack (chips, cloud compute, frontier models) are converging on a recognizable division of labor (Nvidia in accelerators; AWS, Microsoft, and Google Cloud in compute; OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and xAI in frontier models), but the application and deployment layer — where most companies actually try to build, and precisely where this concept's other frameworks operate — remains fluid, with no equivalent yet to the governed app-store or stable-API interfaces that let thousands of developers commit capital with confidence in personal computing or mobile. Institutional architecture lags furthest behind: no AI company has taken on true platform leadership (the deep, ecosystem-wide coordination role Microsoft and Intel played in personal computing or Apple played with iOS), and Anthropic's Model Context Protocol, while a useful technical standard, falls well short of that role; in its absence, leading AI companies are integrating vertically instead, which the article argues is not a substitute for ecosystem governance and may delay rather than accelerate the mature platform ecosystem it appears to replace. Given this incomplete platforming, the article proposes four investment principles that pay off regardless of how the architecture eventually settles, extending this concept's own "durable capability over tool deployment" thesis (echoed in the Rewired manifesto and the seven operating truths above) to the level of an entire technology ecosystem rather than a single company's operating model: learn faster than you commit, since the cost of experimentation is unusually low right now (open-weight models like Llama, Mistral, or DeepSeek let a company run near-zero-marginal-cost experiments on its own hardware, and the corrective signal from watching human experts override the model — the richest source of learning about where AI is and isn't reliable on a company's own tasks — disappears once a model reliably beats those experts); build assets that survive architectural change, which requires looking past the narrow technical question of whether a system would still run if the underlying model were swapped, to the commercial question of vendor lock-in through billing structures and usage terms (Salesforce's Agentforce meters work in "agentic work units" it alone defines, and SAP's 2026 policy restricts what outside AI tools can do with data inside its software); invest in complements rather than in AI capability itself, since intelligence that every competitor can rent from the same handful of vendors cannot be anyone's durable advantage — real advantage instead accrues to whoever holds a defensible "seam," an interface layer resting on something a shared model cannot easily reproduce, such as a regulator's standing trust, an audit trail, an embedded billing relationship, or a proprietary data corpus (many hospitals route clinical AI through Epic specifically because Epic, not the AI, has already cleared the regulatory trust bar); and build organizational capability — understanding where a company's own knowledge sits, how its decisions actually get made, and where its bottlenecks are — as the complement no shared model can ever internalize, and the one that keeps generating new complements as the surrounding architecture continues to shift. The article closes by noting that the deep gains from any general-purpose technology arrive late and require both an external condition no single company controls (the architecture settling) and an internal one every company does control (rebuilding itself around the technology in the meantime), citing manufacturers who redesigned factories around electric power and companies that reorganized around information technology as having captured gains well before their respective platforming processes fully completed. <span class="src">Building on AI’s Unfinished Foundation</span>

<span class="src">Lessons from our alliances What AWS’s agentic journey can teach CEOs about rewiring for AI</span> (McKinsey, August 2026) supplies a concrete, single-company case study of the Rewired framework already covered in this concept, applied to AWS's own go-to-market organization rather than to a McKinsey client. AWS's starting condition mirrored the "bolt-on AI without redesign" failure mode McKinsey's operating-model-advantage research warns against at the industry level: two decades of empowering teams to move fast had produced more than 200 first-party sales tools, opportunity-to-cash processes spanning more than 40 disconnected systems, and no consistent business-wide view of customer data — a fragmentation vivid enough that one financial-services customer noted a smaller, AI-enabled competitor delivered a comparable technical solution in two hours versus AWS's multiple human hand-offs. Following the same domain-concentration principle the Rewired manifesto prescribes (one to three high-value domains, not a thin spread), AWS committed to transforming its go-to-market function end-to-end rather than layering agents onto the existing tangle, consolidating hundreds of data sets into 20 foundational ones and unifying more than 75 field-role definitions into a single taxonomy before attempting any workflow redesign — directly enacting this concept's "fix how the system works before scaling automation" principle rather than accelerating a broken process. Only once that data foundation existed did AWS redesign workflows around agents: an AI-assisted pipeline-analysis agent surfaced nearly $77 million in additional commercial opportunities in one business segment, redesigned prospecting workflows roughly doubled average email open rates and drove a 4.5-fold increase in one pilot's lead conversion, and an AI-enabled selling-strategy capability produced 30% more opportunities created and 44% more launched. Even with this technical success, adoption did not follow automatically — one prospecting capability reached over 90% adoption among its intended users but plateaued below 50% once access expanded to a broader group whose workflows the tool wasn't designed to support, a direct illustration of this concept's own finding (via the "seven operating truths" and the Rewired manifesto's adoption-as-flywheel principle) that technology and workflow redesign alone do not guarantee use; AWS's own response — peer-to-peer "field champion" support, leader immersion sessions, and embedding tools into existing business rhythms like mandatory use in weekly reviews — replays the same role-modeling-and-sharebacks adoption mechanism McKinsey's 15-AI-native-company research documents. AWS's own framing of the lesson echoes this concept's central thesis exactly: "scaling agents is an operating model transformation rather than simply a technology challenge," and the leaders who pull ahead may be "those willing to use \[agents\] to fundamentally rethink how work gets done" rather than those with the most agents. <span class="src">Lessons from our alliances What AWS’s agentic journey can teach CEOs about rewiring for AI</span>

## Open Questions

- The sources outline the stages of forming a strategy and the components of executive fluency, but neither details how firms should measure ROI or success against the roadmap once initiatives are underway.
- The AI transformation manifesto names McKinsey's own 20-company impact study (20 percent average EBITDA uplift) but doesn't specify how those companies were selected or whether they overlap with the case studies (PepsiCo, JD.com, Walmart, etc.) in Match Your AI Strategy to Your Organization's Reality, leaving the two sources' evidence bases only loosely connected.
- The platforming framework argues institutional architecture (platform leadership or public-authority coordination) is what AI most lacks, but doesn't predict which path AI will actually take — a dominant lab assuming Microsoft/Intel-style platform leadership, government-led standards, or continued vertical integration — leaving open how long full platforming might take relative to electrification's roughly 25-year technological-to-institutional sequence.
