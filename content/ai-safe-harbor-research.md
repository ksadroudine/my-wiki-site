## Summary

A proposal, backed by 23 researchers and hundreds of signatories, for legal and technical "safe harbor" protections for independent AI safety research. It would let researchers evaluate and red-team deployed AI models without risking legal liability or account termination.

## Concept

The proposal starts from a premise widely shared across AI companies, academic researchers, and civil society: independent risk assessment of deployed AI models is an essential accountability mechanism, distinct from and complementary to the pre-release red teaming a company conducts internally or with a small set of hand-selected external experts. Independent, post-release evaluation — sometimes called third-party algorithmic auditing — has a track record of surfacing problems that internal testing misses, including vulnerabilities specific to low-resource languages, techniques for bypassing safety measures, and a wide range of jailbreaks that let a model be coaxed past its intended restrictions. <span class="src">A safe harbor for AI evaluation and red teaming</span>

The structural problem the proposal targets is that the terms of service governing popular AI models — including those from OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Inflection, Meta, and Midjourney — generally prohibit precisely this kind of research. While ostensibly written to deter malicious actors, these terms in practice also disincentivize good-faith safety research, since companies retain the right to suspend a researcher's account or pursue legal action, with limited transparency into how enforcement decisions are actually made and no formal mechanism for justification or appeal. This creates a real deterrent even for researchers not worried about legal liability per se: losing account access can itself derail other, unrelated research that depends on continued access to the same account. The result is that only a relatively small group of researchers specifically selected by the companies themselves have clear legal authorization to evaluate models that are already deployed to millions of users and pose immediate real-world risk. Existing protections fall short of closing this gap: several companies (OpenAI, Google, Meta) operate bug bounties with legal protections, but these are scoped narrowly to traditional security issues like unauthorized account access, not the broader category of safety and trustworthiness research the proposal is concerned with, and even where protection exists, companies like Meta and Anthropic explicitly reserve final discretion over whether a given researcher's actions counted as "good faith" — a discretionary standard that can itself chill legitimate research. Cohere and OpenAI are noted as partial exceptions that have moved some distance toward broader protection (Cohere explicitly permitting "intentional stress testing" and adversarial attacks with appropriate disclosure; OpenAI expanding its safe harbor to include model-vulnerability and academic model-safety research in direct response to an early draft of this proposal), though ambiguity remains about the exact scope of what's covered in both cases. <span class="src">A safe harbor for AI evaluation and red teaming</span>

The proposed remedy is two distinct, complementary commitments. A legal safe harbor would protect good-faith, public-interest evaluation research from legal liability — specifically reducing exposure under the U.S. Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) and Section 1201 of the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA), both of which have historically been used against security researchers, and both of which are cited as live risks in AI specifically: OpenAI attempted to dismiss the *NYTimes v. OpenAI* lawsuit in part by framing the New York Times' research into the model as unauthorized hacking, illustrating that this legal exposure is not merely a hypothetical concern for researchers. This proposal is explicitly modeled on a similar prior proposal by the Knight First Amendment Institute for safe-harbor protection of research on social media platforms. A technical safe harbor would separately address the practical enforcement mechanisms — account suspension, rate limiting — that operate independently of legal risk and can deter research even when a researcher is confident they wouldn't ultimately lose a lawsuit. Both protections are explicitly bounded rather than unconditional: they apply only to research that abides by established vulnerability disclosure practices, assessed *ex post*, meaning research that turns out to have been conducted in bad faith or without responsible disclosure would fail to qualify for protection when challenged — the proposal frames this as leaving existing enforcement against genuinely malicious misuse fully intact, since protection was never available to bad-faith actors in the first place. <span class="src">A safe harbor for AI evaluation and red teaming</span>

Implementing the technical safe harbor specifically is acknowledged as harder than it sounds, because it requires distinguishing legitimate research from malicious activity in something closer to real time, before the ex-post legal test can be applied — the proposal suggests this could be handled through scaled-up, impartially reviewed researcher access programs, with review either happening in advance or promptly whenever an account suspension looks unfair, ideally involving independent third parties in that review process rather than leaving the determination solely to the company being scrutinized. <span class="src">A safe harbor for AI evaluation and red teaming</span>
