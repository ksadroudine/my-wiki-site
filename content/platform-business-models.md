## Summary

Platform businesses create value by letting external producers and consumers find each other and exchange value, rather than by producing goods in a linear pipeline, and their design rests on a core interaction of participants, value unit and filter sustained by the functions of pull, facilitate and match. Their defensibility comes from network and learning effects whose strength depends on network structure and is limited by multihoming and disintermediation, countered through switching costs, hub services and bridging into adjacent networks, while their scale raises ethical risks of amplification, bias, security, platform harm and inequality. AI shopping agents now test these foundations by enabling zero-click commerce that bypasses the interfaces where platforms earn advertising, fees and lock-in.

## Concept

Platform business models describe firms that create value not by producing goods but by building infrastructure that lets external producers and consumers find each other, transact and exchange value, with the platform curating participants and governing interactions. The traditional pipeline model by contrast designs, builds and pushes a product through a linear chain to an end consumer. Sangeet Paul Choudary's Platform Scale explains the move from pipes to platforms through three shifts. Markets must be designed around producers as well as consumers, because a platform cannot create value without enticing a supply side, competitive advantage moves from control of proprietary resources to the ability to orchestrate a wider ecosystem using data, and value creation moves from internal, linear processes to externally facilitated interactions among participants the platform does not own. <span class="src">Platform Scale</span>

Designing a platform starts with its core interaction, the single most important recurring exchange of value that draws users. It has three components, namely the participants, a producer who creates a value unit and a consumer who consumes it, with the same user often moving between roles, the value unit, the information, content or service exchanged, which the platform typically does not create or control, and the filter, the algorithmic mechanism that decides which value units reach which consumers, since a well-matched flow of relevant units keeps a platform valuable at scale. To sustain it, a platform continuously performs three functions. Pull solves the chicken-or-egg cold start by attracting both sides before either sees much value, facilitate lowers the friction of participation through tools, rules and sometimes deliberately raised barriers, as Sittercity vets babysitters to build trust, and match uses accumulating data about participants to connect the right producers with the right consumers. <span class="src">Platform Revolution</span>

Defensibility rests on network effects, which Platform Revolution divides into four types that a two-sided platform with separate producer and consumer populations must manage together. Positive and negative same-side effects are the impact of users on others of their own type, more subscribers making a phone network more valuable and too many low-quality producers crowding a market being negative, and positive and negative cross-side effects are the impact across sides, more sellers benefiting buyers through selection but too many sellers overwhelming buyers with irrelevant noise. Network effects are distinguished from growth tools often confused with them, since price and brand effects can attract users but are fragile and easy for a competitor to erode and virality attracts new users without retaining them, so only genuine network effects create the compounding value behind durable lock-in. Data-driven network effects are a related mechanism, in which a growing user base yields more behavioral data for curation and matching and, with good algorithms, steadily improves matches for everyone. <span class="src">Platform Revolution</span>

Platform Scale condenses the transition into a manifesto of maxims. The ecosystem is both the new warehouse and the new supply chain, since value and resources lie outside the firm's walls, data is the new dollar, so business units should be measured on monetizable data absorbed as well as revenue, community management replaces human-resources management, liquidity, the balance of supply and demand, replaces inventory control, curation and reputation replace quality control and algorithms replace many managerial decisions. For large pipe businesses that want to reach platform scale rather than be disrupted by a platform entrant, the recommended sequence is staged. First build a culture of data acquisition, treating every digital service as dollar-generating or data-generating, then achieve data porosity so acquired data flows across the organization, then exploit the free implicit network effects that porosity unlocks, such as recommendations driven by other users' behavior, and only then build explicit communities and enable peer-to-peer exchange, since attempting community or marketplace features before the data foundation exists is premature. <span class="src">Platform Scale</span>

End-to-end design discipline keeps the core stable enough to support an external developer ecosystem as the platform scales. Application-specific features belong at the periphery rather than in the stable core, both because unrelated applications otherwise slow down and because a simple, modular core lets the ecosystem evolve faster than a monolithic, feature-laden one. The principle, borrowed from network architecture, generalizes to software platforms as multi-sided markets, where the openness of core APIs directly shapes how much third-party innovation a platform can absorb from independent developers building complements, as Invisible Engines argues, and The Age of the Platform adds an account of platform ecosystems, planks and freemium dynamics. <span class="src">Platform Revolution</span> <span class="src">Invisible Engines</span> <span class="src">The Age of the Platform</span>

Iansiti and Lakhani's Competing in the Age of AI shows how advantage is created and captured once a digital operating model exists, by analyzing the structure of networks a firm participates in. Because digital connections increasingly ignore industry boundaries, strategic analysis should shift from analyzing an isolated industry to mapping the networks a firm connects to, who and what it connects, what data flows through those connections and what value can be created and captured from that structure. Two mechanisms are at work. Network effects raise value simply because more users join, directly when users value one another, as with messaging apps and fax machines, or indirectly when users on one side value those on another, as Uber's riders value more drivers and vice versa, and a company can sometimes convert one type into the other, as Facebook did by opening its API to developers. Learning effects raise value because more data flowing through the network improves its algorithms, as Google's search quality improves with more searches, and Microsoft Bing's partnership with Yahoo!, which added user scale without comparable learning effects, failed to close the gap with Google, showing that scale alone does not guarantee learning-driven advantage. The two compound, since a larger network generates more data, which improves the algorithms, which raises the network's value and attracts more users. <span class="src">Competing in the Age of AI</span> <span class="src">Competing in the Age of AI</span>

Network structure determines how strongly value scales with size. Globally integrated networks, such as Airbnb's global marketplace, tend to concentrate around a few dominant hubs, because scale accumulated anywhere adds value everywhere, which raises barriers to entry and makes profitability easier for the leader. Clustered networks do not transfer value across clusters, since Uber's global scale contributes little to its position in any single city, a rider in one market gaining nothing from driver density in another, and the same holds for medical networks clustered around disease categories or sports networks around teams, so clustered networks stay far more competitive because a focused challenger can win one cluster without matching the incumbent's global scale, an important test of whether a business is defensible. <span class="src">Competing in the Age of AI</span>

Capturing value from network and learning effects is not automatic, because two forces work against it. Multihoming occurs when users or providers use several competing platforms at once, common where switching costs are low, as ride-hailing drivers and riders routinely use several apps, which caps what any platform can extract, and firms counter it by manufacturing switching costs, such as Uber's driver car-lease financing, which locks a driver into serving enough rides to cover the loan, and mileage-based rate bonuses. Disintermediation occurs when participants, once connected by the hub, trust each other enough to transact directly, a chronic problem for pure connection marketplaces such as Homejoy and early TaskRabbit that deliver most value in the first match and then struggle to justify ongoing fees. Hubs respond by staying continuously valuable through escrow, insurance, dispute resolution and communication tools, which tend to lose value once trust has built between the parties, or more durably by lowering direct transaction fees and monetizing another side of the market. Network bridging, connecting a firm's network to a previously separate one, works reliably, as Uber's entry into food delivery and non-emergency medical transport reuses its driver network and data asset, and the same logic explains why hubs such as Amazon and Alibaba keep expanding into seemingly unrelated markets, since their data and user base transfer. <span class="src">Competing in the Age of AI</span>

The same concentration of power creates the book's central ethical concern, in five categories of risk. Digital amplification is illustrated by a 2017 study of 2.6 million Facebook users in which echo-chamber dynamics measurably boosted anti-vaccine content consumption. Algorithmic bias arises from selection bias in unrepresentative training data, bias introduced in human labeling and the fact that any algorithm is designed for a specific purpose and so encodes bias by construction, which the book argues is never fully eliminable, only reduced through careful model choice and transparent, representative data. Security and platform control and harm follow because open platforms that foster innovation are by the same openness more vulnerable to misuse, and inequality follows because network dynamics concentrate transaction flow and data in a few hubs and so concentrate wealth and power, across firms and across individual workers. The book argues against breaking up dominant winner-take-all platforms, since a successor would simply emerge as the new winner under the same dynamics, and favors a keystone strategy in which the hub aligns its incentives with the long-term health of the ecosystem it depends on, paired with responsive regulation and community involvement. <span class="src">Competing in the Age of AI</span>

The analysis is summarized as a new competitive meta, a shift in the rules of competition governed by five rules. Change is systemic and simultaneous across essentially all industries, not sequential as with earlier industrial technologies, competitive capability is becoming horizontal and universal, covering data sourcing, processing, analytics and algorithm development, rather than vertical and industry-specific, which erodes differentiation based on single-industry specialization, industry boundaries are dissolving as digital operating models recombine capabilities across sectors, operations are shifting from constrained, bound by physical and organizational friction, to near-frictionless at unlimited scale, and, as a structural consequence and not a policy failure of any one firm, concentration and inequality across firms and workers are likely to keep worsening. <span class="src">Competing in the Age of AI</span>

HBR's 2026 article argues that the defensibility described above is being dismantled by AI shopping agents, a real-time stress test of the theory. As consumers delegate search, choice and execution to personal AI agents instead of browsing platform interfaces, transactions run from intent to fulfillment without any interface where advertising, the main revenue engine of Google, at 75% of revenue, Meta, at 97%, and Amazon, whose advertising was its fastest-growing segment at USD 56 billion in 2024, could intervene, a pattern the article calls zero-click commerce. This breaks the two-sided-market equilibrium at the foundation of platform economics, since platforms can no longer subsidize free services to users once advertisers can no longer reach those users through the interface. <span class="src">How AI Is Threatening Platforms’ Revenue Streams</span>

The article maps the damage onto the mechanisms described as sources of defensibility. Transaction fees, long protected by the deliberate friction of the facilitate function and by referral loops that route users deeper into an ecosystem, as from Google Search into Maps into YouTube and from Amazon shoppers into Prime and Whole Foods, collapse because agents search across platforms, unbundle offerings and instantly compare prices, turning winner-take-all into everyone loses together, since agents operate across all networks at once. Subscription lock-in, which depends on sunk-cost biases that a rational agent does not share, unravels, as Amazon Prime's roughly 250 million paying members generated USD 44.37 billion in 2024 but an agent can compare total delivered cost across providers regardless of which one a human already pays, and ecosystem services such as cloud, logistics and payments that platforms bundle to deepen dependence are disaggregated as agents optimize each independently. Agents also erode the personalization advantage behind data-driven network effects, since platforms infer preference only from behavior inside their own walls, such as Amazon's recommendations and Netflix's curation, while a user's agent, with access to email, calendar, cloud drives and private conversations, discerns intent from context, such as a tight budget from bank notifications or a stressful deadline from email tone, producing a vulnerable intimacy and cross-context understanding that no walled-garden data can match. <span class="src">How AI Is Threatening Platforms’ Revenue Streams</span>

Platform responses so far test which design principles survive contact with an intermediary that has no interest in preserving platform monetization. They include legal and technical barriers, such as Amazon's successful injunction against Perplexity's Comet shopping agent, building proprietary agents to control the agentic layer, such as Amazon's Buy for Me, Google's store-calling agents and Visa and Mastercard authentication protocols for autonomous purchases, and industry standards for interoperability, such as the Universal Commerce Protocol led by Google and Shopify and endorsed by Target, Walmart, Visa and Mastercard. Adoption data suggests the shift is no longer speculative, since Salesforce found that AI agents influenced USD 67 billion of global Cyber Week 2025 sales, 20% of all purchases, and Walmart's Sparky and Macy's Gemini-powered Ask Macy's report substantially higher order values among AI-assisted shoppers than unassisted ones. <span class="src">How AI Is Threatening Platforms’ Revenue Streams</span>
