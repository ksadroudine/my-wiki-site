## Summary

The shift from linear "pipeline" businesses to platform businesses that create value by orchestrating interactions between external producers and consumers. Covers the core-interaction design framework, the pull/facilitate/match functions, and the four types of network effects that make platforms defensible — plus HBR's case that AI shopping agents are actively breaking those defenses by bypassing platform interfaces entirely.

## Concept

Platform business models describe firms that create value not by producing goods themselves but by building infrastructure that lets external producers and consumers find each other, transact, and exchange value, with the platform's role limited to curating participants and governing the resulting interactions. This contrasts with the traditional pipeline model, in which a firm designs, builds, and pushes a product through a linear chain to an end consumer. The shift from pipes to platforms is driven by three underlying changes: markets must now be designed around producers as well as consumers, since platforms cannot create value without enticing a supply side to participate; competitive advantage shifts from control of proprietary resources to the ability to orchestrate a broader ecosystem using data; and value creation itself moves from internal, linear processes to externally-facilitated interactions between participants the platform does not own. <span class="src">Platform Scale</span>

Designing a platform starts with its core interaction — the single most important recurring exchange of value that draws users to the platform. This interaction has three components: the participants (a producer who creates a value unit and a consumer who consumes it, with the same user often able to switch roles across different interactions), the value unit itself (the piece of information, content, or service exchanged, which the platform typically does not create or control directly), and the filter (the algorithmic mechanism that decides which value units reach which consumers, since a well-matched flow of relevant units is what keeps a platform valuable at scale). Once the core interaction is defined, a platform must continuously perform three functions to sustain and grow it: pull, which solves the classic chicken-or-egg cold-start problem of attracting both sides of the market before either side sees much value; facilitate, which involves lowering the friction of participation through tools, rules, and sometimes deliberately raised barriers (e.g. Sittercity vetting babysitters to build trust) that shape desirable behavior; and match, which uses accumulating data about participants to connect the right producers with the right consumers. <span class="src">Platform Revolution</span>

The defensibility of a platform rests on network effects, and Platform Revolution decomposes these into four distinct types that a two-sided platform (one with separate producer and consumer populations) must manage simultaneously: positive and negative same-side effects (the impact users have on other users of the same type — more subscribers making a phone network more valuable is a positive same-side effect; too many low-quality producers crowding a market is a negative one), and positive and negative cross-side effects (the impact users on one side have on the other side — more sellers benefiting buyers by increasing selection is positive; too many sellers overwhelming buyers with irrelevant noise turns cross-side effects negative). Network effects are explicitly distinguished from other growth tools that are often confused with them: price effects and brand effects can attract users but are comparatively fragile and easy for a competitor to erode, and virality attracts new users to a platform but does nothing to retain them once there — only genuine network effects create the compounding value that produces durable lock-in. Data-driven network effects are a related but separate mechanism: as a platform's user base grows, the volume of behavioral data available for curation and matching grows with it, which — given well-designed algorithms — steadily improves the platform's ability to serve relevant matches to everyone on it. <span class="src">Platform Revolution</span>

Platform Scale frames this same transition as a set of maxims collected into a "Platform Manifesto": the ecosystem functions as both the new warehouse and the new supply chain (value and resources live outside the firm's walls), data functions as the new dollar (business units should be measured on monetizable data absorbed, not just revenue), community management substitutes for human-resources management, liquidity (supply/demand balance) substitutes for inventory control, curation and reputation substitute for quality control, and algorithms substitute for many traditional managerial decisions. For large, established "pipe" businesses seeking to move toward platform scale rather than being disrupted by a platform entrant, the recommended sequence is deliberately staged: first build a culture of data acquisition (treating every digital service as either dollar-generating or data-generating), then achieve data porosity so that acquired data actually flows and integrates across the organization, then exploit the "free" implicit network effects that data porosity unlocks (e.g. product recommendations powered by other users' behavior), and only after those foundations are in place attempt to build explicit user communities and enable direct peer-to-peer exchange — attempting community-building or marketplace features before the data foundation is in place is described as premature. <span class="src">Platform Scale</span>

End-to-end design discipline is what keeps a platform's core stable enough to support an external developer ecosystem as it scales: application-specific features should live at the periphery of the platform rather than being folded into its stable core, both because unrelated applications otherwise slow down and because a simple, modular core lets the ecosystem evolve faster than a monolithic, feature-laden one could. This principle, borrowed from network architecture, generalizes to software platforms as multi-sided markets, where the openness of a platform's core APIs directly shapes how much third-party innovation it can absorb from independent developers building complementary products on top of it. <span class="src">Invisible Engines</span>

<span class="src">How AI Is Threatening Platforms’ Revenue Streams</span> (HBR, April 2026) argues that the network-effects defensibility this concept describes as durable is being actively dismantled by AI shopping agents, offering a real-time stress test of the theory. As consumers delegate search, choice, and execution to personal AI agents rather than browsing platform interfaces directly, the result is "zero-click commerce" — transactions proceeding from intent to fulfillment without any interface interaction where advertising, the primary revenue engine for Google (75% of revenue), Meta (97%), and Amazon (its fastest-growing segment at $56 billion in 2024), could ever intervene. This breaks the two-sided-market equilibrium at the foundation of platform economics: platforms can no longer subsidize free services to users once the advertiser side can no longer reach those users through the interface. The article maps the damage directly onto the mechanisms this concept's other sources describe as sources of defensibility. Transaction fees, long protected by the "facilitate" function's deliberate friction and by internal referral loops that route users deeper into an ecosystem (Google Search into Maps into YouTube; Amazon shoppers into Prime and Whole Foods), collapse because AI agents search across platforms, unbundle offerings, and instantly compare prices — converting the classic platform "winner-take-all" dynamic into "everyone loses together," since agents operate across all networks simultaneously rather than being channeled into any one platform's walled garden. Subscription lock-in, which depends on the same psychological sunk-cost biases (Amazon Prime's roughly 250 million paying members generated $44.37 billion in 2024) that a rational AI agent simply does not experience, unravels because an agent can instantly compare total delivered cost across providers regardless of which one a human happens to already be paying for. And ecosystem services (cloud, logistics, payments) that platforms bundle to deepen dependence get disaggregated, as agents optimize each service independently rather than accepting a bundled platform default. Most consequentially for this concept's data-driven-network-effects mechanism, AI agents also erode platforms' core personalization advantage: traditional platform personalization (Amazon's recommendation engine, Netflix's content curation) infers preference only from behavior observed within that platform's own walls, while a user's AI agent — granted access to email, calendar, cloud drives, and private conversations — discerns intent directly from context (a tight budget from bank notifications, a stressful work deadline from email tone) rather than inferring it from fragmented click data, producing a "vulnerable intimacy" and cross-context understanding no single platform's walled-garden data can replicate. The article's assessment of platform responses so far — legal and technical barriers (Amazon's successful injunction against Perplexity's Comet AI shopping agent), building proprietary agents to control the agentic layer directly (Amazon's "Buy for Me," Google's store-calling agents, Visa/Mastercard authentication protocols for autonomous purchases), and industry standards for agent interoperability (the Google/Shopify-led Universal Commerce Protocol, endorsed by Target, Walmart, Visa, and Mastercard) — reads as a direct real-world test of which of this concept's platform-design principles (core-interaction control, network-effect defensibility, ecosystem orchestration) can actually survive contact with an intermediary that has no incentive to preserve any platform's monetization model. Concrete adoption data suggests the shift is no longer speculative: Salesforce found AI agents influenced $67 billion in global Cyber Week 2025 sales (20% of all purchases), and Walmart's "Sparky" and Macy's Gemini-powered "Ask Macy's" assistants both report substantially higher order values among AI-assisted shoppers than unassisted ones. <span class="src">How AI Is Threatening Platforms’ Revenue Streams</span>
