## Summary

How Software as a Service and Platform as a Service actually work in practice — multi-tenancy, SaaS economics, and platform ecosystems. Also covers how PaaS's abstraction of the operating system enables agile development, DevOps, and application portability. A companion McKinsey explainer updates the SaaS market's current state, the generative-AI disruption it faces, and the product-led-growth and new-business-building strategies SaaS vendors use to keep growing.

## Concept

Software as a Service is fundamentally enabled by multi-tenancy: a single running software instance and infrastructure footprint serves many customers simultaneously, with each customer's data and configuration kept fully isolated and secured — including protection against other customers of the same SaaS application, not just external attackers. This differs structurally from single-tenant hosted software, where each customer runs their own dedicated instance. The advantages of multi-tenancy compound: customers avoid the capital expense of scaling their own infrastructure as usage grows (the SaaS vendor absorbs that cost across its whole customer base), ongoing maintenance and updates are bundled into the subscription rather than billed and scheduled separately, businesses can configure the application's behavior without touching the shared underlying code base (which keeps future upgrades simple, though heavier business-process customization does make upgrades harder), and — because a single technical glitch can affect every customer of a multi-tenant application simultaneously — the vendor has an unusually strong commercial incentive to invest heavily in uptime, continuity, and performance. Salesforce.com's history is used as the canonical illustration of how a SaaS business actually grows: founder Marc Benioff's "no software" pitch won over small businesses first, because the lack of capital expense and short (month-to-month) commitment made switching low-risk; large enterprises followed more slowly, often adopted first through sales and marketing expense budgets rather than formal IT procurement; and once Salesforce had a large paying customer base, it opened its platform to independent software vendors (ISVs) — branded first Force.com, later Customer 360 Platform — letting partners like Veeva Systems build vertical-specific applications (in Veeva's case, a pharmaceutical-industry CRM with built-in regulatory reporting) on top of Salesforce's infrastructure rather than building an entire application stack from scratch. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Ch.9 Using Software as a Service</span>

Delivering the "effectively unlimited resources" experience SaaS users have come to expect requires genuinely cloud-native architecture — a modular design built from microservices packaged in containers — rather than simply moving a legacy application onto cloud infrastructure unchanged; a lift-and-shift migration retains the legacy application's scaling limits even though it now runs on cloud hardware. Because SaaS applications are expected to be always available, including while new features are actively being deployed, this architectural requirement is closely tied to DevOps practice: continuous, incremental deployment replaces scheduled version releases, letting new features ship without user-facing downtime. On the economics side, SaaS restructures how software is paid for and adopted: entry costs are lower (free trials, incremental per-seat scaling rather than a large upfront capital purchase), maintenance and support are bundled into the ongoing subscription rather than billed and negotiated separately, and upgrades happen automatically and simultaneously for all customers — eliminating the costly, months-long upgrade projects common with on-premises software, at the cost of removing customers' ability to defer an upgrade or interface change they might have preferred to skip. A SaaS vendor that opens its platform to outside developers is, in effect, offering those developers a PaaS: independent software vendors build domain-specific applications on top of the SaaS vendor's existing infrastructure, APIs, and — critically — its established base of paying customers, described using a "food court" analogy (related businesses co-located in one ecosystem do better together than isolated, since the customers drawn to the ecosystem are already qualified prospects for every vendor inside it). This benefits both large ISVs seeking a faster path to establishing a cloud presence and small ISVs who could never afford to build an equivalent platform themselves, in exchange for a real dependency risk: if the underlying SaaS vendor changes its platform's behavior or APIs, dependent applications can break, which is why vetting a SaaS platform vendor's API stability and update-testing practices before building on it is treated as essential due diligence. <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). Platform as a Service is defined by what it removes from the developer's concern: rather than the developer selecting and managing an operating system (as in IaaS), the PaaS environment provides operating-system-level services — networking, file systems, I/O — as abstracted platform services instead, so applications no longer need OS-specific expertise or configuration and can, as a direct consequence, port more easily between different PaaS platforms than equivalent IaaS applications can port between different operating systems. This abstraction has two parts worth distinguishing: the platform itself (the software delivered to the organization) and its ongoing management (continuous monitoring, and seamless rollout of updates and new features without requiring attention from application owners) — and organizations choosing PaaS must decide whether to manage their own PaaS environment (more control, more operational burden) or use a vendor-managed one (more convenient, more generic). PaaS adoption is tightly coupled to two other practice shifts it enables and is enabled by: DevOps, which merges development and operations into one continuous practice so that the highest-priority customer-facing features can be built and deployed immediately rather than bundled into infrequent, numbered releases; and agile development, in which small cross-functional teams (developers, a product manager, sometimes an operations manager, a scrum master, and UX designers) work in short sprints (typically two to four weeks) to produce shippable increments, ideally staffed with "full-stack" developers who can work across the entire stack from server code to UI to middleware — together enabling some efficient agile teams to take a feature from customer request to live deployment in as little as two or three days. Three distinct PaaS variants serve different organizational needs: public PaaS (the fastest path to development and deployment, offered by large vendors like AWS Elastic Beanstalk, Azure App Service, Red Hat OpenShift, and Google App Engine, well suited to mass-market ambitions and to companies already using the same vendor's IaaS); private PaaS (installed inside a company's own data center, typically chosen by regulated industries whose auditors require in-person facility access that public cloud vendors won't grant, or by companies subject to data sovereignty rules requiring data to stay within a specific country's physical infrastructure); and open PaaS (built specifically on open-source software to avoid single-vendor lock-in and support a genuine multicloud strategy, trading off some of the convenience of a fully managed proprietary platform for lower cost and freedom from any one cloud implementation). <span class="src">Cloud Computing For Dummies (2nd Edition). <span class="src">What is SaaS</span> (McKinsey, January 2025) updates this concept's largely 2020-era mechanics with the SaaS market's current economic state and its own generative-AI disruption, directly addressing this concept's previously flagged gap around AI-native SaaS. The global SaaS market reached roughly $3 trillion by 2022 after nearly a decade of software-industry market capitalization growing at double the broader market's rate — a trajectory that once suggested the market could reach $10 trillion by 2030 — but growth has since slowed as enterprises cut IT spending in response to rising interest rates and cash-flow pressure, pushing boards and management teams to prioritize the question "what is the best use of capital?" (Box CEO Aaron Levie's framing of the shift from pure growth toward profitability and operating-margin discipline, eventually adopted via the Rule of 40 — a SaaS company's growth rate plus free cash flow rate should reach at least 40% — the same benchmark this vault's companion concept on Rule-of-40 research treats in depth). Compounding this slowdown, generative AI has disrupted the software industry even faster and more thoroughly than SaaS itself once did: large global enterprises spent roughly $15 billion on gen AI solutions in 2023 alone, reaching about 2% of the global enterprise software market within a single year, a share SaaS itself took four years to reach after its own emergence. McKinsey expects gen AI to make software customers switch vendors more frequently as they chase the newest capability, meaning SaaS companies must simultaneously keep pace with their own application development and actively defend existing client relationships rather than treating subscription renewal as assured — software leaders are advised to ask whether they're moving fast enough, sufficiently reimagining their software category rather than just adding gen AI features, reallocating enough R&D spend (a 10-20% gen-AI revenue contribution within five years should draw a comparable R&D share), and revisiting pricing, packaging, and proprietary-data strategy for the gen AI era. On go-to-market motion specifically, the source finds product-led growth (PLG) — keeping the product itself, rather than a salesperson, at the center of customer acquisition, retention, and expansion, pioneered by SaaS innovators Atlassian and Slack — is rarely successful in isolation; the more durable pattern combines PLG with traditional sales-led growth (SLG) into a hybrid called product-led sales (PLS), which helps incumbent, traditionally sales-led enterprises reach small and medium customers more cheaply while still letting larger prospects experience a product's value before committing to a big contract. Finally, on building genuinely new SaaS revenue from an existing (often non-software) business, the source finds only 6% of global software revenue is currently earned by nontech companies despite widespread ambition to change that, and identifies six success factors: iterating rapidly with real test-and-learn cycles rather than polishing a product in isolation; capitalizing on data and digital assets the company already has (Anheuser-Busch InBev built a B2B SaaS e-commerce platform for retailers directly from its own data analysis); planning for scale with a detailed roadmap and flexible architecture from day one rather than retrofitting it later; recruiting SaaS-specific talent from expanded pools like open-source communities and hackathons, since SaaS talent is scarce; tethering pricing and selling directly to observed product-usage data rather than generic tiers; and protecting the new business's funding through the period before it delivers visible results, since typical metrics like margin and revenue can mislead too early relative to signals like partner and lead interest. <span class="src">What is SaaS</span>

## Open Questions

- The original source (2020) predates the mainstream rise of AI-native SaaS products built directly on foundation-model APIs; the companion 2025 McKinsey source addresses the market-level disruption this creates but doesn't revisit whether multi-tenancy or PaaS-abstraction mechanics themselves need updating for AI-native architectures specifically.
- The companion source's PLG/PLS and new-SaaS-business guidance is aimed primarily at large enterprises with existing scale and data assets; neither source addresses how a resource-constrained startup should sequence these six success factors when it can't pursue all of them simultaneously.
