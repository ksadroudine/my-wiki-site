## Summary

DeepSeek's January 2025 release shocked Western markets by matching leading US chatbots at a fraction of the training cost, but management theory treats it as predictable classic disruption rather than a fluke. It reflects a broader, purpose-built Chinese AI ecosystem built on customization, cost leadership and real-world calibration, which global companies now need a deliberate dual-track strategy to engage with.

## Concept

DeepSeek's emergence as a household name traces to a specific technical claim that upended the prevailing assumption about how AI progress works. Since OpenAI's ChatGPT launched the current AI boom in late 2022, the dominant belief had been that only the handful of companies able to invest billions of dollars in specialized chips — Microsoft, Google, Meta — could build genuinely leading-edge AI systems. When DeepSeek released its DeepSeek-V3 model in December 2024, matching the capability of the best US chatbots, that alone was notable; when the company's accompanying research paper revealed it had trained the system using only about 2,000 Nvidia chips (versus the 16,000 or more typically used by leading labs) and roughly $6 million in raw computing power — about a tenth of what Meta reportedly spent on its comparable system — the reaction was immediate and severe: tech stocks tumbled, and Nvidia lost more single-day stock market value than any company in history. The panic intensified on January 20, 2025, when DeepSeek released its reasoning model, R1, which also impressed experts and pushed the concern from "a capable chatbot" to "a capable reasoning system," the category widely seen as AI's near-term frontier. Venture capitalist Marc Andreessen called it "AI's Sputnik moment." Technically, DeepSeek's efficiency came from a "mixture of experts" approach that spreads data analysis across multiple specialized sub-models while minimizing the computational cost of moving data between them — not a wholly novel idea, but one DeepSeek's engineers executed with unusual skill, leading one AI efficiency researcher to note plainly that "DeepSeek used methods that anyone can duplicate," undercutting any notion of a secret proprietary breakthrough. DeepSeek's decision to open-source its models under a permissive MIT license compounded the disruption: this extended a trend Meta had started with Llama in 2023, but for the first time demonstrated that a genuinely competitive open-source AI ecosystem could emerge from outside the American tech giants, raising a pointed strategic question for US policymakers debating whether to restrict open-source AI releases domestically over safety concerns — since doing so, critics argued, risked ceding the entire open-source AI ecosystem to Chinese developers, with US researchers and companies then building their own systems atop Chinese foundations rather than the reverse. Notably, US chip export controls — intended specifically to constrain China's AI development by limiting advanced Nvidia chip sales — appear to have had the opposite of their intended effect in this instance, forcing DeepSeek's engineers toward the kind of resource-constrained architectural creativity that produced their efficiency gains in the first place. <span class="src">What is DeepSeek And How Is It Upending A.I.</span>

Harvard Business School researchers argued within days of the DeepSeek shock that the surprise itself was avoidable — that classic disruption theory, developed decades earlier to explain phenomena like electric arc furnace "mini-mills" displacing integrated steel plants, predicted almost exactly this pattern: an initially inferior, low-cost alternative, dismissed by incumbents as unable to serve high-end customers, steadily improves and erodes the incumbent's market share segment by segment until the disruption is complete. Chinese LLMs generally, not just DeepSeek specifically, differ from their American counterparts in two structural ways that fit this disruption pattern closely: first, they typically rely on cheaper, less powerful, distributed training hardware — partly a direct consequence of US export controls limiting access to top-tier chips — combined with genuinely more efficient model architecture (DeepSeek's Multi-Head Latent Attention and Mixture-of-Experts designs specifically reduce memory usage to make better use of the more limited compute available); second, many Chinese LLMs are deliberately engineered for narrower, domain-specific applications rather than pursuing the general-purpose ambitions of leading US models, allowing Chinese AI giants — Alibaba, Tencent, Baidu, ByteDance — and startups alike to ship industry-specific applications deeply integrated into China's own digital ecosystems. The resulting price differential is stark: Alibaba's Qwen Plus and ByteDance's Doubao 1.5-pro were priced under $0.30 per million output tokens, compared with more than $60 for OpenAI's o1 and Anthropic's Claude 3.5 Opus at the time. For corporate boardrooms confronting the resulting question of whether to license American or Chinese LLMs (or both), the researchers draw on established management theory around technological diversification to lay out three viable strategies: multi-sourcing, which diversifies against any single provider's downtime and enables "ensembling" — aggregating predictions across multiple models and providers, an approach research has found improves output quality specifically on complex, ambiguous tasks (illustrated by aggregator platforms like Openrouter, which let users compare more than 180 models' performance and cost in real time); single-sourcing, which reduces administrative overhead and builds deeper mutual understanding between a company and its one AI supplier, at the cost of full exposure to that supplier's downtime and, when the supplier is foreign, added data-privacy and cross-border regulatory complexity (a particular concern in sensitive domains like healthcare); and "plural governance," a third option drawn from prior management research on technological diversification, combining external AI suppliers with internally-developed capability — for instance, using a Western LLM for general-purpose research tasks (aiding consultants or lawyers) while deploying an internally-tuned Chinese LLM for narrower, company-specific applications (an HR onboarding chatbot, for example). The researchers caution that American AI companies dismiss the Chinese LLM disruption threat, or the parallel threat from resource-light small language models, at their own peril — self-disruption (deliberately cannibalizing one's own premium offering with a cheaper alternative, as GE did by developing its own lower-cost handheld ultrasound device) is a theoretically available defense but empirically very difficult to execute, not least because sunk costs already invested in expensive chips, hardware, and training data, plus strong incentives to keep selling high-margin solutions, tend to tether incumbent companies to their existing high-end offerings rather than cannibalizing them proactively. <span class="src">Why DeepSeek Shouldn’t Have Been a Surprise</span>

Much of the initial DeepSeek coverage carried an undertone of accusation — specifically, that DeepSeek had obtained proprietary knowledge from OpenAI's closed-source o1 model without permission via a technique called distillation, framed by much of the press as evidence DeepSeek had discovered some new, illicit shortcut to efficient AI. Quanta Magazine's explainer corrects this framing directly: knowledge distillation, in which a smaller "student" model is trained to approximate a larger "teacher" model's behavior at a fraction of the cost, is a decade-old, extremely well-established technique in AI research, not a novel or inherently improper one — "one of the most important tools that companies have today to make models more efficient," in the words of one researcher who studies the technique. The idea traces to a 2015 paper by three Google researchers, including Geoffrey Hinton (later a 2024 Nobel laureate), who were seeking a more efficient alternative to the then-common practice of running expensive "ensembles" of multiple models glued together to improve performance. Their key insight was that a model's wrong answers aren't all equally wrong — confusing a dog with a fox is a smaller error than confusing a dog with a pizza — and that a large "teacher" model's full probability distribution across possible answers (its "soft targets," which Hinton evocatively called "dark knowledge," by analogy to cosmological dark matter) contains far richer information than just its single best answer. By training a smaller "student" model on these soft targets rather than only on hard, final answers, researchers found they could compress a large, complex model down to a much leaner one with barely any loss of accuracy. The technique wasn't an immediate hit — the original paper was initially rejected from a conference — but it arrived at exactly the moment neural networks were exploding in size and cost as researchers discovered more training data reliably improved performance; Google's 2018 BERT language model, powering billions of web searches but expensive to run at scale, was itself distilled into the smaller, widely-adopted DistilBERT the following year, and distillation is now offered as a managed service by Google, OpenAI, and Amazon alike, with the original 2015 paper cited more than 25,000 times. Crucially, distillation in its classic form requires access to a teacher model's internal probability outputs, which makes the specific accusation against DeepSeek — that it covertly distilled OpenAI's fully closed-source o1 model without permission — technically implausible in the traditional sense; what remains genuinely possible, and much harder to detect or prevent, is a "Socratic" variant in which a student model is trained purely on a teacher model's answers to systematically prompted questions, without ever touching the teacher's internals. The technique's ongoing relevance extends well beyond the DeepSeek controversy: researchers at UC Berkeley's NovaSky lab recently showed distillation works effectively even for training complex chain-of-thought reasoning models (multistep "thinking" systems built to answer harder questions), producing their fully open-source Sky-T1 model for under $450 in training cost while achieving results comparable to a much larger open-source model — evidence, as one of the researchers put it, that "distillation is a fundamental technique in AI," not a shortcut peculiar to any one company or country. <span class="src">How Distillation Makes AI Models Smaller and Cheaper</span>

A later HBR analysis, published after the initial DeepSeek shock had settled into sustained strategic attention, argues that DeepSeek is best understood as one visible instance of a much larger, deliberately distinct Chinese generative AI ecosystem — not an isolated anomaly to be reacted to and then set aside. Drawing on executive interviews, performance benchmarks, and case studies conducted at IMD, the researchers propose a "3C framework" distinguishing this ecosystem across the three layers of the gen AI stack (infrastructure, intelligence/LLMs, and application/output): customization, cost leadership, and calibration. Customization in infrastructure reflects Chinese firms' preference for vertically integrated, end-to-end technology stacks over the more decentralized, multi-vendor ecosystem typical of US AI development — Huawei, for instance, built its own deep-learning framework, MindSpore, specifically to run on its own Ascend AI chips, giving it lower-cost customization ability than a company assembling AI capability from disparate, generalized vendors; Ant Group's AI doctor agents, available through its Alipay app, illustrate the same principle applied to an entire vertical, trained not just on clinical literature and structured diagnostic data but explicitly on the decision-making logic of top Chinese physicians, achieving a contextual depth general-purpose models like ChatGPT struggle to match. Cost leadership in model development reflects a genuine mindset difference the researchers observed repeatedly in interviews: where Western firms tend to pursue cutting-edge infrastructure and models on the theory that business results will eventually follow, Chinese firms more often treat models and infrastructure explicitly as a means to a predetermined business outcome, leaning on mature existing AI solutions rather than building from scratch wherever feasible — a mindset made viable by China's vast domestic market and its handful of centralized AI solution suppliers, and one the researchers note has been aided by Chinese firms directly observing both the successes and the costly failures of Western competitors like OpenAI, Google, and Meta along the way. Calibration for real-world applications reflects an obsession with practical effectiveness over theoretical elegance, illustrated by Moonshot AI's Kimi model, which in March 2024 became the first AI system to process up to 2 million Chinese characters within a single conversation — a milestone the researchers frame not as a narrow technical achievement but as a capability deliberately calibrated for document-heavy real-world use cases across healthcare, education, and customer service. This entire ecosystem, the researchers note, developed against a backdrop of intensifying export controls specifically intended to constrain it — controls that instead appear to have catalyzed rather than crippled Chinese AI development: more than 300 gen AI services are now registered with the Cyberspace Administration of China, and Huawei fast-tracked its homegrown Ascend chip series specifically as a domestic alternative to restricted Nvidia hardware, with Ascend chips now powering national-scale Chinese data centers. For global executives, the researchers' central recommendation is a deliberate "dual-track" strategy rather than viewing the choice as East-versus-West: Western tools remain well suited to general-purpose research and maximum flexibility, while Chinese tools increasingly offer superior cost-efficiency, faster deployment, and better real-world calibration for domain-specific applications — but pursuing this dual-track approach requires navigating genuine complexity, since Western technology and infrastructure are already difficult to access within China, and Chinese AI vendors face rising scrutiny in the US, requiring more rigorous due diligence spanning chip sourcing reviews, cybersecurity audits, and alignment with evolving local AI governance requirements on both sides. <span class="src">How Savvy Companies Are Using Chinese AI</span>

## Open Questions

- None of the four sources addresses how durable DeepSeek's specific cost advantage is likely to prove as Western labs adopt similar efficiency techniques (mixture-of-experts, distillation) themselves — it's unclear whether the cost gap between Chinese and Western frontier models will persist, narrow, or reverse over the following months and years.
- The "plural governance" and "dual-track" strategic recommendations from the two HBR pieces aren't reconciled with the data-privacy and cross-border regulatory concerns both pieces separately raise — neither source specifies how a company should decide which specific workflows are safe to route to a Chinese LLM provider versus which carry unacceptable data-sovereignty risk.
