## Summary

Two complementary personal knowledge management methodologies — Tiago Forte's CODE/PARA system, and Niklas Luhmann's slip-box (Zettelkasten), as documented by Sönke Ahrens. Both are for capturing, organizing, and connecting notes so that writing and idea generation emerge from an external system rather than unaided memory.

## Highlights

- A "Second Brain" is built through a four-step method called CODE — Capture (keep only what resonates), Organize (save for actionability, not by topic), Distill (progressively find each note's essence), and Express (share work in small, reusable chunks) — that runs both when first building the system and every time it is used afterward. <span class="src">Building a Second Brain</span>
- Notes should be organized not by where information came from but by where it is going: the PARA system sorts everything into Projects (short-term efforts with a deadline and defined outcome), Areas (long-term standards to maintain), Resources (topics that may be useful later), and Archives (anything inactive from the other three). <span class="src">Building a Second Brain</span>
- Progressive Summarization distills a note across several layers of highlighting, with each layer including no more than 10-20 percent of the previous layer's content, so that a note becomes more discoverable and more essential each time it is revisited rather than being fully processed once and left alone. <span class="src">Building a Second Brain</span>
- Reusable "Intermediate Packets" — distilled notes, outtakes, work-in-progress, finished deliverables, or documents from others — let creative work be assembled from previously created building blocks instead of starting from a blank page each time, and make interrupted work resumable. <span class="src">Building a Second Brain</span>
- Niklas Luhmann's slip-box (Zettelkasten) separates three note types that must be kept apart to build a critical mass of ideas: fleeting notes (discarded within a day or two), permanent notes (written so they remain understandable even once the original context is forgotten, and never thrown away), and project notes (relevant only to one active project, archived or discarded when it ends). <span class="src">How to Take Smart Notes</span>
- Notes in a slip-box are filed and cross-referenced by where you would want to rediscover them in the future — asked from a writer's perspective ("in which circumstances will I want to stumble upon this?") — rather than filed by topic, which the source argues inevitably leads either to overcrowded categories or to an unmanageable proliferation of subcategories. <span class="src">How to Take Smart Notes</span>
- Writing a permanent note that translates an idea into one's own words, and then explicitly linking it to related existing notes, is itself the core intellectual work of research — not a preparatory step before "real" thinking, but the medium in which the thinking actually happens. <span class="src">How to Take Smart Notes</span>
- Both systems share an underlying claim about attention and willpower: because attention, short-term memory, and decision-making capacity are all limited and depleting resources, an external, trusted system that reliably holds unfinished thoughts is what frees the mind to focus on generating and connecting ideas rather than remembering them. <span class="src">How to Take Smart Notes</span>

## Concept

Building a Second Brain and How to Take Smart Notes describe two independently-developed but strongly convergent methodologies for personal knowledge management, both built on the premise that reliable external note systems — rather than unaided memory or willpower — are what actually enable sustained creative and intellectual output. Tiago Forte's CODE method structures the practice around four repeatable steps: Capture, which keeps only what genuinely resonates rather than trying to save everything encountered, filtered by intuitive criteria (does it inspire, is it useful, is it personal, is it surprising) rather than exhaustive analytical review; Organize, which files captured material according to actionability toward current goals rather than by topical category, using the PARA system (Projects, Areas, Resources, Archives) as a universal four-way sort that applies to any kind of information regardless of source; Distill, which progressively highlights a note down through multiple layers — each new layer keeping only 10-20 percent of the material in the layer beneath it — so that revisiting a note makes it more discoverable rather than merely re-reading it in full each time; and Express, which treats notes as reusable "Intermediate Packets" (distilled notes, outtakes, work-in-progress fragments, finished deliverables, or others' documents) that can be assembled into larger projects, letting creative work resume from wherever it was left off instead of restarting from a blank page. <span class="src">Building a Second Brain</span>

Sönke Ahrens's account of Niklas Luhmann's slip-box (Zettelkasten) arrives at a structurally similar discipline from a different starting point — Luhmann's own decades of prolific academic writing, which he attributed largely to his note system functioning as a genuine "dialogue partner" rather than a passive archive. The slip-box separates notes into three categories that must be kept apart for the system to work: fleeting notes, quick reminders captured in any format and reviewed within a day or discarded; permanent notes, written in complete sentences so they remain intelligible even once their original source context is forgotten, and never deleted; and project notes, relevant only to a specific active project and archived or discarded once that project ends. Crucially, notes are filed and interlinked not by topic — which Ahrens argues inevitably forces an impossible choice between overcrowded single categories or an unmanageable proliferation of subcategories — but by the writer's own judgment of where they might usefully be rediscovered in future, reframed as the question "in which circumstances will I want to stumble upon this note?" rather than the archivist's question "under which keyword does this belong?" <span class="src">How to Take Smart Notes</span>

Both methodologies converge on the claim that the act of writing a note in one's own words — not the act of reading, highlighting, or filing — is where the actual thinking happens: translating an idea into permanent-note form, and then deliberately linking it to existing related notes, is presented not as record-keeping ahead of "real" writing but as the substance of the intellectual work itself, since it forces explicit judgments about how a new idea relates to or challenges what is already known. Both also share an explicit psychological rationale grounded in the same resource-scarcity logic: attention can only be directed at one thing at a time, short-term memory holds only a handful of items, and willpower depletes with use, so an external system that reliably captures unfinished thoughts and open questions is what actually frees the mind to focus on generating and connecting new ideas, rather than expending its limited capacity on remembering what has already been thought. <span class="src">How to Take Smart Notes</span>
