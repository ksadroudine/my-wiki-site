## Summary

An argument that AI safety cannot be built into a model alone, because a model deployed without context lacks the information needed to distinguish malicious from benign use. Defenses against misuse must therefore target the downstream attack surface, not the model itself.

## Highlights

- The core claim is that AI safety questions generally can't be meaningfully asked or answered at the level of a model alone, since safety depends on the specific context and environment a model is deployed in — a claim the authors contrast with the pervasive, rarely-stated industry assumption that safety is an intrinsic model property. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>
- Phishing emails illustrate the problem directly: an LLM asked to write a persuasive, urgent email cannot distinguish a phishing attempt from legitimate marketing copy, because the malicious part (a malicious link or attachment) is content the model was never given access to — so the only way to block phishing at the model level is to block email-writing generally, which destroys the tool's legitimate uses. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>
- The same pattern recurs for bioweapon-related information (a model can't distinguish general synthetic-biology questions from bioterrorism-specific ones) and disinformation (even a model that never generates false information can be used to generate accurate content that a bad actor then wraps in misleading context, and true-but-misleading information has been found to be up to 50x more impactful than false information for outcomes like vaccine hesitancy). <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>
- A few narrow exceptions are acknowledged where content is intrinsically problematic regardless of use — AI-generated child sexual abuse material and memorized copyrighted output — where model-level refusal is judged to make sense despite the general argument. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>
- Four recommendations follow: defenses against misuse should be located primarily outside the model, at the downstream attack surface (e.g. email scanners and URL blacklists rather than model refusal); the debate over open vs. closed model release should focus on rigorously assessed *marginal* risk relative to existing defenses and technologies, not release strategy in the abstract; red teaming should be refocused from asking "can this model be misused?" (the answer is always yes) toward tracking the advancing frontier of adversary capability as an early-warning system; and red teaming should be led by third parties with better-aligned incentives, since developers have a structural disincentive to discover that their own model enables serious misuse they can't fix. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>
- The essay's closing argument is that the "safety is a model property" framing persists partly because it is administratively convenient — it lets companies claim a model is safe enough to release and gives regulators and courts a cleaner liability question — whereas the actual picture, where responsibility is distributed across models, deployment context, and downstream defenses, is much messier to assign accountability for. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>

## Concept

The central argument is a direct challenge to what the authors describe as a pervasive, rarely-examined assumption in the AI safety field: that safety is a property of the model itself, checkable and fixable through techniques like alignment training and red teaming applied to the model in isolation. Because of this assumption, companies invest heavily in pre-release red teaming, researchers work to patch the brittleness of alignment techniques, some safety advocates push to restrict open-weight models on the theory they pose unique risk, and policymakers search for a training-compute threshold above which intervention becomes justified (landing, somewhat arbitrarily, on 10^26 FLOPs for lack of any principled basis to pick a different number). The authors argue these efforts are inherently limited, because — with a few specific exceptions — AI safety questions cannot be meaningfully asked or answered at the model level alone: safety depends on the context and environment a model is deployed in, and a particular deployment context has to be specified before a safety question is even well-formed. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>

Three examples illustrate why context, not the model, is where the actual safety-relevant information lives. Phishing emails are, structurally, just persuasive emails — nothing about the text of a well-crafted urgent email is intrinsically malicious; what makes it a phishing attack is the destination of a link or the payload of an attachment, content the model generating the email was never given access to in the first place. The only way to make a model refuse to generate phishing emails at the model level is to make it refuse to generate persuasive emails generally, which would break the tool for entirely legitimate uses like marketing. The same structural problem recurs with concerns about LLMs helping bioterrorists: most of the information relevant to synthetic biology is already publicly available (a claim the authors source to RAND), and the genuinely hard parts of executing a bioweapon attack — sourcing materials, safely culturing pathogens, disseminating them — lie almost entirely outside what a model could meaningfully gate, since nearly all the model's actual assistance would be indistinguishable from ordinary bioengineering education. Disinformation shows the same pattern from a different angle: even a hypothetically perfect model that refuses every direct request to generate false information remains useful to a disinformation campaign, because research has found that true-but-misleading information is far more effective at things like increasing vaccine hesitancy than outright false information — as much as 50x more effective in one cited study — so an adversary can simply have the model generate accurate content (e.g. an accurate summary of a real news story) and supply the misleading framing separately, entirely outside the model's view. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>

The authors are careful to bound the scope of the argument. It applies most directly to *misuse* — both malicious misuse (the examples above) and non-malicious misuse like students using a model to cheat on homework, where the model again lacks the context (whether a given task is actually a homework assignment) needed to distinguish acceptable from unacceptable use. Other categories of AI safety failure — bias and toxicity, accidents, reward hacking, adversarial inputs like prompt injection — are explicitly noted as different problems the argument applies to "less strongly," without a full separate analysis being offered. A small number of misuse cases are treated as genuine exceptions where the argument doesn't hold: content that is intrinsically problematic regardless of how it's used, such as AI-generated child sexual abuse material (where the model, unlike with phishing, isn't lacking necessary context — the content itself is the harm, not something downstream) and models reproducing memorized copyrighted material verbatim. For these categories, refusing at the model level is judged to make sense despite the general pattern. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>

Four recommendations follow from accepting the core argument. First, defenses against misuse should be located primarily outside the model, targeting the downstream attack surface where malicious use actually becomes harmful — the given example is that the best defenses against phishing, whether the email was written by a human or an LLM, remain email scanners and URL blacklists, technologies that have existed and steadily improved for roughly two decades, rather than trying to make the generating model itself refuse. Relying on model alignment instead is predicted to produce both false negatives (misuse the model fails to prevent) and false positives (the model refusing genuinely innocuous requests, acting as an "overzealous censor"), since the model lacks the contextual information to correctly distinguish the two. Second, the debate over open versus closed model release strategies should be reframed around rigorously assessed *marginal* risk — the additional risk a given model's release adds relative to existing models and non-AI technology, accounting for defenses that may already exist outside the model — rather than treating openness itself as inherently more or less safe; the authors cite their own contribution to a framework doing exactly this, which found low marginal cybersecurity risk from open models (for automated vulnerability detection specifically) but substantial marginal risk for enabling non-consensual intimate imagery. Third, red teaming should be refocused: since the answer to "can this model be misused?" is essentially always yes, red teaming's more useful function is tracking the advancing frontier of what adversary capabilities current models actually enable (e.g. whether a model can now automate an entire multi-step cyberattack chain), functioning as an early-warning system that informs what downstream defenses need to be built, rather than as a gate on whether a specific model should ship. Fourth, red teaming aimed at this kind of misuse-capability discovery should be led by third parties with incentives distinct from the model developer's own, because a developer has a structural disincentive to build and disclose the most effective possible offensive pipeline using their own model: doing so successfully would force them to publicly admit they're releasing something they know can be misused with no way to prevent it, whereas a company that simply doesn't look as hard avoids ever having to make that admission. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>

The essay closes by suggesting why the "safety as a model property" framing has persisted despite these problems: it would be genuinely convenient for everyone involved if it were true, since it would let companies make a clean, technical determination of whether a model is "safe enough" to release, let researchers apply a familiar toolkit of technical alignment methods, and give courts and regulators a comparatively clean liability question (companies should be liable for harms only if model-level safety guarantees actually fail, not otherwise). Accepting instead that there is no purely technical fix for misuse risk means responsibility is distributed messily across models, deployment context, and downstream defenses, with no current consensus on how to allocate liability across that distribution — a genuinely harder problem than the one the "safety is a model property" framing lets everyone pretend they're solving. <span class="src">AI safety is not a model property</span>

## Open Questions

- The essay does not fully address who should bear the cost of building and maintaining the downstream defenses (email scanners, content-provenance systems) it recommends as the primary line of defense, beyond noting that developers should morally bear some of this cost without currently having a legal mechanism that requires it.
