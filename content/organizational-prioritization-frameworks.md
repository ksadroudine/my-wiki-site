## Summary

Individuals and organizations are systematically poor at prioritizing, because people struggle to give up goals even when pursuing all of them makes success less likely, and companies accumulate so many priorities that attention is spread too thin, which is known as the peanut-butter problem. Indebted consumers, for example, pay off small debts first because progress feels tangible, even though that costs more. The concept surveys common frameworks, including the Eisenhower matrix of urgent and important tasks, RICE scoring, MoSCoW and the 70:20:10 rule. It also stresses that priorities must be communicated, since a 2017 study found that in a typical company only about half of senior executives could recite their own firm's strategic priorities, and that deliberately deprioritizing, meaning actively stopping something, matters as much as the choice of framework.

## Concept

Prioritization is treated as one of the most consequential and most consistently mishandled skills in organizational life, and the evidence suggests the difficulty isn't primarily about knowing which framework to apply — it's a deeper behavioral bias toward pursuing goals regardless of whether doing so is actually rational. In a controlled lab experiment, participants asked to pursue two competing goals kept trying to achieve both even when the monetary reward for completing just one goal equaled the reward for completing both, and even though splitting effort across both goals made it statistically less likely that either one would actually be completed — people appear to be motivated by the pursuit of any achievable goal, independent of whether pursuing it serves their actual interests. A closely related bias shows achievability, not value, often drives what people choose to work on next: indebted consumers were found to prioritize paying off small debts ahead of larger debts carrying higher interest rates, choosing the tangible, immediate sense of progress over the objectively better financial outcome, and the same pattern shows up constantly in office settings — being, say, the 400th person to review a shared document adds essentially no value to the organization, but it still lets the reviewer feel a concrete sense of accomplishment for the day. <span class="src">Too many people are shockingly bad at prioritisation</span>

Organizations compound this individual-level bias with a structural version of the same problem, described as the "peanut-butter problem": firms accumulate so many nominal priorities that attention and resources end up spread too thin across all of them to make real progress on any. Some of this stems directly from leadership behavior — some executives treat everything they ask for as a priority, and even those who don't may find employees behaving as though every request carries equal weight regardless of what's actually said. A concrete illustration comes from Lego: when Niels Christiansen became CEO, he discovered the company was formally tracking 100 separate "key enterprise risks" at once, and posed the obvious structural question directly — "How can you look after 100 different risks without being risk-averse on everything you do?" — since genuinely prioritizing requires accepting that most things, even legitimately important things, simply won't get top attention. A range of established frameworks exist to counter both the individual and organizational versions of this problem. The Eisenhower matrix, a classic individual time-management tool, sorts tasks into four quadrants by urgency and importance: urgent-and-important tasks go to the front of the queue, important-but-non-urgent tasks need deliberately protected time (since they're the ones most likely to get crowded out by urgency), and tasks that are neither urgent nor important are candidates for simply not doing. The action-priority matrix applies a similar quadrant logic based on impact and effort instead of urgency and importance. Product teams commonly use RICE (reach, impact, confidence, and effort) as a more granular scoring model, while MoSCoW (must-have, should-have, could-have, won't-have) gives teams a shared vocabulary for distinguishing feature priority levels. At the portfolio level, Google popularized the 70:20:10 rule for allocating resources to innovation specifically: 70 percent to the core business, 20 percent to activities adjacent to it, and 10 percent to genuinely new, unproven ideas. <span class="src">Too many people are shockingly bad at prioritisation</span>

Choosing a framework, however, solves only part of the problem, since establishing what matters is not the same as ensuring an organization actually acts on it consistently. A 2017 study asked senior executives at 124 different organizations to simply recite their own company's stated strategic priorities, and found that in the typical organization, only about half of its own leadership team agreed on what those priorities actually were — meaning the communication of priorities, not merely their selection, is itself a distinct and commonly unmet organizational challenge. The step most consistently skipped, though, is deliberate deprioritization: genuinely prioritizing something new almost always requires actively stopping or deprioritizing something else, not simply adding to an already-overloaded list, and doing this well requires explicit rules decided in advance rather than relying on someone finding the political courage to kill a project in the moment. Amazon executives Phil Le-Brun and Jana Werner, in their book "The Octopus Organisation," recommend building explicit "go/kill" decision points directly into project plans, and defining objective "kill criteria" for ongoing initiatives — for instance, a software project might carry predefined thresholds like team attrition rates or cumulative time commitment that automatically trigger a stop/continue review, removing the decision from someone's individual willingness to advocate for cancellation after the fact. The overall discipline this points toward is best summarized as three linked actions rather than one: choosing what matters, communicating that choice clearly enough that leadership actually agrees on it, and stopping the things that lose out — with the observation that most organizational prioritization failures trace back to skipping the second or third step even when the first (picking a framework) has been handled reasonably well. <span class="src">Too many people are shockingly bad at prioritisation</span>
