## Summary

User-driven AI assistants differ from web crawlers because they fetch a page only when a specific user asks for it, whereas crawlers systematically visit and index the web whether or not anyone requested the content. Perplexity argues that infrastructure providers such as Cloudflare wrongly treat the two the same way, as in their 2025 dispute over Cloudflare's accusation of stealth crawling. If providers cannot tell a helpful assistant from a malicious scraper, the result could be a two-tiered internet in which access is controlled by a few infrastructure companies. Perplexity also points to Google, which already treats its search crawler differently from fetchers that act on a user's behalf.

## Concept

The argument draws a categorical distinction between two kinds of automated web access that the source contends are being wrongly conflated as infrastructure providers write policy for the AI era. Traditional crawlers — the historical, well-understood category, used for search indexing, link checking, or bulk scraping — systematically visit large numbers of pages to build a persistent database, independent of whether any specific person has asked for any of that specific content at that moment; the crawl happens on the crawler operator's own schedule and initiative. User-driven AI agents, by contrast, fetch a page only as the direct, immediate consequence of a specific person asking a specific question that requires current information the AI doesn't already have — the source's example is a query like "what are the latest reviews for that restaurant," where the AI has no pre-existing answer sitting in a database and instead goes to the relevant page, reads it, and returns a summary tailored to that exact question. Per Perplexity's account of its own systems, the fetched content is used immediately to answer that one request and is not stored afterward or used to train models — a real-time, on-demand access pattern structurally different from the store-everything-regardless-of-demand pattern of a crawler. The analogy offered for why this distinction matters is that a user-driven AI assistant functions "just like a human assistant": a human assistant asked to look something up doesn't already know the answer, and looking it up on the requester's behalf to complete a specific task is not treated as suspicious activity when a human does it manually — the argument is that the automation of this same lookup shouldn't change its legitimacy. <span class="src">Agents or Bots Making Sense of AI on the Open Web</span>

The source argues this isn't a novel distinction requiring new policy from scratch, but one that search engines have already established precedent for: Google's crawler that builds its search index is treated as functionally different from Google's "user-triggered fetchers" — for instance, generating a preview of a link a user is about to click — with the latter category prioritizing the requesting user's immediate experience over a site's robots.txt crawling restrictions, specifically because the fetch is being made on that particular user's behalf in response to their particular action, not autonomously by the search engine's own initiative. The argument is that AI assistants like Perplexity fall squarely into this second, already-accepted category rather than the first. <span class="src">Agents or Bots Making Sense of AI on the Open Web</span>

This framing is presented as a direct response to a specific 2025 dispute: Cloudflare publicly accused Perplexity of "stealth crawling," alleging it used hidden bots and identity impersonation to evade website access restrictions, and published a technical diagram purporting to show Perplexity's crawling workflow. Perplexity's rebuttal disputes the factual basis of the accusation on two grounds: first, that Cloudflare declined to disclose the methodology behind its claims for outside scrutiny, and second, that the likely actual explanation is a basic traffic-attribution error — Cloudflare appears to have misattributed 3-6 million daily requests originating from BrowserBase, a third-party cloud browser service that Perplexity uses only occasionally for specialized tasks (accounting for fewer than 45,000 daily requests on Perplexity's own account), to Perplexity's systems instead. Perplexity frames this as a serious credibility problem for Cloudflare specifically because traffic classification and analysis is Cloudflare's own core business, and offers two possible explanations without adjudicating between them: either a deliberate publicity opportunity built around a customer's name, or a genuine analytical failure in Cloudflare's own traffic-categorization systems. <span class="src">Agents or Bots Making Sense of AI on the Open Web</span>

The broader stake the source identifies goes beyond this one vendor dispute: if a major infrastructure provider sitting between websites and their visitors cannot reliably distinguish a helpful, user-directed AI assistant from a malicious scraper, the practical consequence is a form of overblocking that harms real users with legitimate needs — the examples given are someone researching a medical condition, comparing product reviews, or trying to access news from multiple sources through an AI assistant, all of whom lose access if their chosen tool gets misclassified and blocked. Structurally, the source argues this produces a two-tiered internet in which a person's actual access to information depends not on their genuine needs but on whether their particular AI tool has been "blessed" as legitimate by whichever infrastructure controller happens to sit in the traffic path — a dynamic explicitly framed as undermining user choice and disproportionately disadvantaging newer, innovative AI services trying to compete against already-established incumbents who may have more established relationships with the same infrastructure gatekeepers. <span class="src">Agents or Bots Making Sense of AI on the Open Web</span>
