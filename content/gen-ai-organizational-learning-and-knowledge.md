## Summary

The real organizational value of generative AI lies less in automating individual tasks than in transforming how knowledge and learning move through the enterprise. Three research teams propose complementary lenses, namely a capability-maturity framework built around the work of words, images, numbers and sounds, a workflow-embedding framework that turns static knowledge repositories into living systems, and a collective-intelligence framework. An HBR piece adds adoption tactics based on pilots, champions and measurement.

## Concept

MIT Sloan Management Review's research on organizational learning argues generative AI represents a genuinely new category of organizational capability rather than merely another automation technology — a distinction the authors illustrate through two companies that treated it accordingly. Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan, a $35 billion health insurer, built a cross-functional GenAI/AI leadership team responsible for employee education, responsible-AI practices, and measurable efficiency projects; when the team applied a GenAI tool to standardize contract terms and pricing analysis across the company's IT contracts, it recouped more than $10 million in savings. Wolters Kluwer, a €4.2 billion business and academic information company, took a different but complementary path, deliberately building organizational slack for learning by scheduling a weekly hour during which employees attended peer-led sessions focused on practical GenAI applications — a program that simultaneously boosted skills, sparked innovation, and improved retention, since employees felt more connected to a company that had visibly invested in their growth and given them a forum to share their own innovations. Rigorous productivity research the authors cite supports the broader pattern: a call-center study found gen AI conversational assistants improved agent productivity by at least 14% along with higher service quality and faster new-agent onboarding, while a study of more than 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants found productivity gains of 12% and 25% faster task completion when the AI tool matched the task — with lower-performing employees in both studies seeing a larger boost from the tool than more experienced ones. The authors frame gen AI's specific economic impact through a new category they call "WINS work" — work involving words, images, numbers, and sounds — arguing that "cognitive work" as a category is too broad to be analytically useful, since a carpenter and an attorney are both cognitive workers but gen AI's impact will be dramatically faster and deeper for the attorney; every organization, they argue, contains WINS work that can be radically improved once identified. Drawing an explicit parallel to prior management breakthroughs (scientific management, the quality movement, lean practices), the authors propose that gen AI enables three genuinely new organizational capabilities: dialogue-enabled processes and products, where AI is the first technology able to explain its own workings in ordinary human language; "citizen programmers," where any employee can build their own AI agents and tools, analogous to a machinist crafting a custom jig, but for cognitive work; and the ability to process the 80-90% of enterprise information that remains unstructured, which traditional automation has never been able to reach. In a real-world example of progressive structuring, Jerry Insurance, a 4-million-customer auto insurance and loan-refinancing startup, used gen AI to automate 89% of what had previously been semistructured customer chat interactions requiring human intervention — including a chatbot capable of detecting a caller's tone and emotion and escalating immediately when warranted — and its leaders now believe the same 32-person customer service team can handle three or more times its previous volume, with the company already extending the same approach to voice calls beyond its initial text and chat scope. To help leaders reason about where GenAI versus traditional AI is the right tool, the authors adapt the Keen-Scott Morton decision model, which maps tasks along two dimensions — management level (operational, managerial, strategic) and degree of structuredness — concluding that GenAI is best suited to unstructured and semistructured tasks at every management level, while traditional AI remains most appropriate for lower-level, fully structured tasks with clear, objective success criteria (a FICO credit score being a canonical example of a hard problem that's nonetheless been fully structured). To operationalize capability-building at scale, the authors propose a martial-arts-inspired belt-ranking system — white belt (hands-on introduction to core GenAI concepts and the value of dialogue-driven work), yellow belt (deeper skills including prompt engineering and chat-agent creation, with an emphasis on knowledge-sharing), green belt (building and implementing multiple agents, leading pilot teams, understanding security and ROI, and teaching others), and black belt (deep MLOps/AIOps expertise, the ability to design a full teaching curriculum, and managing a portfolio of GenAI talent and projects) — arguing this structured, internally-built progression lets organizations develop local experts who understand their own business context directly, rather than translating that context repeatedly to expensive outside consultants who lack it. The authors' closing argument is explicitly against chasing a single "killer app": organizations should instead build comprehensive, adaptable GenAI capability across the entire enterprise, treating the combination of human judgment and gen AI agents — not either alone — as the new fundamental unit of analysis for how work itself gets designed. <span class="src">Turbocharging Organizational Learning With GenAI</span>

A later MIT SMR study, based on research with a dozen global organizations, opens with a sobering statistic — Gartner's July 2025 prediction that 30% of GenAI initiatives would be abandoned after proof-of-concept by the end of that year — and argues the underlying cause is less about immature technology than about a persistent knowledge-management bottleneck: organizational data that remains fragmented, inaccessible, and underused by the people who need it. Decades of prior knowledge-management technology (portals, intranets, wikis) promised to deliver "the right insights to the right people at the right time" but largely failed, because these systems treated knowledge as static content to be separately stored and retrieved, sitting outside employees' actual daily workflows, which meant they were rarely used in practice. The study's central claim is that gen AI changes this dynamic fundamentally by making it possible to embed knowledge directly into workflows themselves, transforming knowledge from a static repository into what one analyst calls "a living system" that can be generated, adapted, and reconfigured in real time as work happens, rather than consulted separately from it. Two contrasting organizational case studies illustrate this shift converging on the same underlying lesson despite very different missions and structures. At the Gates Foundation, where knowledge is the core asset enabling staff to design and evaluate global health and poverty-reduction initiatives, the first target was automating meeting-note capture — freeing staff to engage more fully in discussions while improving note consistency, a particularly high-value change for a meeting-heavy organization. At Mott MacDonald, a global engineering and consulting firm, the challenge was instead knowledge dispersed across local offices, historically requiring the firm to physically relocate people around the globe to bring expertise to a given project; the company instead built a unified, expert-reviewed repository of more than 15,000 documents so that a new project could start with prior insights already collected, regardless of where in the world it was launching. Both organizations converged on the same underlying lesson — GenAI's impact came specifically from preparing and connecting knowledge so it could be applied dynamically and broadly, not from deploying GenAI tools for isolated, one-off use cases — and both invested heavily in unglamorous metadata and curation work (the Gates Foundation standardizing file naming conventions to prevent "garbage in, garbage out," Mott MacDonald's knowledge managers manually adding metadata like regional applicability that wasn't present in source documents themselves) before the technology could reliably deliver value. The study identifies four specific ways gen AI rewires knowledge workflows in practice: remodeling knowledge creation, where GenAI acts as a genuine cocreator rather than a passive retrieval tool (McKinsey's internal Lilli platform gives consultants access to more than 100,000 documents and can generate tailored, on-brand communications and drafts — "it takes any prose and translates it into McKinsey-quality writing," in one senior partner's description — with every platform improvement explicitly tied back to user feedback and analytics); unifying access across fragmented data repositories (Evoke PLC's RAG-based GenAI Studio provides a single conversational interface across previously siloed internal repositories, but only after the company first used custom algorithms to identify and eliminate obsolete or duplicate documents that would otherwise have undermined the system's reliability; Mott MacDonald's RAG-based virtual assistant EMMA, curated by a community of nearly 30 knowledge managers across 38 practice areas, deliberately relies on human curation rather than exhaustive automated tagging, since — in one manager's words — "the value of human curation lies in making implicit knowledge explicit... AI can parse and tag the obvious; humans must add the nuance"); personalizing knowledge transfer, particularly during onboarding (pharmaceutical firm Novartis embedded GenAI into its HR systems so new hires can access role-specific policies conversationally, coordinated through a dedicated Knowledge Management Centre of Excellence that sits at the intersection of HR and a technology-focused group reporting to the chief people, technology, data, and insights officer, giving it visibility across the full employee lifecycle alongside direct access to technical infrastructure); and applying knowledge in daily work, where value is ultimately realized (Austrian insurer Uniqa's real-time "infobot" automatically identifies the customer, pulls policy and tariff data from multiple back-end systems, and prepares detailed responses for a live customer service agent during the call itself, requiring deep observational work with agents to identify precise, high-friction moments worth automating). Synthesizing across all studied organizations, the research identifies five groundwork requirements leaders consistently need to get right, none of which are primarily technical: start with visible, immediately obvious pain points, scoped rigorously against feasibility (can the data and systems actually support it), viability (is there a genuine business case), and desirability (do people actually want this) — since, as one consultancy's principal data scientist put it, "if a bot only answers a question once a week, there's no use case"; get data and documents in order before expecting GenAI to compensate for messy, fragmented, untrustworthy source knowledge, since this was the single most consistent lesson across every organization studied; integrate the technology stack properly, recognizing that a proof-of-concept demo can run in weeks on test data, but a secure, compliant, enterprise-scale deployment connected to real systems can take a year or more (Uniqa's approach — building governed subagents tied to different insurance product lines, testing retrieval accuracy against a 65-document benchmark pack until reaching roughly 95% accuracy, and scaling in deliberate stages starting with its highest-volume product line — illustrates the discipline required); build governance into workflows from the very start rather than treating early experimentation as a compliance-free zone, involving legal, risk, and security groups early specifically when connecting GenAI systems to internal knowledge stores (Evoke institutionalized this through a formal AI steering group spanning IT, legal, compliance, and the business, with every new use case required to pass its review before deployment); and drive adoption deliberately through culture, since even a carefully engineered system fails if employees remain reluctant to use it — successful organizations combined visible leadership endorsement, genuine grassroots experimentation, and trusted internal champions who could bridge the gap between the technology and day-to-day practice (the Gates Foundation's approach spanned a foundation-wide launch event, ongoing training and lunch-and-learns, hackathons, and a "GPT gallery" for sharing custom tools, supported by knowledge librarians who continued acting as champions after the initial rollout). The study's closing argument frames gen AI as fundamentally as much a workflow-transformation journey as a knowledge-transformation journey — the two cannot be separated, since workflows give knowledge its movement through the organization, and knowledge gives those same workflows their intelligence. <span class="src">Rewire Organizational Knowledge With GenAI</span>

A third analysis, from Harvard Business Review, challenges the framing both prior studies implicitly rely on — that the central management choice about AI is between automation (machines replacing human tasks) and augmentation (humans and machines cooperating) — arguing this framing misses a crucial point: augmentation doesn't actually avoid automation, it simply hides it at a lower level. When an organization uses a gen AI assistant to "augment" the task of writing product descriptions, it is, in practice, automating the writing of first drafts specifically; that lower-level automation still replaces human intuition, expertise, and reasoning with whatever the AI system produces, and can still produce the same deskilling effects the augmentation framing was supposed to avoid — the downsides simply get pushed down a level rather than eliminated. The alternative mindset the author proposes is designing AI deployment specifically to increase an organization's collective intelligence — the shared intelligence that emerges from collaboration, collective effort, and competition, reflecting a group's capacity to reach consensus, solve complex problems, and adapt to a changing environment — built from three interdependent components drawn from recent collective-intelligence research: collective memory, collective attention, and collective reasoning. Collective memory concerns how a group allocates, retrieves, and updates its shared knowledge, allowing individuals to specialize in different, complementary areas so the group collectively knows and can recall more than any individual member could alone; this requires groups to continually update their shared understanding of "who knows what" as members' expertise develops. NVIDIA's internal ChipNeMo chatbot — a foundation model fine-tuned on the company's internal documents, code, and communications specifically for complex chip-design engineering questions — illustrates AI's potential to support this function directly, particularly valuable in a globally distributed organization where finding the right human expert quickly is otherwise genuinely difficult; the author explicitly contrasts this collective-memory-supporting use with the alternative some Wall Street firms are pursuing, where entry-level analyst roles are simply automated away, a choice that risks stunting institutional knowledge development and disrupting the social networks and knowledge-sharing relationships an organization's collective memory actually depends on. Collective attention concerns how a group allocates and aligns its focus on key tasks and priorities — sometimes multiple people need to attend to the same thing together, other times a group maximizes its collective attention precisely by dividing focus across different targets — and AI can support this by streamlining communication, optimizing schedules and workflows, reducing the cognitive cost of task-switching and interdependent-task coordination, and helping team members develop joint awareness of each other's current workload and availability. Collective reasoning concerns a group's ability to reason about both individual and shared goals well enough to align on collective priorities that maximize joint benefit — AI can support this by integrating information from disparate sources to improve goal alignment, by summarizing and analyzing individual viewpoints to help bridge communication gaps between people with different backgrounds, and by making the reasoning underlying a given goal or priority more accessible to people who don't share the same technical or disciplinary background (the author cites HubSpot's exploration of AI-automated A/B test generation as an example of gen AI extending participation in a technical process to people who previously lacked the technical background to contribute directly). The author is careful to flag that these benefits are not automatic and can cut sharply the other way. Automating AI deployment as a pure production tool tends to be inherently deskilling, produces rigid organizational structures, and homogenizes solutions — all of which reduce an organization's capacity to adapt to changing conditions by narrowing the diversity of ideas, hypotheses, and processes it actually considers. Two studies from the author's own lab document this risk concretely: in one, teams working alongside an AI voice assistant began aligning their attention with the assistant and even adopting its specific terminology on topics entirely unrelated to the task at hand — an effect that persisted even when the AI's contributions were explicitly unhelpful and team members reported, accurately, that they didn't trust the assistant at all; in another, AI-generated feedback measurably helped individuals learn and improve their own skills, but simultaneously produced population-level "algorithmic monoculture" — because many individuals were receiving feedback from the same centralized AI system, they tended to specialize in increasingly similar ways over time, reducing intellectual diversity across the group as a whole, while the same study found these learning benefits accrued disproportionately to employees who were already highly skilled and motivated, rather than lifting less-experienced workers the most (a finding in some tension with the productivity research MIT SMR cites elsewhere, which found lower-performing workers gained more from gen AI tools on discrete tasks). The author's practical guidance for managers is threefold: use AI explicitly as a coach and coordination tool, not solely a production tool, giving employees feedback and critiques rather than only generating finished output on their behalf, and evaluate whether these support systems are actually working via usage statistics, satisfaction surveys, and performance evaluation, not assumption; use AI deliberately to expand the intellectual space under consideration when deploying it as a production tool — generating multiple first drafts or exploring multiple design candidates rather than converging immediately on one — while actively resisting the instinctive urge to treat that expanded option space as wasteful redundancy that should be streamlined away for short-term productivity's sake, since whether AI expands or narrows an organization's options depends entirely on how it's embedded into existing processes; and stay actively alert to the specific risk that AI use can quietly reduce cognitive diversity and amplify existing inequalities within a workforce, designing systems that keep humans genuinely central rather than treating AI's ability to process large volumes of input as a reason to remove human judgment from the loop. The author's closing argument is that realizing gen AI's transformative potential requires moving past its immediate performance and efficiency benefits and treating collective intelligence itself — an organization's shared capacity to remember, attend, and reason together — as the design target, not merely a byproduct of individual task automation. <span class="src">How to Use AI to Build Your Company’s Collective Intelligence</span>

<span class="src">How AI Can Change the Way Your Company Gets Work Done</span> (HBR, July 2024) is an earlier, adoption-focused treatment of the same capability theme. It compares gen AI's adoption speed with the internet's, proposes demand-risk screening for safe first projects, advises leaders to model use in meetings (for example running ChatGPT live on a stumping question) and notes that trust is a major barrier, with about half of people not trusting gen AI, best overcome by finding a use case that matters to the individual. It acknowledges that capability-led growth takes longer to appear in revenue and that no perfect measure exists. <span class="src">How AI Can Change the Way Your Company Gets Work Done</span>

## Open Questions

- None of the three sources directly reconciles the tension between the "WINS work" framework's finding that lower-performing employees gain the most from gen AI tools on discrete tasks, and the collective-intelligence research's finding that AI-generated learning feedback disproportionately benefits already highly-skilled, motivated employees — it's unclear whether these findings genuinely conflict or reflect different task types, timescales, or measurement approaches.
- None of the three sources addresses how an organization should sequence these three frameworks relative to each other — whether building workflow-embedded knowledge infrastructure (Rewire) is a prerequisite for pursuing capability-maturity progression (Turbocharging) or collective-intelligence design (Collective Intelligence), or whether they can be pursued in parallel.
- The 2023 study the article relies on used models that are no longer frontier, and the article offers no outcome data for its recommended champions and pilots.
