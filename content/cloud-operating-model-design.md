## Summary

Forrester's six-layer framework for replacing an obsolete, shared-services IT operating model with one purpose-built for cloud at scale — customers, value, capabilities, structure, governance, and leadership. Each layer poses a specific set of questions an organization must answer for itself rather than adopt off the shelf. A companion Forrester report extends the vendor-ecosystem and sourcing-governance dimensions with a dedicated playbook for architecting enterprise applications and strategic partnerships.

## Concept

Forrester's central argument is that cloud has already overwhelmed the traditional shared-services IT operating model, yet most organizations — even after a decade or more of cloud usage — have never deliberately redesigned their operating model to match, instead accumulating one informally as decisions piled up over time. The legacy shared-services model relied fundamentally on hierarchy, triage, and rationing, because on-premises data centers simply couldn't scale fast enough to meet the competing demands of every internal and external stakeholder simultaneously; virtualization provided some relief but never resolved the underlying problem of allocating scarce resources across competing priorities. Cloud removes that structural constraint entirely by aligning platforms, applications, and data directly with the specific needs of the users, partners, and customers who actually consume them — but Forrester's research finds that early-edition cloud operating models, built when usage was lower and complexity was lower, now genuinely require rework given today's scale, the proliferation of managed cloud services (databases, cloud-native platforms), and the recent explosion of AI workloads specifically. To replace the obsolete shared-services model, Forrester applied its general Operating Model Framework to cloud specifically, producing a six-layer Cloud Operating Model Framework meant to function as a blueprint any organization can adapt to its own circumstances, rather than a template to copy wholesale. <span class="src">Design Your Cloud Operating Model</span>

The framework deliberately starts with customers, not technology. Layer One requires organizations to explicitly define both their internal stakeholders — developers, business users, and the cross-functional set of groups (security; operations; governance, risk, and compliance; procurement; C-suite executives) whose initiatives touch cloud, each with a distinct interest in what cloud provides them — and their external stakeholders, since serving customers and partners must sit at the center of a cloud operating model given how much cloud and mobile technology have already raised baseline service expectations in both B2C and B2B contexts; cloud can serve external stakeholders by rapidly shipping new features, lowering cost, improving resilience, or simply facilitating partner collaboration that happens outside the traditional corporate firewall. Layer Two builds on this foundation to define cloud value specifically: the journeys stakeholders actually undergo to obtain cloud services (mapped using customer-journey and orchestration tools, to reveal friction points and opportunities for real-time enhancement); the concrete cloud offering itself — which specific services, platforms, and instances are available via self-service, including associated SLAs, training, and repository resources, prioritized based on genuine stakeholder and business need rather than IT's own preferences (while still exercising enough oversight to prevent inappropriate, overly expensive, or non-compliant service adoption); a value proposition that goes meaningfully beyond positioning cloud operations as a passive, secure/compliant "broker" — since old operational habits like take-it-or-leave-it attitudes and overly hierarchical manual approvals will otherwise persist and undermine cloud's actual advantages — instead aggregating and genuinely inspiring stakeholders around near-term, measurable business impact; and the actual experience delivered, since legacy shared-services IT was notorious for long lead times even on basic infrastructure requests, meaning a modern cloud experience needs to deliberately embrace automation, self-service, and fast availability of new offerings while still preserving a white-glove option for genuinely complex requests. <span class="src">Design Your Cloud Operating Model</span>

Layer Three shifts from defining what cloud should deliver to documenting how the organization will actually deliver it, through five specific capability elements. Activities need to be fully outlined and mapped to higher-level workflows (value streams, workstreams, lifecycle streams) using business-capability maps, since cloud's expanded capacity for action carries a real risk of service sprawl and chaotic, undocumented activity if left unmanaged. Metrics require rethinking rather than simple carryover from the data-center era: legacy measures like uptime, mean-time-to-resolve, resource usage, and developer velocity still matter, but cloud's shared-responsibility model complicates something as basic as an uptime metric, pushing teams toward root-cause analysis and observability/AIOps tooling specifically to distinguish platform-caused outages from other failure modes, alongside a blended mix of business-level OKRs/KPIs and more tactical operational metrics like deployment flow. Skills remain a persistent constraint even as cloud services grow more abstracted and automated — organizations need to identify which specific cloud roles matter most for their context (cloud administrator, cloud architect, solution architect, with cloud infrastructure engineer and cloud security engineer as natural next additions, plus non-operational roles like cloud vendor management professional and FinOps practitioner), augmenting internal capacity with third-party managed services where staffing limitations require it. Competencies are related but distinct from skills: certain competencies genuinely need to live inside the organization, while others can reasonably be sourced from partners or a cloud provider's own support teams — and Forrester specifically cautions that passing a vendor certification exam doesn't automatically mean someone is prepared to assume the real-world responsibilities that certification implies, meaning organizations still need to assess actual readiness rather than treating certification alone as sufficient. And underlying technologies need to be catalogued explicitly — the specific cloud platforms and services in use, plus the surrounding ecosystem of governance tools, project management and collaboration software, portfolio visualization, developer platforms, and incident management software that together constitute the organization's genuinely full cloud technology stack. <span class="src">Design Your Cloud Operating Model</span>

Layer Four addresses organizational structure directly, since cloud providers have effectively remade enterprise IT in their own image — welcome when it means access to new services and a sharper focus on innovation, but something organizations still need to deliberately align with their own priorities rather than accept passively. Five elements make up this layer: reporting lines, where cross-functional technical roles may need a "two-in-a-box" structure that keeps them accountable to business requirements while still connecting them to a technical manager who can assess skills and support career development; definition of work, moving away from legacy IT's narrow, siloed functional definitions (the canonical example being a server team standing up virtual machines with no visibility into what applications actually run on them) toward genuine clarity about scope and ownership, achieved through pragmatism, collaboration, and formal training rather than idealized org-chart design; operating units, where cloud centers of excellence and a cloud business office can facilitate coordination beyond formal reporting lines, paired with more tactical cloud platform teams, while the organization decides deliberately how centralized those units should be; location, where cloud increasingly enables virtual placement of an operating unit to best match stakeholder needs rather than defaulting to physical co-location, subject to real national, regional, or business-structure constraints where they genuinely apply; and interactions, where Forrester identifies four distinct patterns describing how operating units actually affect each other's ability to deliver value — independent (autonomous delivery of a new cloud service), coordinated (multiple units working toward a shared goal, like a Zero Trust security program), contracted (minimal-cooperation delivery against a specified scope, like new networking capacity), and collaborative (broad, guardrail-supported coordination for something like a revamped, multi-service customer engagement program) — with cloud's underlying infrastructure unity making consistent interaction patterns more achievable than they were under fragmented, siloed data-center-era IT. <span class="src">Design Your Cloud Operating Model</span>

Layer Five addresses governance, framed as an organization's effort to reassert autonomy over its own IT resources even as it hands substantial capability and control to external cloud providers. Security starts from Zero Trust as the near-universal foundational model, built on solid identity-and-access-management practices, leaning on the inherited security properties of ephemeral, cloud-native workloads, and paying careful attention to the shared-responsibility model that defines which security obligations sit with the provider versus the customer at every layer of the stack. Compliance requires embedding GRC and security functions directly into the cloud operating model itself, starting from a baseline checklist that goes beyond standard controls to address cloud-specific risks — since even substantial vendor-provided regulatory documentation won't automatically align to an organization's own control framework, especially as the quantity and nature of cloud services keeps expanding (generative AI deployment being the current frontier). Decisions require a formal decision framework and catalog, since the early, informal days of cloud famously let business users stand up new services outside IT entirely with nothing more than a credit card — a dynamic that still creates constant pressure toward decentralization, genuinely valuable when it avoids data-center-era bottlenecks but genuinely risky if left completely unchecked. Transparency needs to shift decisively away from the narrow, hierarchical, and bureaucratic GRC interactions typical of the private-data-center era toward real-time dashboards and readily auditable artifacts that let both engineers and the C-suite understand current status quickly — while still maintaining a strict separation of duties between GRC stakeholders and cloud operations itself. And accountability requires cloud-era RACI charts that are meaningfully different from their data-center-era predecessors in both the pace of interaction and the sheer number of participants involved in each category, with cloud specifically empowering architects to assert genuine responsibility ("R") while broadening who needs to be accountable and consulted as cloud services and their business impact continue expanding over time. <span class="src">Design Your Cloud Operating Model</span>

Layer Six, leadership, closes the framework by addressing what the cloud operating model needs from the people actually running it, across six dimensions. Vision matters more now than in cloud's early days, when adoption mostly meant standing up a specific service unavailable on-premises (managed databases, a VMware lift-and-shift); the advent of cloud-native technologies (serverless, Kubernetes) and now cloud-based AI workloads demands a genuinely expansive vision that ties the cloud operating model to broader organizational goals, such as a traditional bank explicitly using cloud to stay competitive against fintech rivals. Strategy is the day-to-day operational reference point that translates that vision into practice — a US-based regulated company expanding internationally, for instance, might make multicloud adoption an explicit part of its strategy specifically to address regulator concerns about vendor concentration while also satisfying sovereign-cloud requirements abroad. Culture needs to genuinely interlock cloud with the organization's broader culture rather than exist as a separate technical initiative — most companies blend their existing culture with DevOps-driven momentum toward a more empowered, collaborative model, since the underlying cloud technology is itself designed to support exactly that kind of culture if the organization actually embraces it. Performance needs to be measured against the organization's own broader goals and its chosen IT style — Forrester identifies four: the enabling style (stabilizing, operating, and protecting existing IT resources, such as a straightforward VMware-to-cloud migration), the amplifying style (optimizing business outcomes at scale by tapping native cloud services for new global reach), the cocreating style (delivering new products faster, such as standing up customer-facing web apps rapidly in cloud-based development environments), and the transforming style (technology-led innovation, such as cloud AI infrastructure and managed services) — with automatic adoption of whatever cloud service is newest explicitly rejected as a valid performance strategy on its own. Corporate values need to extend to the cloud provider relationship itself, since choosing a primary cloud provider carries genuinely long-term implications and an organization needs to be comfortable with that provider's own positions on privacy and responsible AI use, alongside its own sustainability, accessibility, and inclusive-hiring commitments shaping how cloud services actually get adopted and deployed. And motivation closes the framework by recognizing that a cloud operating model needs to actively motivate the people working within it — freeing IT personnel from shared-services rigidity to build genuinely empowered platform teams that interact more productively with the business — illustrated by a US federal agency that built its finalized cloud strategy around explicitly motivational documents connecting the agency's cloud strategy directly to its core mission, using compelling visuals and language specifically to get individuals and leaders genuinely excited about participating rather than simply complying. <span class="src">Design Your Cloud Operating Model</span>

A companion Forrester report, <span class="src">Architect Enterprise — Applications and Services for Impact</span>, extends this concept's Layer Three (capabilities/competencies) and Layer Four (structure) into a dedicated playbook for enterprise applications and vendor-partner ecosystems specifically. Its central data point sets the stakes: services decision-makers at digitally advanced enterprises were far more likely to rely on third-party services for digital transformation (90%) than intermediate (79%) or beginner (69%) organizations, making ecosystem strategy — not just internal cloud operating-model design — a genuine determinant of transformation success. The report identifies six elements of "architecting smarter" enterprise applications and services: prioritizing composability so business processes can be quickly reconfigured as market conditions shift; cultivating democratized work styles by upskilling citizen developers and identifying new contributors across lines of business; increasing use of communities and marketplaces (low-code platforms, online stores, talent marketplaces) to stand up solutions faster; taking advantage of prebuilt industry-platform accelerators to compress implementation timelines; unlocking intelligent test automation, now treated as necessary rather than optional given the pace of SaaS product updates; and embracing ConfigOps to centralize configuration management as application portfolios grow decentralized and unmanageable at scale. On the partner-ecosystem side, the report cautions against treating services partners as interchangeable "body shops" for order-taking, instead recommending organizations select partners for innovation and outcomes rather than capability checklists, co-create solutions through incubator-style relationships with outcome-tied contracts rather than pure time-and-materials arrangements, and use partners' cross-client benchmark data as a genuine source of process and industry best practice. On sourcing governance specifically — directly extending this concept's own Layer Five — the report recommends shifting from an "ivory tower" center of excellence that controls every decision toward a "center of enablement" that works closely with the business, using a multitiered approach with oversight calibrated to a given decision's scale, risk, and need for speed, revisited regularly rather than fixed once and left alone. <span class="src">Architect Enterprise — Applications and Services for Impact</span>

## Open Questions

- The source doesn't address how the six layers should be sequenced for an organization redesigning its operating model from scratch — whether, for instance, Layer One (customers) genuinely needs to be settled before Layer Four (structure) can be meaningfully addressed, or whether some layers can be worked in parallel.
- The companion applications-and-services report doesn't specify how its ecosystem/sourcing recommendations should be prioritized against the core six-layer framework when an organization has limited transformation capacity to spend on both simultaneously.
