## Summary

Algorithmic auditing identifies the ways an algorithmic system could harm the people it affects and builds monitoring for them, using the Ethical Matrix to map stakeholder harms, Explainable Fairness to separate legitimate from unfair disparities, and benchmarking and red teaming for large language models. Documented failures of risk-prediction tools, including beach-safety, sepsis, welfare-fraud, child-welfare, hiring and pretrial-detention systems and the Tessa chatbot, show what happens when such checks are skipped and vendors claim human oversight without evidence. The shared lesson is to treat AI tools as suspect until the vendor shows that they work and that they cannot cause harm even when they do.

## Concept

Algorithmic auditing, as developed by O'Neil Risk Consulting & Algorithmic Auditing, rests on a deliberately simple idea, to identify the failure scenarios for people who might be hurt by an algorithmic system and to build monitoring that catches them, using general methods that work whether the system is a bureaucratic flowchart, a classic machine-learning risk score or a large language model. The approach is narrow in scope, since an audit raises alerts while resolving them, or deciding that a use case is too stochastic for AI at all, is left to organizational leaders within internal rules and law. Doing it well requires knowing the complete use case, including how the technology is used, by whom, for whom and for what purpose, because the same model can be benign in one application and dangerous in another, and the diversity of LLM use cases more than their technical complexity makes auditing them hard, which argues for an application-specific approach that starts with the highest-stakes uses. <span class="src">Auditing Algorithmic Risk</span>

The first framework, the Ethical Matrix, structures the question of who could be harmed and how. Its rows are the stakeholders, defined broadly to include builders, deployers, users and any other community affected by adoption, with subgroups split out when their concerns genuinely differ, as lighter- and darker-skinned people may need separate rows for a facial-recognition system. Its columns come from nontechnical conversations in which representatives of each group are asked how the system could fail and succeed for them, and a fraud-detection example shows how concerns differ, since false flags are a major headache for customers whose accounts are frozen and a moderate business worry, while an undetected fraudulent transaction harms the company but leaves other customers indifferent. Each cell is judged on the likelihood of the risk, the number of people affected and the severity, informed by data and by legal constraints such as anti-discrimination law, and then colored red for existential risks where a stakeholder could be gravely harmed or a hard constraint is violated, yellow for moderate ethical concern and green for no worries. The matrix is meant to be a living document, drafted during design and revised as new subgroups, indirect effects or concerns surface after deployment. <span class="src">Auditing Algorithmic Risk</span>

The second framework, Explainable Fairness, addresses the harder question inside many fairness concerns, whether an observed disparity reflects legitimate discrimination, a difference explained by factors that belong in the decision, or unfair treatment. If male candidates score higher on average because they have more relevant experience that is legitimate, and the real concern is whether equally qualified men and women, holding constant the legitimately relevant factors, are scored differently. The method controls for agreed legitimate factors, a technique already used implicitly, for instance in Federal Reserve analyses of mortgage denial rates that control for loan amount, FICO score and debt-to-income ratio, and its real contribution is forcing an explicit conversation about why those factors are legitimate. In the worked example, lenders treat FICO scores as legitimate because they measure creditworthiness, yet the scores encode historical unfairness, since mortgage payments have counted for decades while rent payments began counting only in 2014 and only in some versions, which favors homeowners over renters and interacts with decades of redlining that shaped homeownership disparities by race. The article declines to decide whether FICO scores should still count, framing that as an ethical and policy question for the right decision-makers, and once a disparity is not explained by agreed factors the framework requires a fixed threshold, such as the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission's four-fifths rule for hiring, or a relative one, such as an above-average pay gap, that triggers a required response. <span class="src">Auditing Algorithmic Risk</span>

Colorado's SB21-169, passed in 2021 to protect consumers from unfair algorithmic discrimination in insurance, applied both frameworks with ORCAA assisting the state's Division of Insurance. The Ethical Matrix was straightforward because the law defined the protected classes, which include race, color, national or ethnic origin, religion, sex, sexual orientation, disability, gender identity and gender expression, and the Division chose to start with life-insurance underwriting and with race and ethnicity. Explainable Fairness was harder, since the Division had to decide what makes two applicants similar and settled on policy type, death-benefit amount, age, gender and tobacco use as legitimate controls, and its draft regulation requires insurers to run regressions of approval and pricing outcomes by race with those controls, with statistically significant and substantial disparities, defined as a p-value below 0.05 and a difference above 5 percentage points, triggering mandatory investigation and, if warranted, remediation. <span class="src">Auditing Algorithmic Risk</span>

Large language models call for two further techniques, because their open-ended output space makes them harder to pin down than a scoring model. Benchmarking scores outputs against a predefined dataset of inputs and desired outputs, with metrics suited to the task such as accuracy for multiple-choice questions, n-gram overlap for summarization and many others covering calibration, robustness and bias. It has structural limits, since benchmark data that leaks into training data lets a model memorize answers rather than show capability, a risk that grows as benchmarks are published and reused, optimizing for a benchmark degrades broader usefulness under Goodhart's law, and capable models increasingly detect evaluation, as Claude 3 did when it responded to a needle-in-a-haystack prompt by noting that an inserted fact seemed placed to test whether it was paying attention. Red teaming probes a model adversarially for violent or dangerous content, leaked training data, copyright infringement and attempts to compromise the provider's systems, through technical attacks such as injecting adversarial text or social-engineering attempts to talk the model into bad output, ideally by a multidisciplinary team that targets each red cell of the Ethical Matrix. Its limits mirror benchmarking's, since its value is subjective and varies with the creativity and risk tolerance of the team, there is no standard for how much is enough, and it cannot surface risks nobody thought to test, so it must be paired with benchmarking and with continuous post-deployment monitoring rather than treated as a one-time gate. <span class="src">Auditing Algorithmic Risk</span>

The cost of skipping this process is illustrated by Tessa, a chatbot that the National Eating Disorders Association launched in 2023, built by the vendor X2AI, now Cass, to replace a helpline it had run for two decades. NEDA closed the human-staffed helpline and laid off five employees, citing call volume that had grown and become more acute through the pandemic, and Tessa then gave harmful advice, including a safe daily calorie deficit, to users who by the nature of the service were likely struggling with eating disorders. A retrospective Ethical Matrix would have flagged two red cells, eating-disorder sufferers facing direct harm from bad advice and NEDA's own institutional trust, which was in fact damaged, and the article argues that red teaming aimed at that cell and benchmarking against curated, evidence-based question-and-answer pairs could plausibly have caught the problem. It notes that Tessa is not isolated, citing a New York City government chatbot that told users landlords did not have to accept rental-assistance tenants and employers could take a cut of tips, both illegal, and tax-preparation chatbots from TurboTax and H&R Block that gave faulty advice. <span class="src">Auditing Algorithmic Risk</span>

Documented failures of risk-prediction tools show the same lack of verification. A Toronto tool for predicting when public beaches were safe to swim claimed over 90% accuracy but performed worse than daily bacteria testing and left beaches open on a majority of days when the water was unsafe, because city officials never checked its performance over the summer it was used. The author treats this as the default rather than an unusual failure, with subtler modes also possible, such as a model that is accurate in general but misses extreme cases or fails completely at one site, which non-expert buyers cannot be expected to catch. Unless the buyer has genuine internal technical experts it purchases on faith, and if it has them it is often easier and safer to build the tool in-house than to trust an outside vendor's unverifiable claims. <span class="src">The bait and switch behind AI risk prediction tools</span>

A recurring evasion is a vendor bait-and-switch on human oversight. Tools are sold on full automation and job elimination, the commercial case that justifies the purchase, but once concerns arise about bias, catastrophic failure or limitations, vendors retreat to fine print saying the tool was never meant to operate autonomously and that a human always makes the final call. In every documented case officials and vendors declined to say how often the human overrides the tool's recommendation, so the human-in-the-loop claim works as an unfalsifiable liability shield rather than a verified safeguard. The author calls the pattern over-automation, in which tools are used well beyond their demonstrated capability and human oversight is invoked only reactively after failure. <span class="src">The bait and switch behind AI risk prediction tools</span>

Three further cases show a systemic pattern. Epic's sepsis prediction model, deployed across hundreds of US hospitals after its 2017 release, was found by University of Michigan researchers to vastly underperform its developer's claims because of data leakage, since one input was whether a patient had already received antibiotics, which usually meant a clinician had already diagnosed sepsis, so the evaluation silently counted circular reasoning as success. Measured independently, 88% of its alerts were false alarms that added to clinical workload, and Epic stopped selling the one-size-fits-all version about a year after the critical study, having first argued that its surrounding clinical workflow gave the tool purpose. The Netherlands' childcare-benefits fraud algorithm, deployed from 2013, used statistical correlations, including dual nationality, which disproportionately implicated Turkish, Moroccan and Eastern European parents, to accuse people of fraud without corroborating evidence, wrongly accusing about 30,000 parents and demanding repayments of hundreds of thousands of euros in many cases, removed the human review step that existed before, ran unchecked for over six years and ended in a EUR 3.7 million government fine and the resignation of the whole Dutch cabinet. The Allegheny Family Screening Tool, used since 2016 in Allegheny County, Pennsylvania to decide which families are investigated for neglect, investigations that can lead to children being removed even without an abuse allegation, was found two years later to have the same data-leakage problem and to be systematically biased against Black families, and its creators offered the same human-makes-the-final-decision defense without disclosing override rates. <span class="src">The bait and switch behind AI risk prediction tools</span>

Scrutiny of these systems is further blocked when companies cite trade secrets to refuse outside examination, as Epic and Northpointe, maker of the COMPAS recidivism tool used in criminal sentencing, did, closing off the independent scrutiny that surfaced the leakage and bias in the cases above. The proposed remedy reverses the default, so that the vendor must justify a tool's validity with evidence before deployment, and absent such evidence any risk-assessment tool, including in the author's assessment most currently on the market, should be treated as suspect. <span class="src">The bait and switch behind AI risk prediction tools</span>

A companion piece reports Princeton's Arvind Narayanan, co-author with Sayash Kapoor of the book AI Snake Oil, on two categories of failure, tools that simply do not work despite their marketing and tools that work as advertised but are used in the wrong context or for harmful ends. Further examples of the first category include video-based personality scoring for job candidates that proved to be an elaborate random-number generator, with scores changing radically when a bookshelf was added to the background or the candidate's glasses removed, and pretrial-detention risk algorithms across the US criminal justice system with predictive accuracy below 70%, only slightly better than a coin flip, while deciding whether someone is jailed before trial. Narayanan distinguishes generative AI, which he considers useful to nearly every knowledge worker and not snake oil in the same way, although it carries distinct risks, namely hallucinations inherent to how the technology works, misinformation such as inaccurate mushroom-foraging guides, nonconsensual deepfakes and web-navigating agents he calls pretty much dead on arrival for reliability. <span class="src">How to spot real value in AI — and avoid the snake oil</span>

His practical contribution is a two-question test for any AI tool, first whether it works as claimed, and second, as a distinct question, whether it can cause harm even if it does. Facial recognition shows why the second question cannot be skipped, since its high technical accuracy is part of what makes it harmful, because it enables effective mass surveillance without adequate guardrails, while AI-based academic cheating detectors, which systematically misflag non-native English speakers, fail the first question outright and are called snake oil. His closing frame is to treat AI as infrastructure to be evaluated soberly rather than as magic, since much of what is called AI today will fade into the background as it matures, which extends the call for default skepticism from risk-prediction tools to generative AI. <span class="src">How to spot real value in AI — and avoid the snake oil</span>
