## Summary

The Economist argues that advanced economies' public debt, near 110% of GDP, has no politically viable repayment path. Spending cuts, immigration, AI-driven growth and tax rises each fall short, leaving inflation as the most likely resolution and long-term bondholders as the likeliest losers.

## Concept

The Economist's October 2025 special report on rich-world public finances frames government debt as a historic invention whose temptation was already feared by Hume and Hamilton: a creditworthy state can always borrow more, so debt tends to ratchet upward until bond markets push back. Historically such crises were a poor-country phenomenon, but the largest advanced economies now combine record debt with persistent deficits, and the report predicts what happens next. Rich-world debt is near 110% of GDP, a level previously seen only after the Napoleonic wars, and a post-2022 rise in interest rates has made it far costlier to carry. Governments have adopted fiscal rules (America's pay-as-you-go rules, the EU Stability and Growth Pact) but routinely suspend or evade them, and there is little appetite for tightening: America has spent its tariff windfall and extended deficit-financed tax cuts, France has lost five prime ministers in two years over modest budget trims, and Japan's incoming leadership favours expansion despite the highest debt among major economies. Bond markets are responding, with long yields at multi-decade highs in Britain and Japan and French long-term yields approaching Italy's. <span class="src">Across the rich world, fiscal crises loom</span>

The report stresses that the real danger is not outright default but inflation, because nominal debts lose real value as prices rise and the pressure on central banks to tolerate it grows as debt mounts. It notes that conditions are paradoxically benign (no major recession, rates below growth in many countries), which means small primary deficits could keep debt stable in principle, but starting positions in Britain, America and France are too far from balance, and a wave of future pressure from ageing, defence and the climate transition (nearly 6% of GDP annually by 2050 across Europe's advanced economies per the IMF) is not yet priced in. Forecasts of debt paths also tend to be too optimistic: Britain's fiscal watchdog underestimated five-year-ahead deficits by 3.1% of GDP. A companion explainer observes that no debt ratio is inherently "too high": a stable ratio can become explosive with a modest rise in rates or fall in growth, and a government seen as unwilling to tighten can be run on like a bank, with fear of a crisis itself raising financing costs. The Economist's own simulations show America and France on rising paths in every scenario, while Italy and Japan are most sensitive to economic conditions. <span class="src">How much public debt is too much?</span>

History offers little comfort for the idea that big economies pay debts back from surpluses. Before 1913, Britain ran about nine decades of primary surpluses and cut its debt from 194% to 28% of GDP between 1822 and 1913, France halved its ratio between 1896 and 1913, and America nearly eliminated its debt with surpluses from 1867 to 1913 — all under a gold standard that ruled out inflating debts away. After the Second World War, by contrast, rich governments spent roughly half of 1945-80 gaining more from inflation than they paid in interest (annual savings of 1-5% of GDP), and Britain's 131-point reduction in 1947-56 came about half from inflation exceeding interest rates, with America's smaller reduction relying more on growth. The only post-war G7 episode driven mainly by budget adjustment was Canada in 1997-2006. Defaults by leading economies, while now taboo (Greece and Cyprus in the 2010s being the exceptions), occurred even in the 20th century. <span class="src">Big, rich countries have rarely repaid debt with surpluses</span>

The politics of ageing explain why adjustment looks electorally impossible. The composition of government spending has shifted heavily toward the old, who retain power over benefit design: public pensions were set when retirement was short, but retirement ages have lagged life expectancy, and the share of life spent in retirement for an OECD man grew from a sixth to about a quarter between 1972 and 2000. Attempts at even minor reform provoke backlash (more than a million French marched against raising the retirement age from 62 to 64; Britain's Labour government nearly reversed means-testing the winter fuel allowance), and IMF analysis of party manifestos in 65 countries finds both left and right have become more expansionist since about 1990, followed by bigger deficits. The "Nicolas qui paie" meme of the young bearing the cost of the old captures a real tension, though pensions are popular across age groups and taxes on workers have not risen nearly enough to pay for old-age spending, which is why governments borrow instead. The report also records more optimistic findings on ageing itself: employment has risen as a share of population since 2000, healthy ageing could add about 0.4 points to annual global growth over 2025-2050, and ageing populations may lower real interest rates by over a point by 2100. If budgets are eventually balanced, higher taxes on higher earners are the likeliest route, since the rest of the state has already been squeezed. <span class="src">Fixing the welfare state looks electorally impossible</span>

Growth is the favourite escape of politicians but is judged unlikely to work. Immigration mechanically boosts GDP and tax receipts, but migrants age too, housing and infrastructure constraints generate backlash (one Bank of Canada finding links a 1% rise in skilled migrant population to 6-8% higher local house prices), and the fiscal value varies enormously with skill: a young graduate-degree migrant to America brings about $2.3 million in discounted lifetime federal fiscal benefit versus under $15,000 for one without a high-school diploma, yet the recent surge has skewed lower-skilled. Productivity growth, including from AI, helps less than assumed: the Congressional Budget Office estimates half a point of extra productivity growth would cut the 2055 debt ratio from 156% to 113%, but pensions and health spending rise with wages, and, following the Ramsey growth framework, higher expected growth raises investment demand and lowers saving, pushing up real interest rates and potentially worsening debt sustainability; global capital flows transmit American growth into everyone's borrowing costs, so Europe risks "importing higher rates while productivity stays flat." AI could also raise demand for redistribution as knowledge work is disrupted. <span class="src">Economic growth is unlikely to prevent fiscal crisis</span>

Why some countries avoid the problem is answered by institutions rather than political system. Ray Dalio's thesis of a debt supercycle ending in default or inflation is contrasted with a significant minority of rich economies (a third of rich-world output) with net debt under 50% of GDP: the junior Anglosphere, Scandinavia, the Rhineland economies and the Asian tigers. Older research linking fragmented, coalition-based politics to profligacy no longer predicts outcomes: consensus-oriented Rhineland and Scandinavian states have low debts while majoritarian Britain and America are deep in deficit. What distinguishes the frugal is adopting budget rules after fiscal scares — Sweden's surplus-over-the-cycle rule after its early-1990s crisis, New Zealand's 1991 budget and strict rules, Switzerland's 2003 debt brake (backed by 85% of voters), Germany's 2009 deficit cap — and, in many cases, pre-funded pension systems with assets above 100% of GDP. Rules can be gamed in adversarial systems (no country has ever been fined under the EU pact), tight constitutional limits cost flexibility (Germany relaxed its debt brake in 2025 for infrastructure and defence), and political polarisation creates a "war of attrition" in which each side prefers delay to austerity. The scope of the political deal needed grows each year adjustment is postponed. <span class="src">How do some countries avoid debt?</span>

The report's conclusion follows Mankiw's five options, narrowed by elimination. Spending cuts are blocked by ageing politics, growth will not solve the problem, and tax rises are possible only in some places (America has no value-added tax but its Republican party will not back large increases absent a crisis, while France already takes 52% of GDP in revenue and the deadweight cost of taxation rises roughly with the square of the rate). That leaves default and inflation. Since inflation is the standard route for countries issuing in their own currency, and central banks have grown used to buying bonds with newly created reserves, the machinery for debt-eroding inflation and financial repression exists: pressure on the Fed to control long rates as in the 1940s, the ECB's quasi-underwriting of Italian debt, and Reform UK's proposal to stop paying interest on reserves. The accompanying leader argues that inflation arrives by accident as well as design, redistributes wealth from creditors to debtors, and could scramble the social contract — Argentina is the parable — but that the 1970s also produced the sound-money consensus of Reagan, Thatcher and independent central banks, so the outcome depends on clashes between bond markets and politicians. <span class="src">The case against holding bonds</span> <span class="src">The rich world faces a painful bout of inflation</span>

## Open Questions

- The report predicts inflation as the likeliest resolution but gives no timing; the source offers no mechanism for how soon bond markets or politicians would force the issue.
- The sources disagree internally on ageing: the welfare-state chapter cites evidence that ageing is less of an economic drag than feared, while the broader report treats age-related spending as the main unaddressed pressure.
- The special report does not quantify how much of the AI-growth-raises-interest-rates argument rests on empirical evidence versus the Ramsey model, which the source itself calls hard to prove.
