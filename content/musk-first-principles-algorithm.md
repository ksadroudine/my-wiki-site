## Summary

Elon Musk reportedly solves large engineering and manufacturing problems at Tesla and SpaceX with a five-step process that former Tesla president Jon McNeill documents, which is to question every requirement, delete every possible step or part, simplify what remains, speed up the cycle time and then automate. The process rests on first-principles thinking, meaning breaking a problem down to its most basic elements instead of reasoning by analogy, and on a less formal practice of driving one or two urgent issues relentlessly through recurring reviews. Musk is applying it to Terafab, a planned chip factory costing $20 billion or more that is meant to remove a single point of failure in the chip supply for Tesla, SpaceX and xAI. The source is skeptical, noting that the same playbook produced ideas that fizzled, such as Tesla's abandoned goal of 20 million vehicles a year.

## Concept

The Algorithm, as documented by former Tesla president Jon McNeill in a book of the same name, is presented as the operating discipline behind much of what the book calls "the genius in Musk's companies" — not a single insight but a repeatable five-step sequence Musk expects his teams at Tesla and SpaceX to apply to hard engineering and manufacturing problems: question every requirement (treating even seemingly fixed specifications as challengeable rather than given), delete every possible step or part (removing anything not proven necessary, rather than merely optimizing what exists), simplify and optimize what remains once unnecessary elements are removed, accelerate cycle time, and only then automate — deliberately placed last, since automating a flawed or unnecessarily complex process just makes the flaws move faster. The approach was first documented publicly in Walter Isaacson's 2023 biography of Musk, and became formalized into a named process during Tesla's difficult struggle to ramp Model 3 production and scale the Model X, when the problem-solving pattern had become routine enough that an executive suggested giving it a name so it could be communicated consistently throughout the organization. McNeill traces its philosophical root to first-principles thinking, which he characterizes as breaking a problem down to its "lowest common denominator" — its truly atomic-level elements — rather than reasoning from how comparable problems have historically been solved, a mode of reasoning popularly associated with Musk more broadly and treated as the conceptual foundation the five concrete steps operationalize. <span class="src">The Playbook That Elon Musk Relies On to Make His Wild Ideas Work</span>

The Algorithm's most visible current application is Terafab, a planned joint Tesla-SpaceX AI chip factory estimated to cost $20 billion or more, which Musk claims would exceed the combined output of every chip fabrication plant currently operating on the planet. The justification follows the Algorithm's logic directly: global chip suppliers combined, in Musk's account, can currently produce only about 2 percent of what his companies collectively need to fuel Tesla's robotaxi and humanoid-robot ambitions, SpaceX's AI-oriented data centers (part of a broader strategic shift toward building data centers in space, where Musk argues solar power will eventually be cheaper than on Earth), and xAI. Musk has reportedly tried encouraging existing suppliers to expand capacity faster, but found there's a maximum rate they're comfortable scaling at — and rather than accept that constraint as fixed, his stated conclusion follows the Algorithm's "question every requirement" step directly: there's no actual requirement that he source chips from anyone else at all. As McNeill frames it, with three separate businesses (Tesla, SpaceX, and xAI) all structurally dependent on chip supply, Musk treats that shared dependency itself as an unacceptable single point of failure across his whole ecosystem of companies, which is what turns a chip shortage into a rationale for entering an entirely new, capital-intensive, and unfamiliar industry rather than continuing to negotiate with existing suppliers. This is not the first time the same underlying pattern has played out: following the commercial success of the Model S, Musk anticipated that global battery production wouldn't scale fast enough to meet Tesla's future EV demand, and rather than simply waiting on suppliers, began building a dedicated battery factory — eventually persuading supplier Panasonic to open a major facility in Nevada, which became a critical enabler of the Model 3's eventual commercial success once that vehicle needed to ramp production at scale. <span class="src">The Playbook That Elon Musk Relies On to Make His Wild Ideas Work</span>

A less formalized but explicitly named ingredient of how the Algorithm actually operates day to day is deliberately injected urgency: Musk is described as fixating on one or two existential issues at a time and driving them relentlessly, week after week, through recurring engineering review meetings — McNeill recounts sitting in these reviews and being convinced that rival companies' CEOs weren't running their own organizations at anywhere near the same operational tempo, meaning the sustained pace itself compounds into a competitive advantage independent of any single technical decision. This same urgency, per the source, is currently being redirected toward AI and space specifically. The article treats genuine skepticism toward the Terafab plan as warranted rather than dismissing it, noting explicitly that the same underlying playbook has also produced Musk ideas that fizzled — Tesla's abandoned goal of manufacturing 20 million vehicles annually, against the 1.6 million the company actually delivered in the most recent year cited — and that the Terafab announcement arrives at a moment when Musk is preparing to take SpaceX public, raising the practical stakes of whether this particular application of the Algorithm succeeds or becomes another example of grandiose ambition outrunning execution. Supporters, on the other side of that skepticism, point to Musk's track record of turning Tesla into a genuine EV powerhouse and SpaceX into the dominant commercial player in the space economy as evidence for what tends to happen when the same pattern does succeed — leaving the Terafab bet as, in the source's own framing, an open test case for the Algorithm's limits rather than a settled success story. <span class="src">The Playbook That Elon Musk Relies On to Make His Wild Ideas Work</span>
