## Summary

Agentic AI is reshaping technology delivery from two directions at once — McKinsey projects a 20-30% compression in tech services providers' core business even as it opens $100-400 billion in new agentic-workflow spending for those who reposition fast enough, while internal software delivery organizations are separately redesigning around a "24-hour sprint" model where agents execute overnight and humans supervise by day, with early adopters reporting three-to-fivefold productivity gains alongside 60% smaller teams. Bain adds that tech-services share prices have fallen sharply as clients demand productivity commitments providers have not yet achieved.

## Concept

McKinsey's research into technology services providers' response to agentic AI, based on a July 2025 survey of 200 C-suite executives across Asia, Europe, and North America (companies with revenues above $500 million, spanning six industries), finds the sector facing a genuinely two-sided disruption. More than 80% of surveyed enterprises are already running agentic AI pilots, roughly 12% have scaled deployments across multiple functions, and half plan significant scaled investment within six months — a momentum translating directly into budget growth, with more than a third of respondents expecting AI-specific outlays to rise above 25% this fiscal year and close to three-quarters expecting overall IT spending to grow 2-10% over the next two years. That growth, however, does not translate straightforwardly into more spending on traditional tech services. McKinsey projects agentic AI will drive a 20-30% compression in the core tech services market across existing service lines, driven by two forces working simultaneously: direct employee productivity gains from agent deployment (reducing the volume of billable execution work), and the largest enterprises increasingly building agentic capabilities in-house through global capability centers — entities tech services providers have traditionally only helped stand up, rather than continuing to staff and run. Structurally, the compression also reshapes the nature of the work that remains, shifting employee focus from execution tasks (monitoring, processing) toward enablement-focused activities (strategy design, stakeholder engagement). Despite this contraction pressure, McKinsey projects the overall tech services market will actually grow faster with agentic AI than without it — 4-7% annually to $1.6-1.9 trillion by 2030, versus a pre-agentic-AI baseline forecast of 4-5% CAGR — because new agentic-specific value pools more than offset the legacy decline for providers positioned to capture them. Two such pools stand out: "agentic AI workflow services" (infrastructure support, multiagent architecture design, orchestration, agent engineering, agent security, and governance), worth an estimated nearly $200 billion in new enterprise spending, and "business function transformation" through joint human-agent operating models in knowledge roles, worth $100-400 billion annually. Enterprises surveyed expect as much as 15-30% of current roles' work could be taken on by agents within three years, concentrated in four investment areas — technology and engineering function agents (code testing, migration, root-cause analysis, DevOps), customer-facing agents (content creation, sales support, onboarding), back-end function agents (call center coverage, legal services, ticket routing), and vertical-specific process agents (patient care management, fleet routing, claims authorization, credit risk assessment) — with more than 70% of this opportunity concentrated in five industries especially suited to automation: financial services, retail, high tech, healthcare, and manufacturing. This shift is also blurring the traditional competitive boundaries in the sector: software vendors are moving into "service as software" through agentic operations, hyperscalers are offering managed agentic solutions leveraging their infrastructure scale, and a new class of pure-play AI disruptors — including LLM providers building domain-specific agents — threatens to capture value directly from traditional services players (McKinsey cites one AI startup claiming its customer service agents resolve up to 90% of inquiries without human intervention). To navigate this, McKinsey identifies four distinct value propositions tech services providers can pursue, which may vary by vertical even within the same provider: "agentic AI enabler," providing foundational infrastructure and agent services for others to build on, requiring deep technical/architectural expertise and strong hyperscaler and LLM-provider partnerships; "packaged agent implementer," deploying prebuilt third-party agent solutions with minimal customization via a strong partner ecosystem; "custom agent developer," building bespoke solutions tailored to a client's specific business context, requiring deep domain expertise; and "end-to-end workflow disruptor," acting as a full transformational partner that redesigns and implements entire customized workflows rather than just deploying agents within existing ones. Underlying all four, McKinsey identifies five capabilities that will distinguish leading providers: reimagining positioning around agentic-first opportunities rather than overindexing on unscalable experimental copilots; building proprietary, modular agentic architecture with built-in observability and governance (to avoid the compliance breaches and costly remediation that come from deploying opaque, unpredictable systems); leading with consultative, domain-driven go-to-market models rather than positioning AI as a generic horizontal capability; redesigning the operating model and talent base around human-agent collaboration, including new roles for overseeing, retraining, and managing autonomous agents; and reinventing commercial models to align fees directly with impact, since traditional time-and-material billing is under direct pressure — more than 70% of surveyed enterprises now prefer alternative pricing, pushing providers toward subscription-based "pods" (billing a flat monthly fee for a combination of engineers and AI agents) or gain-share models tied to measurable outcomes like cost savings, as full-time-equivalent-based billing loses relevance in an increasingly agent-automated delivery model. <span class="src">Reengineering tech services for the agentic AI era</span>

Where McKinsey's tech-services research examines how external providers must reposition their business, a companion McKinsey analysis examines how software delivery works internally once organizations actually rewire their operating model around agentic AI — describing a concrete emerging pattern the authors call the "24-hour sprint." In this model, humans work during the day reviewing agent output, resolving ambiguity, strengthening architectural guardrails, and aligning stakeholders — their role shifting from producing artifacts to supervising and improving the system that produces them — while AI agents execute structured work overnight at scale: enriching requirements, validating architecture, generating and testing code, and packaging outputs for morning review. This compresses the traditional two-week sprint cycle dramatically, and some companies are already reporting threefold-to-fivefold productivity improvements alongside a 60% reduction in team size from adopting it. The model only functions reliably given four practical foundations: a clear business vision (a product roadmap or defined standard) against which agent-generated requirements can be assessed for quality and alignment; a standardized, consistent underlying technology environment (common frameworks, modular architecture) so solutions can scale and components be reused rather than reinvented per project; a standard structure for the path from requirements to code, so agents can reliably interpret inputs and produce predictable outputs across different projects; and consistent engagement from the same core stakeholders across the value stream, since gaps here produce misalignment and constant rework — without this foundation, agent output becomes fragmented and untrustworthy. Beyond the sprint cadence itself, McKinsey argues scaling AI requires extending automation past its traditional home in testing and deployment (CI/CD, which can represent up to 30% of total technology spend) into the much larger, historically manual and interpretation-heavy zone spanning requirements through coding — where most organizational friction actually accumulates, since requirements, standards, and specifications typically live scattered across disconnected documents and tools, forcing humans to repeatedly translate intent between artifacts. The agentic model removes this friction by codifying functional descriptions, nonfunctional requirements, guardrails, sequence diagrams, and repositories into standardized, machine-readable formats, letting the pipeline run end to end in hours with humans intervening only at defined review gates rather than acting as intermediaries at every handoff. A third foundational element McKinsey identifies is knowledge infrastructure: to produce accurate results, "agent factories" need organizational context and memory, which leading organizations are building as knowledge graphs functioning as an AI memory layer across the software development life cycle — semantically linking customer feedback, architecture decisions, design documents, tickets, GitHub activity, incident reports, and compliance rules for each domain. The practical impact is substantial: questions that once required weeks of interviews with multiple subject-matter experts can be answered in minutes by a "librarian" agent drawing on structured institutional memory, every decision becomes traceable back to its source (customer survey data, usage analytics), and implicit tribal knowledge becomes explicit and explainable — reducing new-team-member ramp-up time and strengthening governance in the process. McKinsey specifically cautions against starting this effort with a grand, top-down ontology project; instead, the graph should evolve organically around priority domains and live programs, compounding value over time as knowledge shifts from static documentation into production infrastructure. The organizational payoff of all this — smaller, more capable teams doing materially more work — requires three deliberate follow-through moves to actually capture, rather than simply letting productivity gains evaporate into unmeasured slack: reskilling people, with a central team developing and maintaining "factories" of agents (standardization, compliance, best practice) while software engineers throughout the organization develop judgment, code review, and supervisory skills, shifting roles away from manual coordination and testing toward architecture coherence, domain modeling, and AI supervision; ensuring "outer loop" roles — support and compliance functions in risk, legal, testing, and procurement — are embedded as part of the agentic development effort from the start (for example through policy as code) rather than becoming an end-stage bottleneck that negates the speed gained upstream; and redesigning how freed capacity is allocated, since productivity improvements only translate into new value if that freed capacity is deliberately reinvested to accelerate roadmaps, modernize platforms, or launch new products rather than simply being absorbed without a plan. McKinsey recommends organizations begin this transformation where impact is greatest — targeting the small number of large programs (legacy modernization, brownfield rebuilds, new product launches) that typically account for the majority of a technology organization's total spend — since as agents take on more execution at scale, human roles concentrate increasingly in architecture, product judgment, and system design, making institutional knowledge and technical coherence the decisive competitive differentiators going forward. <span class="src">Rewiring software delivery for the agentic era</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source reconciles how a tech services provider is supposed to simultaneously help a client build in-house agentic capability (one of the "four value propositions") while that same in-house capability is the structural force compressing the provider's core business by 20-30% — the tension between these two roles isn't directly addressed.
- The "24-hour sprint" source doesn't specify what happens when overnight agent work introduces a defect that isn't caught until the following day's review — whether this is treated as an acceptable cost of the compressed cycle time, or whether it undermines the reported productivity gains once debugging and rework are factored in.
