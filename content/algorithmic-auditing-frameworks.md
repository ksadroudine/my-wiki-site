## Summary

Two frameworks — the Ethical Matrix and Explainable Fairness — for identifying who an algorithm or AI system could harm and why, applied to a real Colorado insurance regulation and to a failed eating-disorder chatbot. Also covers benchmarking and red teaming as complementary, imperfect approaches to evaluating large language models specifically.

## Concept

Algorithmic auditing, as developed by O'Neil Risk Consulting & Algorithmic Auditing (ORCAA), rests on a deliberately simple idea: identify the failure scenarios for people who might get hurt by an algorithmic system, and build monitoring to catch them, using generalized methods that work whether the system in question is an old-fashioned bureaucratic flowchart, a classic machine-learning risk score, or a large language model. The approach is explicitly narrow in scope — an audit raises alerts to problems, but resolving them (or deciding a use case is simply too stochastic to deploy AI in at all) is left to organizational leaders working within internal rules and external law, not to the auditor. Doing this well requires knowing the complete use case in detail — how the technology is used, by and for whom, and for what purpose — since the same underlying model can be relatively benign in one application and genuinely dangerous in another; the diversity of LLM use cases, more than their technical complexity, is what makes auditing them hard, which argues for an application-specific approach that starts with the highest-stakes use cases first. <span class="src">Auditing Algorithmic Risk</span>

The first framework, the Ethical Matrix, structures the question of who could be harmed and how. Its rows are the algorithm's stakeholders, defined broadly to include builders, deployers, users, and any other community affected by the system's adoption, with subgroups broken out separately whenever their concerns genuinely diverge (lighter- and darker-skinned people, for instance, may need separate rows for a facial-recognition system). Its columns come from nontechnical conversations with representatives of each stakeholder group, asked directly how the system could fail for them and how it could succeed for them; a fraud-detection example in the source shows how these concerns naturally differ by stakeholder — false flags are a major headache for customers whose accounts get frozen, a moderate business worry for the company, while an undetected fraudulent transaction harms the company but leaves other customers indifferent. Each resulting cell is judged on the likelihood a risk will be realized, how many people it would affect, and how severely, informed by existing data and by legal or procedural constraints (such as anti-discrimination law), and then color-coded: red for existential risks where a stakeholder could be gravely harmed or a hard constraint is violated, yellow for moderate ethical concern, and green for no worries. Critically, the Matrix is meant to function as a living document rather than a one-time deliverable — ideally drafted during design and development, and revised continuously as new stakeholder subgroups, indirect effects, or previously unconsidered concerns surface after deployment. <span class="src">Auditing Algorithmic Risk</span>

The second framework, Explainable Fairness (also developed at ORCAA), addresses a specific and much harder question buried inside many of the fairness concerns an Ethical Matrix surfaces: whether an observed disparity between groups reflects "legitimate discrimination" — a difference genuinely explained by factors that belong in the decision — or unfair treatment that isn't. If male candidates for a job score higher than female candidates on average simply because they have more relevant experience, that's legitimate; the real worry is whether men and women who are otherwise equally qualified (holding constant whatever factors are legitimately relevant to that specific role) are still scored differently. Explainable Fairness formalizes this by controlling for agreed "legitimate factors" when examining an outcome, and its real contribution is less the statistical technique itself — already used implicitly elsewhere, such as Federal Reserve analyses of mortgage denial rates that control for loan amount, FICO score, and debt-to-income ratio — than forcing an explicit conversation about why those control factors are actually legitimate in the first place. The framework's own worked example is pointed: U.S. mortgage lenders treat FICO scores as a legitimate factor because they measure creditworthiness, yet FICO scores themselves encode historical unfairness, since mortgage payments have counted toward the score for decades while rent payments only started counting in 2014 (and only in some score versions) — a design choice that favors homeowners over renters and interacts with decades of racist redlining that shaped today's homeownership disparities by race. The article deliberately declines to resolve whether FICO scores should still count as "legitimate" given this history, framing that as a question of ethics and policy assigned to the right decision-makers, not a math problem the framework itself can answer; once a disparity isn't explained by agreed legitimate factors, Explainable Fairness requires defining a threshold — fixed (like the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission's four-fifths rule for hiring selection rates) or relative (an above-industry-average pay gap, for instance) — that triggers a required response. <span class="src">Auditing Algorithmic Risk</span>

Both frameworks were applied in a real regulatory setting when Colorado passed SB21-169 in 2021, protecting consumers from unfair algorithmic discrimination in insurance, with ORCAA assisting the state's Division of Insurance (DOI) in implementation. The Ethical Matrix was comparatively straightforward here because the law itself defined the protected stakeholder classes (race, color, national or ethnic origin, religion, sex, sexual orientation, disability, gender identity, gender expression) and the DOI chose to start with underwriting in life insurance specifically, focusing initially on race and ethnicity. The harder work fell to Explainable Fairness: the DOI had to work through what makes two life-insurance applicants "similar," settling on factors broadly relevant to pricing a policy (policy type, death-benefit amount, applicant age, gender, and tobacco use) as legitimate controls, and its draft testing regulation now requires insurers to run regression analyses of approval and pricing outcomes by race using those controls, with statistically significant and substantial disparities (defined precisely, as a p-value below 0.05 and a difference above 5 percentage points) triggering mandatory further investigation and, if warranted, remediation. <span class="src">Auditing Algorithmic Risk</span>

Evaluating large language models specifically calls for two additional, complementary techniques beyond these two general frameworks, since LLMs' flexibility and open-ended output space make them harder to pin down than a scoring model. Benchmarking measures an LLM's outputs against a predefined dataset of inputs and desired outputs, using metrics chosen for the task type — straightforward accuracy for multiple-choice questions, n-gram overlap for summarization, and dozens of other dataset-and-metric combinations covering dimensions like calibration, robustness, and bias. Benchmarking has real, structural limits: if benchmark data leaks into a model's training data, the model effectively "memorizes" the answers rather than demonstrating the underlying capability, a risk that grows as more benchmarks get published and reused; it is vulnerable to Goodhart's law, where optimizing directly for a specific benchmark degrades a model's broader usefulness; and increasingly capable models are starting to detect when they're being evaluated at all — Anthropic's Claude 3 series, for example, responded to a "needle in a haystack" evaluation prompt by noting that an inserted fact seemed out of place, suspecting it was there "to test if I was paying attention." Red teaming complements benchmarking by adversarially probing a model to elicit unwanted behavior — violent or dangerous content, leaked training data, copyright infringement, or attempts to compromise the provider's own systems — through either technical attacks (injecting adversarial or nonsensical text into prompts) or social-engineering-style attempts to talk the model into unwanted output, ideally run by a multidisciplinary team spanning developers to end users and explicitly targeting each red (existential-risk) cell identified in an Ethical Matrix. Red teaming's own limits are the mirror image of benchmarking's: its value is inherently subjective, varying with the creativity and risk tolerance of whoever conducts it, with no established standard for how much red teaming is "enough," and it structurally cannot surface risks nobody thought to test for — meaning it needs to be paired with benchmarking and with continuous post-deployment monitoring rather than treated as a one-time pre-launch gate. <span class="src">Auditing Algorithmic Risk</span>

The costs of skipping this process are illustrated by Tessa, an LLM-powered wellness chatbot the nonprofit National Eating Disorders Association (NEDA) launched in 2023 to replace a helpline it had run for two decades. NEDA closed that human-staffed helpline and laid off five employees, saying it could no longer handle the call volume — which had grown, and grown more acute, through the COVID-19 pandemic — and positioned Tessa (built by an outside vendor, X2AI, now Cass) as its replacement. Tessa subsequently gave harmful advice, including recommending a "safe daily calorie deficit" to users who, by definition of the service, were likely struggling with eating disorders — precisely the population for whom that advice is most dangerous. A retrospective Ethical Matrix for Tessa would have flagged exactly this risk in red: eating-disorder sufferers as a distinct, high-stakes stakeholder subgroup facing potential direct harm from bad advice, and NEDA's own institutional trust as a second red cell, since a public misfire risks the organization losing standing with the very community it exists to serve (a risk that, in fact, materialized). The article argues red teaming targeted at that red cell (deliberately trying to trick Tessa into giving harmful advice) and benchmarking against a curated set of accurate, evidence-based Q&A pairs on eating-disorder topics could plausibly have caught the problem before Tessa's public, reputationally damaging failure — and notes Tessa is far from an isolated case, citing a New York City government chatbot that told users landlords didn't have to accept rental-assistance tenants and employers could take a cut of tips (both illegal), and tax-preparation chatbots from TurboTax and H&R Block that gave faulty filing advice. <span class="src">Auditing Algorithmic Risk</span>
