## Summary

Organizational structures have always followed the dominant technology of their era, and AI breaks the old constraint by adding intelligence to a project without adding people. The sources describe the change at three levels. At the level of structure, hierarchies flatten, middle managers' roles change and employees' hidden use of AI has to be brought into the open and channeled, as at BBVA. At the level of work, tasks are redesigned one by one, using approaches such as Design for Dialogue, which treats AI as a team member whose lead role shifts with the task, and sorting tasks by the cost of an error and the kind of knowledge needed. At the level of the company, some argue for rebuilding the business around AI agents in a separate unit. The sources also describe the frictions that stall transformation, the way identical use of AI erodes competitive advantage and the continuing need for human oversight as agents become more autonomous.

## Concept

Organizational structures have historically formed around the constraints of the dominant technology of their era. The modern org chart originated with the New York and Erie Railroad in 1855, when executive Daniel McCallum used the telegraph to coordinate a large workforce across a wide geography, Henry Ford's assembly line exploited mechanical clocks and standardized parts and Agile software development, formalized in 2001, exploited new tooling and internet communication. Each persisted for decades because it was built around fixed human limits, finite attention, the need to be physically present and the fact that adding capability meant adding people or making them work harder. AI breaks that constraint, since for the first time intelligence can be added to a project without adding headcount. <span class="src">Reshaping the tree rebuilding organizations for AI</span>

This is already happening informally. A 2023 Salesforce survey found that over half of employees using AI at work did so without organizational approval and 64% passed AI-assisted output off as their own, and this shadow AI use is possible because LLMs operate at human scale, reading documents, drafting emails and adapting to context without specialized training or bespoke integration, so anyone with a capable model can begin delegating tasks regardless of any rollout. Mollick's case study of a small educational-software team shows what redesigning a process around AI, rather than automating one step, looks like, since AI personas gave first-pass simulated user feedback, AI synthesized scattered feedback into one meeting agenda and shortened the meeting, and screenshot-to-code tools let the team build and iterate working prototypes live, reducing a process of about a week to one or two days, with autonomous agents expected to carry concepts to deployed code under human supervision. He proposes three principles, let teams develop and share their own methods because AI behaves more like a team member than an IT system and top-down mandates fit local contexts poorly, build processes for the more capable models expected soon since change takes time, and move quickly, with lightweight bottom-up guidelines rather than slow, centrally designed rollouts. <span class="src">Reshaping the tree rebuilding organizations for AI</span>

Empirical research on managers supports the structural shift. A Harvard Business School study led by Manuel Hoffmann followed 50,032 software developers from 2022 to 2024, half of whom used GitHub Copilot, classifying more than 2.4 million platform actions into core coding work and managerial work such as coordination and project management, and found that access shifted allocation toward core work by 5% and away from project management by 10%, because developers using gen AI worked more autonomously, needed less coordination and had time freed for exploration and learning. The authors argue that this lets companies flatten reporting structures, not by eliminating managers but by reducing how many are needed and redirecting the rest from coordination, scheduling and quality-checking to hands-on work and process improvement. Effects vary by skill, since less-skilled workers are more likely to trust AI guidance over their own judgment and so benefit disproportionately, which relieves managers of much of the upskilling of low performers. KPMG's head of Workforce Innovation, Edwige Sacco, corroborates it, since gen AI blurs the line between middle-manager and associate work, as associates who compress a five-hour client-discovery task to three hours reinvest the time in strategic work once reserved for managers while remaining managers focus on human-centric client conversations, reading between the lines and articulating a client's real problem. <span class="src">How AI Is Redefining Managerial Roles</span>

The same flattening appears with physical robots, according to a Management Science study by Bryan Hong and Lynn Wu of firms adopting industrial and collaborative robots. Robots do not make individual employees more autonomous as a coding copilot does, but they let performance be measured and compared much more precisely, since a worker's output beside a consistent, transparent robot is easy to isolate from teammates', so one manager can supervise more people, and each USD 1 million of robot investment was associated with roughly five more people in the average span of control. The effect is not simple job destruction, because middle-skilled roles such as machinists, welders and assembly workers decline, while robot-investing companies hire significantly more high-skilled workers to program, design and manage robots and more low-skilled workers for tasks robots cannot yet do reliably, as Amazon uses people to load and operate delivery vans, and the combined increase outweighs the middle-skill losses. At Stanley Black & Decker's robot-based tape-inspection system, robot-collected data revealed upstream quality patterns that neither executives nor frontline workers could see, and middle managers' cross-functional vantage point found the analytics opportunity and drove the fix, an instance of role redefinition rather than elimination. <span class="src">Why Robots Will Displace Managers — and Create Other Jobs</span>

Mollick's later article extends the argument to whether AI-enabled change tends toward control or coaching. Algorithmic management, as with Uber's driver ratings and UPS's route-efficiency tracking, already gave companies comprehensive, real-time and largely opaque visibility into worker behavior, which MIT Sloan's Katherine Kellogg and coauthors called categorically different from traditional supervision, and LLMs could make it more totalizing, tracking, targeting, evaluating and rewarding workers while delivering feedback that feels warm and personal, which could make comprehensive surveillance more palatable without changing who is in charge, in effect covering the Skinner box in bright wrapping paper. The trajectory is a choice, since organizations that let AI remove the most tedious parts of a job, leaving more interesting work, see workers respond positively in ways that support engagement and retention. Because workers who use AI productively may hide it for fear for their jobs, surfacing shadow use takes concrete incentives, namely cash prizes, promotions and flexible work arrangements for employees who find substantial AI-driven process improvements, not merely permission. <span class="src">Reinventing the Organization for GenAI and LLMs</span>

BBVA, a bank with 125,000 employees in 25 countries, supplies the enterprise answer to what to do about a shadow AI economy. Most corporate programs treat unauthorized consumer-AI use as a compliance problem when it is a demand signal, since an industry report found that although only 40% of companies had bought official LLM subscriptions, employees at more than 90% of those companies reported regular personal AI use for work. BBVA treated AI as an assistant rather than a replacement, gave employees autonomy with accountability and built a peer network of Champions and Wizards, formalized power users, about 25 Champions and 200 Wizards, supported by a Community of Practice that became the most active internal forum in company history. It moved fast, compressing legal and GDPR review into two months, and scaled through engineered scarcity, issuing only 3,000 ChatGPT Enterprise licenses under a use-it-or-lose-it policy that reassigned unused ones, which made access a status signal and grew usage to 11,000 active users within a year, over 4,800 employee-built custom GPTs and time savings of 2-5 hours a week, with 3-4 hours among the 600-person internal audit team, which reached 99% adoption. <span class="src">The Hidden Demand for AI Inside Your Company</span>

Governance stayed centralized where it mattered most, with a hard rule that outputs could never write directly to core systems without human validation, mandatory training and a signed accountability document for every user, and an automated Quality Score evaluating the guardrails and clarity of thousands of employee-created GPTs, since manual review at that scale was infeasible. The transferable lessons are to secure the environment quickly rather than study the problem at length, make access attractive through scarcity rather than mandate, designate power users to spread tacit knowledge, train managers concretely before asking them to sponsor change and enforce human ownership of AI-assisted work, and BBVA's experience is an empirical proof of concept that shadow use signals demand and that surfacing it needs incentives, with operational detail the theoretical pieces lack. <span class="src">The Hidden Demand for AI Inside Your Company</span> <span class="src">Reinventing the Organization for GenAI and LLMs</span> <span class="src">Reshaping the tree rebuilding organizations for AI</span>

Process redesign is the second scale, and HBR's earliest source argues that the standard playbook for integrating technology does not fit generative AI. Michael Hammer's 1990 business process reengineering, with its dictum to obliterate rather than automate, assigns each task to a human or a fixed automated step and locks the workflow in place, which suits technologies with fixed capabilities but not an iterative, dialogue-based, continually improving one. The alternative, Design for Dialogue, treats AI like a team member whose leadership on a task shifts with context and competence, as a high-performing team reallocates leadership during a project, and has three components, task analysis, assessing which of human or AI should lead each component task, interaction protocols, rules for handoffs such as AI escalating when it detects an anomaly, negative sentiment or low confidence, and feedback loops such as error reporting, satisfaction surveys and AI decision explanations that keep the collaboration improving. At Jerry, a USD 450 million car-ownership platform handling over 200,000 customer messages a month, installing a gen AI solution in May 2023 moved customer service from humans leading all interactions and answering 54% of inquiries within 24 hours to AI leading all of them and answering over 96% within 30 seconds by June 2023, projecting USD 4 million in annual savings with headcount maintained, since AI absorbed information-hunting and left agents the escalations that its protocols flagged. The four-step sequence is to identify high-value WINS work, rich in words, images, numbers and sounds, do the task analysis, design protocols and feedback, then train, evaluate and scale. <span class="src">Your Organization Isn’t Designed to Work with GenAI</span>

A second HBR piece frames generative AI as a second wave of Toyota's kaizen, continuous incremental improvement by empowered employees, made universal because natural-language interfaces put process-improvement capability in nontechnical hands. At Mercedes-Benz, shop-floor employees query production data in plain language through the MO360 Data Platform while Turn2Learn offers more than 40,000 AI and data courses and GitHub Copilot frees developers to focus on complex integration, Merck used generative adversarial networks and variational autoencoders to synthesize defect-image training data, cutting false rejects in automated drug inspection by more than 50% where real examples of rare defects were scarce, a single NASA research engineer used commercial AI design software to cut the creation of one-off spacecraft components from months to one to two hours with structures two-thirds lighter and ten times more stress-resistant, and Sereact's PickGPT lets warehouse operators direct robotic picking in plain language. Autonomous agents are kaizen's next stage, from DoNotPay's agent canceling a CEO's unnecessary subscriptions to a Stanford multi-agent system that identified workflow steps with 93% accuracy in real hospital revenue-cycle and B2B invoice processes, outperforming robotic process automation because foundation-model agents can reason through the variations and exceptions that trip up hard-coded bots, although human oversight, alignment and feedback remain the linchpin. <span class="src">The Secret to Successful AI-Driven Process Redesign</span>

MIT Sloan Management Review supplies a structured method, deconstruct, redeploy and reconstruct, which treats work as a fluid set of discrete tasks rather than fixed jobs and favors a work-backward approach that starts from the tasks needing doing over a tech-forward one that starts from what technology can automate, on the grounds that AI, gig platforms and agile talent models are bending the demand curve of work and decoupling productivity from resource intensity. At a global financial-services company redesigning a customer-order process, 68 tasks were automated outright, 17 shifted from senior managers to junior staff with generative AI support, 29 analytically demanding tasks were redeployed to an existing global capability center and 14 senior tasks such as client relationship management were augmented, producing a 50% reduction in workforce hours, a 40% reduction in operating cost and an 18% reduction in turnover that the original technology-only business case had not anticipated. The five steps are to start with the work, consider the entire technology stack, as the case combined gen AI with existing RPA and machine learning, think beyond AI to where tasks are best performed, including by junior talent or capability centers, plan how freed senior capacity will be redeployed and treat work design as a permanent capability. <span class="src">Want AI-Driven Productivity Redesign Work</span>

Classical business process management offers an older and complementary discipline. The 1990s reengineering movement fell out of favor after failed projects and layoffs, but Davenport and Redman argue it is ripe for revival because AI and process management reinforce each other, as AI can scale a well-managed process beyond manual redesign and an end-to-end managed process produces the clean, consistent data an AI system needs. Their seven steps are to establish a cross-departmental process owner, identify who the process serves, map the existing process, increasingly through AI-driven process mining, set targets, select enablers from RPA for narrow processes to gen AI and traditional ML for broader ones, redesign with AI help, as Deutsche Telekom used Pega's Blueprint tool to streamline 250 of roughly 800 global HR processes by letting business experts describe workflows in plain language, and implement with continuous process-mining monitoring. PepsiCo's multiyear program, which began with accounts payable in 2019 and extended to nine more processes including order-to-cash, saved thousands of labor hours and millions in write-offs and used AI to diagnose and cut the order-rejection rate after a new SAP rollout from 30% to 4%, showing process mining as both redesign diagnostic and ongoing control, Mars Wrigley's supply chain uses a digital twin of its production line, decision-intelligence software and better truck utilization, and Siemens, BMW and Reckitt run standardized cross-functional order-to-cash processes, which suit the large cross-departmental processes that task-level frameworks do not directly address. <span class="src">How to Marry Process Management and AI</span>

A task-level decision framework sits beneath these methods. Bharat Anand and Andy Wu argue that leaders ask the wrong question when they focus on how intelligent gen AI is, and that the right one is how to use it effectively today, task by task. They cross the cost of errors with the type of knowledge needed, explicit or tacit, such as empathy, ethical reasoning and contextual judgment, into four zones. In no regrets, with low cost and explicit knowledge, AI simply does the task, such as screening résumés or approving low-dollar reimbursements, since value comes from speed and scale and breakthroughs often come from work previously too tedious or expensive to do at all. In creative catalyst, with low cost and tacit knowledge, AI generates options such as ad taglines or design variations for a human to select, because no correct creative output exists to get wrong. In quality control, with high cost and explicit knowledge, such as legal contracts, code and financial due diligence, AI produces and a human verifies, and in human-first, with high cost and tacit knowledge, such as hiring, strategy and crisis management, AI may assist but never leads. <span class="src">The Gen AI Playbook for Organizations</span>

The same article's paradox of access explains why redesign must be differentiated. Because every competitor can use the same tools, applying them to the same tasks the same way erodes advantage, as margins flow to customers and suppliers, as with airline e-ticketing and CAD and ERP software that became table stakes, and customers and suppliers can use gen AI to disintermediate a firm, as corporate legal departments increasingly pull work in-house from outside law firms. Differentiation comes from applying gen AI differently and faster than rivals who stay paralyzed by imperfect accuracy, from proprietary, centralized data that makes AI perform better inside one organization, and from complementary assets, unique people, processes and culture, that make a deployment more valuable in one firm's hands. It also warns that freed-up time does not automatically become profit, since without deliberate management saved hours evaporate into tinkering or busywork, so managers must track the time saved, set expectations for its redeployment and tie recognition to effective redeployment. <span class="src">The Gen AI Playbook for Organizations</span>

Why redesign stalls is the subject of a Harvard Business School and Microsoft summit of senior leaders from a dozen large organizations, enthusiastic adopters several of which ran hundreds of deployments, who found that the main obstacle is rarely model quality or data availability but the last mile where technical capability meets organizational design. Seven frictions recur, ungeneralizable pilots, as at a global investment bank with more than 250 LLM-connected applications and no repeatable path to a standard operating model, a productivity gap in which individual time savings are reabsorbed into low-value work instead of being harvested structurally, process debt that AI exposes faster than it can be resolved, so that an agent drafts a contract in seconds that then waits in a two-week manual legal queue, an identity problem in which asking experts to externalize tribal knowledge threatens their professional worth and must be treated as identity work, an agentic-governance gap in which human-in-the-loop controls that work for isolated cases collapse under multi-agent architectures, as one asset-servicing firm with over 100 agents and plans for tens of thousands found, architectural complexity from multi-hyperscaler stacks and an efficiency trap in which framing AI as cost reduction narrows ambition and triggers defensive behavior by middle management. <span class="src">The “Last Mile” Problem Slowing AI Transformation</span>

The four-pillar blueprint answers them. Clean-sheet process redesign asks how a process would be built today with agents as first-class participants rather than retrofitting AI onto an unchanged workflow, strategic knowledge capture treats tribal knowledge as a strategic asset and presents it to experts as legacy-building rather than obsolescence, managing the digital workforce uses agentic control planes that monitor agent performance and permissions as an HR system tracks people, which raises questions of how a digital worker is onboarded, evaluated, secured and retired, and role redesign and career pathing shift human roles toward orchestration, verification and storytelling around AI output so that adoption reads as career evolution and not erosion. <span class="src">The “Last Mile” Problem Slowing AI Transformation</span>

The third scale asks how an entire company could be rebuilt around agents, according to Salim Ismail, co-author of Exponential Organizations, in a podcast interview that presents his framework as the successor to that book. It starts from Ronald Coase's 1937 insight that companies exist because internal coordination is cheaper than external transaction, extended by Herbert Simon, Clayton Christensen and the exponential-organizations model, which used community, crowd and platforms to push boundaries outward, and argues that agentic AI breaks the logic, since a website that needs branding, privacy and IT approvals inside a large company can be built in minutes by prompting an external tool that knows the brand guidelines, so that building the feature is cheaper than having the meeting about the feature. Organizations remain necessary only for a fiduciary wedge, the company as a legal, liability-bearing container, like a special purpose vehicle, that holds IP, assets, agents and fewer humans and covers the gap between what AI can be trusted to decide unsupervised and where human judgment and legal accountability are required. <span class="src">The Organizational Singularity AI-Proof Your Company EP #258</span>

Inside that container the architecture has three parts. A Massive Transformative Purpose is operationalized as a protocol that constrains what agents may do rather than as a poster, an intelligence engine modeled loosely on the military OODA loop runs through six layers, purpose, sensing, interpretation, decision, orchestration and learning, so that, for example, a competitor's same-day-delivery announcement is picked up by sensing agents, assessed by interpretation agents, resolved by decision agents into matching, acquiring a startup or ignoring and executed by an orchestration agent that coordinates legal, M&A and other functions, and a govern-and-assure layer wraps the loop with a searchable audit log for every agent, granular rollback and a human review queue, framed as a response to agents going rogue, such as one that deleted volumes of rental car data, with each agent carrying a passport of metadata that defines permitted data and actions. Human roles shift, as the C-suite becomes an accountability and evaluation layer, middle management, described as roughly 90% coordination work, is expected to shrink most, accounting for an estimated 60% of the headcount reduction with the rest split between front-line and senior roles, and the framework estimates a transitioned company could run on 10-25% of its previous headcount, depending on how physical or regulated the business is, with a proposed 1-to-20 manager-to-contributor ratio against roughly 1-to-3 or 1-to-5 today. <span class="src">The Organizational Singularity AI-Proof Your Company EP #258</span>

The recommended path never transforms the existing organization in place, on an argument attributed to Buckminster Fuller and illustrated by Nestlé's decade of failed attempts to run Nespresso as an internal line before spinning it out, that internal immune-system resistance defeats disruptive change from within. The method, called rewrite, starts with backcasting from a target future state and scores the organization on about seven dimensions, such as how many approval layers separate an idea from execution and whether AI is a first-class capability or an IT add-on, documents the most standardized workflows, flagging undocumented tacit knowledge and anecdotal evidence that some workers feed AI bad information to protect their jobs, builds a small separate AI-native digital twin at the edge with a forked copy of the relevant data, and replicates, never migrates, one workflow at a time, running it in parallel with the legacy process, quality-checking against it and replacing the original only when it shows a faster self-improvement loop. The edge unit reports directly to the CEO with board support while core operations stay untouched, reported gains are about 100x throughput per workflow, AI-native customer support and marketing content generation are cited as domains that have completed the transition, and the transition across the market is estimated at five to seven years, with laggards displaced by AI-native competitors. The diagnostic question for leaders is whether a two-or-three-person team using off-the-shelf AI could replicate a high-margin line within 60-90 days, with proprietary data, regulatory position, an intelligence moat from learning faster, customer relationships and brand as the defenses, and early evidence cited includes Cognition Labs' annual recurring revenue reportedly growing 73x after an agentic-native transition and an estimate that power-plant operator Fermi America could run with about 80 people instead of 800. <span class="src">The Organizational Singularity AI-Proof Your Company EP #258</span>

Across the three scales the sources share a warning against deploying AI as an isolated substitute bolted onto unchanged jobs, processes or governance, and several point to multi-agent, increasingly autonomous systems as the next stage of redesign, with human oversight remaining essential as agent capability grows. <span class="src">Your Organization Isn’t Designed to Work with GenAI</span> <span class="src">The Secret to Successful AI-Driven Process Redesign</span> <span class="src">Want AI-Driven Productivity Redesign Work</span> <span class="src">The “Last Mile” Problem Slowing AI Transformation</span> <span class="src">The Gen AI Playbook for Organizations</span> <span class="src">How to Marry Process Management and AI</span>
