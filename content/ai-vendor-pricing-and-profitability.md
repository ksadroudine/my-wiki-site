## Summary

Generative AI vendors price their products well below the true cost of running them, because every prompt costs real computing power while revenue per user stays low and the underlying models are becoming commodities. Andy Wu of Harvard Business School describes a bubble as a time when everyone can see the value being created but no one is thinking about how to capture it. Flat-fee subscriptions are in effect capped usage pricing, and the typical $20 monthly price does not cover costs for heavy users. Application vendors can respond by pricing at the highest layer of value they can measure, such as credits or outcomes, instead of per token, and several investors argue that current prices are too low for the value delivered.

## Concept

Harvard Business School's Andy Wu, interviewed by HBR off his June 2025 case study "AI Wars in 2025," frames the generative AI industry's central business problem as a structural mismatch between cost and revenue: inference — the compute expended every time a user submits a prompt and receives a response — carries a real, non-trivial variable cost (Wu cites the example of AI-generated images, which consume several cents of electricity and chip capacity apiece, and notes that OpenAI alone expects to spend more than $150 billion on inference through 2030), while the revenue vendors collect per use remains flat and low. This is, in Wu's account, a direct legacy of software-era pricing habits: subscription models made economic sense when the marginal cost of serving one more user of a piece of software was zero or near-zero, but generative AI breaks that assumption, since every additional prompt costs real money to serve. Today's AI "subscriptions," Wu argues, aren't really subscriptions at all — because they cap usage, they already function as usage-based pricing wearing a subscription's branding — but even so, the standard $20-a-month price point remains too low to cover the underlying variable cost for most services, in part simply because far fewer people are willing to pay $20 a month for an AI assistant than are willing to pay the same for Netflix. Wu expects the industry to eventually complete the transition to genuine pay-per-use pricing, drawing an analogy to the earlier, "annoying" historical transition from one-time software licenses to monthly subscriptions — a shift that took real time to complete and that he expects the coming usage-based transition to repeat, only more painfully. Compounding the pricing problem is Wu's view that the foundation model itself has become a commodity: intellectual-property protection in the space is weak, and well-funded new entrants like xAI's Grok and China's DeepSeek have been able to approach the capability of frontier labs like OpenAI with dramatically smaller investment, which caps how much any single vendor can safely raise prices without simply handing market share to a cheaper or free competitor. Wu's read of how public markets have actually rewarded gen AI winners cuts against the popular narrative that OpenAI-adjacent "pure plays" are the obvious beneficiaries: since the end of 2022, Nvidia — the "shovel seller" supplying the compute infrastructure everyone needs regardless of who wins the model race — has been the single biggest winner, and Meta, whose advertising and social-platform business is directly complementary to (rather than competitive with) generative AI capability, has meaningfully outperformed both Google and Microsoft, despite the latter two being far more closely associated with generative AI in the public imagination. Google in particular, Wu observes, has the technical capability to embed generative AI throughout its search product but has deliberately held back, because the search queries generative AI helps with most (e.g., "write my third-grade essay about dinosaurs") tend to be exactly the queries Google doesn't monetize anyway, while the commercial queries it does make money on — flight searches, shopping — are ones where generative AI currently offers little or no advantage over traditional search. Wu's working definition of a technology bubble — "when everyone can see the value creation, but no one is thinking about the value capture" — is the frame through which he reads current AI-bubble warnings, situating the current moment alongside the dot-com bubble, the sharing-economy bubble (WeWork, Bird), the pandemic-era work-from-home bubble (Zoom, Peloton), virtual reality, and multiple cryptocurrency cycles as recurring instances of the same underlying pattern in technology history. His central historical parallel is Pets.com: a genuinely valuable idea (home delivery of dog food) whose potential for value creation was obvious, but which collapsed because no real business model existed yet to capture that value at the time — value capture only became viable years later, when Chewy entered with improved underlying economics. Wu is careful to distinguish his own position from outright AI skepticism: gen AI's low barriers to entry make it genuinely valuable and widely available precisely because so many people and companies can create value with it, but that same openness is exactly what makes it hard for any single company to capture a durable share of the value it collectively creates — which is the risk he flags for the sector overall, not a prediction that gen AI itself lacks worth. <span class="src">AI Companies Don’t Have a Profitable Business Model. Does That Matter</span>

The same underpricing dynamic that undermines AI vendors' own profitability is, from a different vantage point, exactly what worries some AI entrepreneurs about the labor market. Loti AI founder Luke Arrigoni frames the comparison starkly: he earned $63,000 in his first junior developer job in 2007, and today believes AI coding tools that write better code than he did then cost about $120 a year — a gap he argues gives companies a direct economic incentive to eliminate entry-level roles, since there is currently no offsetting incentive to keep hiring inexperienced humans instead. Decagon CEO Jesse Zhang embraces the same logic from the vendor side rather than resisting it: his company deliberately prices its customer-service chatbot at $1 or less per conversation, roughly half the cost of equivalent human support, because he believes undercutting human labor cost is simply the point of the technology — "you're going to be less than human labor. That's kind of like the point of technology" — even while declining to say whether Decagon is profitable overall, since $100 million in venture funding gives the company room to prioritize growth over near-term margin. Not every voice in the space agrees current pricing reflects true value, however. Redpoint managing director (and former GitHub COO) Erica Brescia called Google's $250-a-month AI Ultra plan "so cheap" it's "disproportionate to the value people are getting" and would set the price at least double; she separately estimates that GitHub Copilot, which launched at $10 a month in 2022 specifically to attract a critical mass of users and gather improvement data (with Microsoft comfortable absorbing a loss to make that happen), would better reflect the value it delivers developers today at roughly 100 times its current $21-a-month price. Zed CEO Nathan Sobo expects AI pricing to rise over time because current models aren't sustainable, but frames keeping AI affordable relative to human labor as a deliberate goal rather than an unfortunate side effect, since he wants "as much intelligence at my disposal at as low a cost as possible," explicitly including a junior engineer's access to that intelligence. Economist Simon Johnson of MIT doesn't expect AI vendors to price in the social cost of career disruption to new workers on their own initiative, and instead argues the more realistic lever is government policy — specifically lowering payroll taxes on entry-level roles to directly reduce employers' cost of hiring inexperienced humans, rather than relying on AI vendors to voluntarily raise their own prices. Arrigoni, for his part, has chosen a third path at his own company: steadily hiring junior engineers and deliberately avoiding AI coding tools in his own hiring pipeline, saying of a potential AI-driven "job apocalypse" for new graduates, "I don't want to be at fault." <span class="src">AI Agents Are Too Cheap for Our Own Good</span>

<span class="src">You are not a model. Don’t price per token.</span> (a16z, August 2026) addresses the opposite problem from the underpricing described above, namely how applications should price once they sit above the model layer. Token pricing imports the model provider's falling cost curve, trains buyers to compare an application with raw compute, and gives away data and workflow value, while buyers' real needs are comparability and spend allocation, which transparency of usage can meet without making tokens the billing meter. Hybrids of seats, credits, token pass-through and outcome fees are acceptable provided each meter fits its layer. <span class="src">You are not a model. Don’t price per token.</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses whether the entry-level-jobs concern (AI Agents Are Too Cheap) and the vendor-unprofitability concern (AI Companies Don't Have a Profitable Business Model) are actually in tension — if AI vendors did raise prices enough to become profitable, as Brescia and Wu's pay-per-use logic both suggest is coming, would that also resolve Arrigoni's worry about entry-level job displacement, or does the job-displacement risk persist even at "correctly priced" AI given the productivity gap involved?
- The sources don't reconcile Wu's commodity/low-differentiation argument with Decagon's and Zed's deliberate below-human-cost pricing strategy — it's unclear whether intentional underpricing (as a market-share strategy) versus structural commodity pricing (as a competitive constraint) are being conflated across the two articles.
- The buyer preference finding rests on a survey of 50 technical buyers, and the sample's composition is not described.
