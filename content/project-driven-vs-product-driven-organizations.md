## Summary

Two HBR articles prescribe opposite ways of organizing digital work. One argues that the entire enterprise should become project-driven around a fast pipeline of short initiatives, while the other argues that IT should abandon project-based delivery for permanent digital product teams, and reconciling them suggests the real divide is between temporary infrastructure-style work suited to projects and ongoing customer-facing systems suited to products.

## Concept

Two HBR articles on how large organizations should structure digital work were published within about two months of each other in early 2026, and stake out prescriptions that are, on their face, direct opposites. Antonio Nieto-Rodriguez's "The Project-Driven Organization" argues that companies have already arrived at a moment where projects — not steady-state operations — are the primary engine of value creation, a shift he traces back to the COVID-19 pandemic's forced compression of years-long digital transformations into weeks, and one he first identified in a 2021 HBR piece, "The Project Economy Has Arrived." In the years since, he notes, companies have invested seriously in project-management maturity — training more project managers, establishing project management offices and centers of excellence, upskilling middle managers, introducing better governance — with real gains in execution consistency and framework clarity. Yet failure rates have remained stubbornly high and value creation elusive, which Nieto-Rodriguez attributes to organizations still approaching projects with an underlying operational mindset that prioritizes hierarchy, control, stability, and efficiency over adaptability. His diagnosis lists recurring missteps: committing to too many initiatives and spreading resources thin, mistaking output for value and measuring what's easy rather than what matters, failing to deploy fully dedicated cross-functional teams, relying on long complex transformation efforts instead of short manageable ones, not appointing genuinely accountable executive sponsors, outsourcing transformation capability to consultants rather than building it internally, and failing to kill underperforming projects out of fear of blame. His proposed solution is the "project-driven organization," which he positions as the next evolutionary step beyond the agile organization — a 20-year-old model he credits with real innovations (smaller teams, shorter feedback loops, customer-centric design, iterative development) but argues was never designed to handle the scale and pace of enterprise-wide transformation that AI and compressed decision cycles now demand. Rather than simply optimizing individual teams or workflows the way agile does, the project-driven organization redesigns the entire enterprise — structure, leadership, and culture — around a continuous pipeline of dynamic, empowered, cross-functional project teams, requiring leaders to pull eight specific levers across three categories: organizational design (shifting culture away from risk avoidance, breaking down silos in structure, and retooling governance to enable decentralized decision-making — illustrated by Haier's more than 4,000 self-managed microenterprises, each accountable for its own P&L, and Bayer's elimination of 95% of its middle-management layers in favor of more than 5,000 autonomous teams operating on rolling 90-day cycles); leadership (ruthless but fluid prioritization of a small number of strategic initiatives, illustrated by Repsol concentrating 450 candidate ideas down to a few high-impact priorities like its Waylet payment app, which reached 9 million users; rapid deployment of people to where they create the most value, with fully dedicated rather than fractionally-staffed teams; and shifting performance management from efficiency metrics to impact metrics, illustrated by Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 project-management office using real-time reporting to reallocate resources across hundreds of initiatives); and value generation (turning operational support functions into enablers rather than gatekeepers, illustrated by Haier's internal shared-service platforms operating like market-facing providers that project teams choose based on performance; and an adaptive, iterative execution approach that breaks major transformations into three-to-six-month projects with dedicated teams and measurable benefits, increasingly assisted by AI forecasting tools like Octant AI that flag budget overruns and timeline risk before they occur). Nieto-Rodriguez frames automation and AI explicitly as accelerants of this shift, not competitors to it — citing IBM's automation of 90% of routine HR tasks (which freed talent for higher-value work via attrition and redeployment rather than mass layoffs) as evidence that AI-driven efficiency gains fund, rather than replace, the shift toward project-centered value creation. <span class="src">The Project-Driven Organization</span>

Ryan Nelson and Thomas Davenport's "Why the Digital Product Model Beats Project-Based Approaches," published roughly two months later in the same outlet, argues for a strikingly different structural answer to what is, in significant part, the same underlying problem: persistently poor delivery outcomes in enterprise technology work. Where Nieto-Rodriguez diagnoses the failure as insufficient commitment to project-centric organizing, Nelson and Davenport diagnose it as an inherent structural flaw in the project construct itself, at least for IT-delivered digital work specifically. Citing Standish Group data that only 31% of global IT projects succeed (with some other sources putting failure rates as high as 85%), and warning that AI adoption will likely make project outcomes even less predictable if companies don't change course, they trace the core problem to what a project fundamentally is: an effort defined around building a system rather than achieving a business outcome, whose success or intended user is often engaged with only when necessary, and which — once launched — disbands its team and largely stops learning how the solution actually performs or needs to evolve. Their proposed alternative, digital product management, differs from project management along several structural dimensions: teams are permanent rather than temporary, staying with a product for its full life and eventually managing it as something closer to its own P&L, rather than being staffed, launched, and redeployed; funding is ongoing and tied to usage and returns rather than a one-time upfront budget that incentivizes managers to ask for everything at the start; governance shifts from milestone inspection to outcome review (what changed for customers, what did the team learn, what should be built next); and success is measured by adoption, retention, and revenue rather than on-time/on-budget delivery — a shift Nelson and Davenport summarize as risk management moving from delivery-focused concerns (schedule slips, cost overruns) toward value-focused concerns (building the wrong thing, poor usability, weak market fit). The scale of the payoff they cite is substantial: McKinsey research found companies with a strong digital-product focus generate roughly 60% higher total shareholder returns and 16% higher operating margins than peers with a weak product focus, along with 40-70% shorter time to market — yet despite that evidence, a 2023 Planview report found only 8% of enterprises had actually completed the transition from projects to products, which Nelson and Davenport attribute largely to the absence of a C-suite mandate rather than to any inherent difficulty of the model itself. Their five-step framework for making the transition — identify a small number of initiatives genuinely suited to a product lens, form a couple of pilot teams and validate value/feasibility/scale before expanding, define a product vision and cascading KPIs, staff permanent cross-functional teams led by a dedicated product manager, and build lasting product-friendly infrastructure while committing to a genuinely multiyear journey — is illustrated through The New York Times (which grew from $44 million in digital subscription revenue in 2011 to over $1.2 billion annually today by shifting from temporary IT project teams to permanent, P&L-owning product teams behind NYT Cooking, Wordle, The Daily, and Wirecutter), CarMax (which grew from a single four-person, six-week pilot into more than 70 permanent product teams over more than a decade, using biweekly "open house" show-and-tell sessions and paired "two-in-a-box" product/technology leadership to build momentum), and Capital One (which reframed itself top-down as "a technology company that does banking" and treated the transition as a multiyear operating-model change rather than a one-off initiative). Crucially, Nelson and Davenport do not argue projects should disappear entirely: they explicitly carve out an exception for short, one-off infrastructure work with no well-defined internal or external customer — a cloud migration, a language rewrite — describing this kind of work as "a poor fit for a product lens" that should remain a time-boxed project with a clear end state, since such efforts rarely justify the investment of a long-term, dedicated cross-functional team. <span class="src">Why the Digital Product Model Beats Project-Based Approaches</span>

Read side by side, the two articles' apparent contradiction resolves into something closer to a scope disagreement than a genuine disagreement about facts. Both authors are responding to the same underlying evidence — persistently poor enterprise technology delivery outcomes, an urgent need for organizations to move faster, and AI both accelerating the pace of change and raising the stakes of getting the operating model wrong — but they define their unit of analysis differently. Nieto-Rodriguez's "project-driven organization" argues for restructuring the entire enterprise (strategy, operations, customer experience, and technology together) around fast, dedicated, time-boxed initiatives with clear start and end points, positioning this explicitly as the successor to agile and as a broader claim than IT delivery specifically; the projects he describes at Haier, Bayer, Repsol, and Saudi Arabia's PIF span far beyond software and into organizational restructuring, physical operations, and national economic strategy. Nelson and Davenport's argument, by contrast, is narrower and specifically about how technology-delivered digital products and systems should be organized once launched — and their own explicit exception for short-lived, well-scoped infrastructure work with no ongoing customer relationship (cloud migrations, language rewrites) is, in substance, describing exactly the kind of short, dedicated, outcome-focused initiative Nieto-Rodriguez's project-driven model is built around. The tension that remains genuine, rather than merely apparent, is over ongoing, customer-facing digital systems specifically: Nieto-Rodriguez's model would organize even these around a rotating pipeline of time-boxed projects sponsored by executives and staffed with fully dedicated teams for each initiative's duration, while Nelson and Davenport argue precisely this kind of system needs a permanent team that never disbands, because a project's natural conclusion — launch, then redeployment — is what causes organizations to lose the ability to learn how a live system actually performs and needs to evolve over time. Both authors nonetheless converge on several points that likely matter more in practice than which label an organization adopts: both insist the underlying shift requires genuine top-down executive sponsorship and multiyear patience rather than a quick methodology swap (Nieto-Rodriguez's eight levers and Nelson and Davenport's five-step framework both stress structural and cultural change over new tools or frameworks); both explicitly credit AI as an accelerant that raises the urgency and stakes of getting the operating model right rather than as a reason to delay the shift; and both cite real organizations (Haier, Bayer, Repsol, and Saudi Arabia's PIF for the project-driven model; The New York Times, CarMax, and Capital One for the product model) that succeeded specifically by starting small, proving value quickly, and scaling deliberately over years — suggesting that however an organization labels its target operating model, the empirically supported path to it looks broadly similar. <span class="src">The Project-Driven Organization</span> <span class="src">Why the Digital Product Model Beats Project-Based Approaches</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither article addresses how an organization should handle a system that starts as clearly project-appropriate (e.g., a first-generation MVP) but evolves into something customer-facing and ongoing — at what point, and by what trigger, should a project team transition into a permanent product team, and who makes that call?
- Nieto-Rodriguez's project-driven examples (Haier, Bayer, Repsol, Saudi Arabia's PIF) are drawn from operations, manufacturing, energy, and national economic policy, while Nelson and Davenport's product examples (NYT, CarMax, Capital One) are all specifically digital/software products — it's unclear whether Nieto-Rodriguez's model, if applied narrowly to ongoing digital-product work specifically (rather than the entire enterprise), would actually produce different guidance than Nelson and Davenport's, or whether the apparent disagreement is partly an artifact of the two articles simply choosing different illustrative case studies.
