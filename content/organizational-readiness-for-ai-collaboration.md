## Summary

Workplace AI adoption depends less on how capable the models are than on solving predictable human and organizational problems. Employees have reasons to hide productivity gains, for example to avoid signaling that their job could be cut, and managers often lack output-based measures and use management systems designed for a workforce without AI. AI also produces workslop, which is polished-looking content with too little substance, reported by 40 percent of surveyed US workers in a single month. Companies that handle adoption well treat it as a change-management and incentive-design challenge and not a technology rollout, they reward output rather than effort, they separate generic AI skills from domain-specific ones, and they invest in middle managers, who absorb most of the strain. Johnson and Johnson's move from open experimentation to a central AI governance council shows a common maturation path.

## Highlights

- Employees have strong incentives to hide their AI-driven productivity gains from managers rather than report them — taking credit for AI-assisted work as their own, or avoiding any signal that a manager might conclude their job is automatable — a dynamic HBR frames through a Satya Nadella statistic (85% of managers think employees are slacking while 85% of employees say they're overworked) and the Economist illustrates concretely (workers avoiding phrases like "look boss, I'm redundant").
- Organizations should measure and reward output rather than input once AI is in the mix, since punishing or monitoring reduced effort for the same output only teaches employees to fake busyness; two viable responses are raising expected outcomes or directly rewarding employees who achieve existing outcomes with less effort, for instance through time credits or learning stipends.
- As AI commoditizes any given skill it can replicate, human differentiation shifts to skills AI is unlikely to master soon — emotional intelligence, curiosity, and the ability to ask better questions and vet AI's output rather than just consume it — and organizations can deliberately cultivate curiosity through knowledge gaps, rewarding "why" questions, and leaders modeling inquisitive behavior.
- Mid-level managers are identified as the highest-leverage, most commonly neglected investment for AI ROI, since they translate strategy into execution and are now expected to understand both AI capability and change-management skills on top of traditional management competencies — historically an underinvested role due to promotion practices that reward strong individual contributors and confident self-promoters rather than people-management skill.
- Across real deployments, the same behavioral problem recurs in different forms: a third-party AI sales-coaching tool at Glowforge was universally ignored by sales reps because it didn't fit into the team's existing workflow rhythm, while a functionally similar in-house tool succeeded once its output became a required part of a standing weekly manager-employee discussion — illustrating that workflow integration determines adoption more than raw tool quality.
- A large four-country executive survey (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, Macquarie University, Bank of England, and Bundesbank) found almost three-quarters of businesses already use AI in some way, yet 86% of bosses report no measurable labor-productivity impact over the past three years — a gap the reporting attributes to general-purpose technologies like AI taking years to show their full economic impact, echoing the historical pattern of electricity and the internet.
- Companies solving AI adoption at scale increasingly separate "horizontal" AI capabilities (generic tasks useful to nearly all knowledge workers, like drafting or research) from "vertical" ones (domain-specific skills like building a banking cashflow model), since vertical capability requires industry expertise that AI vendors are still working to acquire, and narrow, well-defined task scopes (small-claims debt collection at the startup Garfield; standardized retail return policies) produce far more reliable AI performance than open-ended ones.
- Johnson & Johnson's shift from a "let a thousand flowers bloom" experimentation phase to a centralized AI governance council illustrates a common organizational maturation arc: the company found 85% of its AI-generated value came from just 15% of its applications, prompting a move toward centrally vetting which AI projects get resourced and ensuring the underlying data access needed to support them.
- "Workslop" — AI-generated content that looks polished but lacks the substance to meaningfully advance a task — was reported by 40% of a 1,150-person U.S. workforce survey in the last month alone, costing an average of nearly two hours of rework per instance and carrying an estimated $186/month "invisible tax" per affected employee, or over $9 million per year for a 10,000-person organization.
- Workslop is a distinct failure mode from ordinary sloppy work because it offloads cognitive labor onto another human rather than onto a machine: the recipient must decode ambiguous or missing context, and about half of respondents subsequently rated the colleague who sent it as less creative, capable, reliable (42% less trustworthy), and intelligent than before, with a third saying they'd be less willing to work with that person again.
- Employees with a combination of high agency and optimism about AI ("pilots") use gen AI 75% more at work than low-agency, low-optimism employees ("passengers") and use it purposefully to enhance their own creativity, while passengers disproportionately use AI to avoid doing work altogether — the underlying behavioral split driving whether AI use produces workslop or genuine value.
- The proposed fix for workslop mirrors this concept's broader finding that indiscriminate AI mandates backfire: leaders who push "use AI everywhere, all the time" without guidance model a lack of discernment that translates directly into employees thoughtlessly pasting AI output into deliverables, whereas leaders who set clear quality norms and model purposeful use see better outcomes.
- A randomized controlled trial giving 640 Kenyan small business owners a GPT-4 WhatsApp business adviser found a near-zero average effect on revenue and profit that concealed a stark split — a 15% gain for owners already in the top half of performance versus a nearly 10% decline for those in the bottom half — because both groups received similar advice but only high performers had the judgment to act on context-specific suggestions rather than generic ones like "lower your prices," which eroded margins without generating offsetting business.
- Leaders deploying open-ended AI tools (as opposed to narrow, well-defined tasks like drafting or code review, where even struggling users tend to benefit) should audit for uneven effects across three areas — whether some groups adopt the tool less than others, whether different users' interactions with it differ in kind, and whether the resulting real-world decisions produce better outcomes for some users than others — and design for heterogeneity by giving weaker performers tighter guardrails, more contextual grounding, or escalation paths rather than assuming a single deployment will help everyone equally.
- At Novo Nordisk, one of the largest enterprise gen AI rollouts to date (a few hundred Copilot users in January 2024 growing to 20,000 by February 2025), employees saved 2.17 hours per week on average, but employee satisfaction with the tool was three times more strongly correlated with perceived improvements in work quality than with time saved — challenging the default assumption that raw efficiency is generative AI's primary value, since many employees reinvested saved time in people interactions, strategic planning, and creative work rather than treating it as a pure productivity win.
- HBR's 18 interviews at two consulting firms find AI adoption succeeds or fails at the middle layer, where managers must validate AI output, catch "workslop," coach teams and uphold quality under unchanged delivery pressure, while juniors and partners both report gains. <span class="src">AI Adoption Is Overloading Your Middle Managers</span>
- Role elevation reached juniors (days of desktop research cut to 30 minutes) and partners (selling AI-enhanced judgment), but managers' new duties were layered onto existing work; Gallup puts manager engagement at 22% in 2025, down from 30% in 2023. <span class="src">AI Adoption Is Overloading Your Middle Managers</span>
- Three breakdowns recur — informal learning against relentless delivery, incentives that reward billable hours over knowledge sharing, and leaders and managers living in different realities — and teams coped better by protecting learning time, building a searchable internal hub of tools and use cases, and joining working sessions. <span class="src">AI Adoption Is Overloading Your Middle Managers</span>

## Concept

<span class="src">Set Your Team Up to Collaborate with AI Successfully</span> (HBR, November 2024) offers five leadership principles for building an organization capable of genuine human-AI collaboration, starting from the premise that knowledge workers are less likely to lose their jobs to AI than to lose them to another human who uses AI more effectively. First, develop an explicit AI augmentation strategy that identifies where humans should redeploy the time AI frees up — illustrated by recruiters who save up to 40 percent of their time on resume screening and job-description editing, with the real value coming not from that automation itself but from redirecting the freed time toward higher-value human work like helping candidates understand their career fit. Second, shift performance evaluation from measuring input (hours, visible effort) to measuring output, since organizations that don't make this shift create exactly the "clandestine AI use" dynamic where employees achieve the same output with 30 to 40 percent less effort but conceal it rather than report it, for fear of simply being handed more work. Third, deliberately cultivate the human skills — emotional intelligence, curiosity, the ability to ask better questions and critically vet AI output — that remain differentiating even as AI commoditizes any specific skill it can replicate. Fourth, invest specifically in mid-level managers, historically an underinvested group promoted for individual technical performance rather than people-management skill, who are nonetheless the most consequential link between AI strategy and actual execution. Fifth, actively promote AI experimentation and psychological safety around AI-related failure, since senior leaders' visible skepticism or enthusiasm strongly shapes how comfortable the rest of the organization feels experimenting.

<span class="src">How to avoid common AI pitfalls in the workplace</span> (The Economist, January 2026) grounds these prescriptions in a wide-ranging survey of real corporate AI deployments, organized around behavioral, technical, and organizational problem categories. On the behavioral side, it documents the same clandestine-use dynamic HBR identifies, adding fresh texture from DBS bank (which runs reskilling programs to help customer-service agents transition into sales roles as their original tasks automate) and Glowforge (whose AI sales-coaching tool was universally ignored by reps until it was rebuilt to feed into a mandatory weekly manager discussion, proving that workflow fit determines adoption more than tool quality). On the technical side, the article details the "hidden costs" of AI deployment MIT's Rama Ramakrishnan identifies — adapting a model to a specific use case through fine-tuning and hallucination reduction, and building safeguards for high-stakes use cases, illustrated by Sierra's "supervisor model" that monitors AI-customer interactions in real time and escalates uncertain cases to humans. On the organizational side, it covers talent, data access, and evaluation difficulty (verifying code correctness is easy; verifying whether AI-generated humor or nuanced business judgment is "good enough" is not), and profiles Johnson & Johnson's shift from unrestricted AI experimentation — which produced a lot of "weeds" alongside a few genuinely valuable applications (85 percent of value from just 15 percent of applications) — toward a centralized AI governance council. The article also cites a major four-country executive survey (from the Atlanta Fed, Macquarie University, the Bank of England, and the Bundesbank) finding that although nearly three-quarters of businesses already use AI in some way, 86 percent of executives report no measurable labor-productivity impact over the past three years, attributing the gap to the historical pattern of general-purpose technologies (electricity, the internet) taking years to realize their full economic potential, since impact depends on organizational and behavioral adaptation, not just model capability.

<span class="src">AI-Generated “Workslop” Is Destroying Productivity</span> (HBR, September 2025) documents a third, closely related failure mode: research from BetterUp Labs and Stanford's Social Media Lab, drawing on a 1,150-person survey of U.S. full-time employees, coins "workslop" for AI-generated work content that looks polished but lacks the substance to meaningfully advance the task it's meant to serve. Unlike ordinary cognitive offloading to a machine (the kind of concern raised about search engines or, further back, writing itself), workslop offloads cognitive labor onto another human being — the recipient has to decode ambiguous or missing context, and often ends up redoing the work entirely. Forty percent of respondents reported receiving workslop in the past month, at an estimated cost of nearly two hours of rework per instance and a self-reported "invisible tax" of $186 per affected employee per month, scaling to more than $9 million annually for a 10,000-person organization; the interpersonal cost compounds the financial one, since roughly half of respondents rated a colleague who sent them workslop as less creative, capable, and reliable than before, and a third said they'd be less willing to work with that person again. The research traces workslop to indiscriminate AI mandates: when leaders push AI adoption everywhere without quality guidance, they model a lack of discernment that employees translate into thoughtlessly pasting AI output into deliverables. A parallel finding — that employees with high agency and optimism about AI ("pilots") use it 75 percent more at work and specifically to enhance their own creativity, while less engaged "passengers" disproportionately use AI to avoid doing work — offers one explanation for why the same technology produces genuine value in some hands and workslop in others. The article's prescriptions echo the other two sources directly: set clear quality norms rather than indiscriminate usage mandates, model purposeful AI use as a leader, and treat AI as a collaborative tool rather than a shortcut for offloading effort.

<span class="src">How AI Helps the Best and Hurts the Rest</span> (MIT SMR, April 2026) supplies causal, experimental evidence for the same underlying dynamic the other three sources describe qualitatively: that the value AI creates depends less on the tool than on the judgment of the person using it. Researchers ran a randomized controlled trial with 640 small business owners in Kenya, giving half of them WhatsApp access to a GPT-4 instance prompted as a business adviser and tracking sales and profits from May to November 2023. The average treatment effect was close to zero, but this masked a sharp split: entrepreneurs already in the top half of performance saw revenue and profit gains of 15%, while those in the bottom half saw declines of nearly 10%. Both groups asked similar numbers and types of questions and received similarly high-quality advice; the difference was entirely in what they chose to act on. Low performers disproportionately implemented generic, one-size-fits-all suggestions (cutting prices, boosting ad spend) that eroded margins without generating offsetting revenue, while high performers used the same tool to surface context-specific ideas — a cybercafe owner began renting gaming accessories, a car-wash owner added detergent sales and cold sodas, another found alternative power sources for local blackouts. The article frames this as a structural feature of open-ended, ambiguous tasks (managing a business) rather than narrow, well-defined ones (drafting an email, reviewing code), where prior research has found that struggling users benefit most from AI assistance precisely because the tool's output can be used with little modification. Its prescriptions for leaders directly extend this concept's change-management thesis: cultivate awareness that AI will not help everyone equally and that average effects can conceal serious harm to specific groups; design for heterogeneity by giving less experienced users tighter guardrails, more contextual grounding, or escalation paths to human support; and audit deployments for uneven effects across three areas — adoption (are some groups using the tool far less than others), the interactions themselves (are different users asking different questions or getting different outputs), and downstream outcomes (are the resulting real-world decisions producing better results for some users than others).

<span class="src">How to Scale GenAI in the Workplace</span> (MIT SMR, July 2025) studies one of the largest real-world generative AI deployments to date — Novo Nordisk's enterprisewide rollout of Microsoft Copilot, which grew from a few hundred users in January 2024 to 20,000 by February 2025 — and finds a result that complicates the efficiency-first framing most of the other sources in this concept push back against implicitly rather than empirically. Novo Nordisk launched the rollout under the leadership principle "time is the ultimate currency" and a "Make Your Time Count" campaign explicitly targeting productivity, and by that narrow measure it succeeded: employees saved 2.17 hours per week on average. But employee satisfaction with the tool turned out to be three times more strongly correlated with perceived improvements in work quality (better content summarization, content creation, and ideation) than with the raw hours saved, and many employees reported reinvesting freed-up time into people interactions, strategic planning, and creative work rather than simply working faster. This gives empirical backing to the other sources' shared warning that measuring AI adoption success by time saved alone misses where the real value and the real risk lie: an organization optimizing purely for efficiency gains, as this concept's other sources document, is the same one prone to producing workslop, hiding productivity gains, and rewarding busyness over genuine output improvement. <span class="src">How to Scale GenAI in the Workplace</span>

Read together, the five articles describe the same underlying challenge from complementary angles: HBR's two pieces specify what leaders should do to build an AI-ready organization and what specifically goes wrong when they don't (workslop being one concrete, richly quantified failure mode), the Economist documents, across a wide range of real companies, what actually happens when organizations try to adopt AI at scale, the MIT SMR field experiment supplies the causal evidence and a concrete audit methodology for the pattern all three describe, and the Novo Nordisk case study shows a real large-scale deployment discovering, empirically, that its own efficiency-first framing undersold the technology's real value — that AI's benefit is contingent on the judgment, agency, and guardrails surrounding its use, not on the technology itself. All five converge on the conclusion that AI adoption is fundamentally a change-management and incentive-design problem rather than a technology-deployment one: the constraint on realizing AI's productivity potential is less about model capability and more about redesigning performance metrics, workflows, and management practices that were built for a pre-AI workforce, and about ensuring the people with the least judgment to use AI well are not left to adopt it unguided.

<span class="src">AI Adoption Is Overloading Your Middle Managers</span> (HBR, Julia Shin and Sandra Sucher, June 2026) adds the management-layer view to this concept's incentive and metrics problems. Roughly 88% of organizations use AI in at least one function, but only about a quarter have capabilities that generate value beyond pilots, and McKinsey finds workflow redesign rather than technology sophistication is the main driver of impact. The authors argue the middle layer should be reinforced rather than thinned (Gartner predicts 20% of organizations will use AI to flatten structure in 2026, eliminating over half of middle-management roles): leaders should lower utilization targets during the transition, tie reviews to documenting and sharing AI use, reward coaching and knowledge transfer, provide training in AI oversight such as hallucination detection and fact-checking, and set firm-wide standards so managers do not interpret rules alone. They also flag a leadership-pipeline risk, because AI compresses junior technical tasks before judgment is built, weakening the apprenticeship that develops future leaders. Executives are about twice as likely as individual contributors to describe employees as enthusiastic about AI (BCG). <span class="src">AI Adoption Is Overloading Your Middle Managers</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source specifies how organizations should measure "output" in roles where output is inherently difficult to quantify (the Economist's own example of evaluating whether AI-generated humor or business judgment is "good enough"), leaving a gap between the output-based-metrics prescription and its practical implementation in knowledge-work roles without clean, verifiable outputs.
- The four-country executive survey cited in the Economist article measures a national/macro labor-productivity impact, while HBR's recommendations operate at the individual-employee and team level; neither source addresses how or whether team-level adoption success (of the kind HBR's five principles aim to produce) is expected to show up in that macro-level productivity data, or on what timeline.
- The middle-manager findings come from 18 interviews at two consulting firms, and the authors suggest but do not demonstrate that the pattern generalises to other knowledge industries.
