## Summary

AI agents are replacing traditional customer service software and call centers, and they can be more consistent than human agents even though the underlying models are imperfect and do not always give the same answer twice. Sierra's co-founder Bret Taylor argues that by 2026 every company will need an AI agent, in the way every company needed a website in 1995. Sierra charges only when an agent actually resolves a customer's problem, which replaces software licensing with outcome-based pricing. Risk is managed with layers of defense borrowed from cybersecurity, including supervisor models that watch the main agent's decisions in real time for errors and rule violations.

## Highlights

- The core thesis, from Sierra co-founder and OpenAI chairman Bret Taylor, is that by 2026 "every company needs an AI agent" the way every company needed a website in 1995, and that the majority of digital customer interactions will move to agents that do everything a website can do. <span class="src">Boss Class S3 Bonus 1 Bret Taylor of Sierra and OpenAI</span>
- Sierra prices its agents on outcomes, not software usage: clients are only charged when the AI agent actually resolves a customer's problem; escalation to a human is free — a deliberate break from traditional software licensing. <span class="src">Boss Class S3 Bonus 1 Bret Taylor of Sierra and OpenAI</span>
- AI agents are argued to already be more *consistent* than humans in regulated, high-stakes contexts (e.g. financial advice) even though the underlying models are imperfect and non-deterministic, because human error rates in the same interactions are also high but less scrutinized. <span class="src">Boss Class S3 Bonus 1 Bret Taylor of Sierra and OpenAI</span>
- Reported customer satisfaction scores for Sierra-built AI agents are "almost uniformly higher" than the human interactions they replaced, and Taylor reports one enterprise client re-onshoring customer service (reversing an earlier offshoring decision) once AI made higher-quality interactions affordable again. <span class="src">Boss Class S3 Bonus 1 Bret Taylor of Sierra and OpenAI</span>
- Risk is managed through "defense in depth," borrowed from cybersecurity practice: real-time "supervisor models" monitor the primary agent's decisions for hallucination or guardrail violations as they happen, followed by a second, more thorough model that reviews completed conversations afterward and surfaces the most problematic ones for human review, rather than a random sample. <span class="src">Boss Class S3 Bonus 1 Bret Taylor of Sierra and OpenAI</span>
- Taylor's stated first-principles decision framework for whether to build an AI agent now versus wait for an off-the-shelf vendor solution weighs competitive risk (a savvy competitor adopting AI first may gain a compounding structural advantage) against the cost and complexity of building and later discarding custom infrastructure once a mature vendor solution exists. <span class="src">Boss Class S3 Bonus 1 Bret Taylor of Sierra and OpenAI</span>
- Taylor expects most companies will not want to become software companies in the AI era any more than they do today, and predicts an ecosystem of vendors selling purpose-built agents for specific business functions (financial audit, customer service, sales lead generation) rather than companies generating custom software from foundation models themselves. <span class="src">Boss Class S3 Bonus 1 Bret Taylor of Sierra and OpenAI</span>
- On jobs, Taylor rejects the premise that AI leads to mass unemployment, but distinguishes that from the challenge of reskilling pace: he frames current AI adoption as unusually fast compared to past technology transitions (e.g. decades-long electrification), which he sees as the harder problem for individual careers, not a lack of new jobs being created in principle. <span class="src">Boss Class S3 Bonus 1 Bret Taylor of Sierra and OpenAI</span>

## Concept

The argument for AI customer service agents rests on an analogy to an earlier technology transition: just as companies in the mid-1990s had to build websites from scratch, at enormous consulting cost, before a mature vendor ecosystem existed, companies today are largely building AI agent capability themselves — stringing together foundation models and agent-building toolkits — because purpose-built, off-the-shelf agents don't yet exist for most business functions. The prediction is that within roughly five years this matures into a vendor ecosystem selling agents as complete solutions to specific problems (financial audit, legal review, customer service, lead generation) the way software vendors emerged after the early internet, rather than companies building agent infrastructure themselves indefinitely. In the interim, the recommended approach for most companies is deliberate experimentation rather than waiting, because AI's inherently deflationary economics mean that whichever competitor adopts effective automation first can reinvest the resulting cost savings into further competitive advantage while a slower competitor sits on the sidelines. <span class="src">Boss Class S3 Bonus 1 Bret Taylor of Sierra and OpenAI</span>

A recurring theme is that AI agents do not need to be perfect to outperform the human processes they replace — they need only to be more consistent, and to have adequate monitoring in place for when they fail. Language models are explicitly acknowledged as imperfect and non-deterministic (the same prompt can yield different answers), which makes strict claims like "the model never does X" essentially impossible to make. But human performance in the same roles is also imperfect and is managed today through procedural controls — call recording and transcription review in financial services, for instance — rather than an expectation of human perfection; the argument is that AI agents should be held to, and can meet, that same practical bar rather than a standard of flawlessness. Risk is managed through narrowing the scope of what an agent is allowed to do (turning what would otherwise be an open-ended "science problem," akin to pursuing AGI, into a bounded "engineering problem" with concrete guardrails — e.g. an item return governed by a specific 30-day policy) and through layered monitoring modeled on cybersecurity's "defense in depth": a real-time "supervisor model" checks the primary agent's decisions as the conversation happens, flagging hallucinations or guardrail violations, while a second, more thorough model reviews completed conversations afterward and surfaces the most problematic ones — rather than a random sample — for human review. This lets human reviewers spend their attention on the cases most likely to need it, instead of being spread evenly across all interactions. <span class="src">Boss Class S3 Bonus 1 Bret Taylor of Sierra and OpenAI</span>

Reported outcomes support the consistency argument in practice: customer satisfaction scores for Sierra-built agents are described as almost uniformly higher than the human interactions they replaced, credited partly to AI agents' patience, availability in multiple languages at effectively no marginal cost, and immunity to the fatigue or quota pressure that can make human agents impatient. One concrete second-order effect reported by a client is re-onshoring customer service that had previously been offshored, because AI-driven cost savings made higher-quality (and now domestically staffed) service affordable again — illustrating that the primary business case is not simply labor cost reduction. Sierra's own value proposition is framed less as cost savings and more as improving customer retention and sales: because customer churn is costly relative to acquisition, and because lowering the cost of each customer interaction effectively multiplies the available budget for customer engagement, the highest-value effect of cheaper AI-driven interactions may be reinvestment into reducing churn rather than simply cutting headcount. This is reflected directly in Sierra's pricing model, which charges clients only when the AI agent actually resolves the customer's problem (escalation to a human is free) — explicitly framed as paying for a job well done rather than paying for the right to use software, on the premise that software itself is becoming "ancient history" as a category once AI-driven outcomes can be measured directly. <span class="src">Boss Class S3 Bonus 1 Bret Taylor of Sierra and OpenAI</span>

On the boundary between AI autonomy and human involvement, the guiding principle offered is that this should be a deliberate business decision made per use case rather than a fixed default: an AI agent can serve as a copilot to a human professional (e.g. collecting mortgage application documents so a banker can focus on advice) or can operate autonomously end-to-end, and the customer should retain the ability to reach a human when they want one. Regulated industries have adopted these agents faster than expected by starting with lower-risk tasks (e.g. scheduling a medical appointment) before moving toward higher-risk ones (e.g. medical triage), a pattern expected to accelerate as the technology and its surrounding regulatory framework mature together. <span class="src">Boss Class S3 Bonus 1 Bret Taylor of Sierra and OpenAI</span>

On the future of the underlying software industry, the source draws a distinction between the falling marginal cost of *generating* code (which foundation models are increasingly good at) and companies' actual willingness to *own and maintain* the resulting software. The argument is that most companies do not want to become software companies even when they technically could generate their own tools, in the same way that most companies did not build their own accounting software after a new revenue-recognition standard was introduced — instead paying a vendor to amortize the cost of getting the rules right, testing them, and fixing bugs across many similar clients. This underpins the prediction that companies will keep buying purpose-built agents from vendors that specialize in a given business function, rather than generating bespoke software internally via foundation models, even as the cost of code generation itself falls toward zero. On employment, the source explicitly rejects the premise that AI leads to a lasting shortage of human work, drawing on historical analogies (the ATM's effect on bank branch staffing, the shift of the U.S. workforce away from farming since 1776), but distinguishes this from the challenge of reskilling speed: unlike past technology transitions that unfolded over decades, current AI-driven changes to what counts as "best practice" in a field like software engineering are happening within a period of months, which is characterized as the harder, more disruptive part of the transition for individual careers rather than a sign that meaningful work will run out. <span class="src">Boss Class S3 Bonus 1 Bret Taylor of Sierra and OpenAI</span>

## Open Questions

- The source is an interview with a company founder discussing his own product category and pricing model; reported figures (customer satisfaction scores, the onshoring anecdote) are not independently sourced or quantified beyond Taylor's own account.
- The account does not address how outcomes-based pricing is defined or verified in ambiguous cases — e.g. what counts as "the AI agent solving the problem" versus a customer simply giving up before escalating to a human.
