## Summary

A three-year Forrester research program tracks marketing technology's evolution from a tactical collection of point tools into a strategic, CMO-owned ecosystem — covering the six mission-critical capabilities the "next generation" of martech needs, the five business capabilities and four architectural pillars a real stack must deliver, a two-phase process for keeping technology aligned with business goals, and three reports on navigating generative AI's hype, adoption-maturity segmentation, and operationalizing it once the hype settles. A Forrester landscape of customer analytics technologies adds the converging vendor market behind the customer-data layer.

## Concept

Forrester's earliest report in this martech research program, published in 2021, opens by arguing that despite martech's remarkable strides, treating it as a tactical solution rather than a strategic asset badly undersells its actual business impact — few technology categories have such direct influence on revenue, customer engagement, and brand. The report identifies four dimensions of this strategic mission: martech must engage customers to drive growth by orchestrating and delivering relevant experiences across the full customer journey, not just isolated touchpoints; it must genuinely help marketers do their jobs rather than constrain their creativity or understanding of customers, which requires usable interfaces and workflows, reduced silos, and exposed insight into what's actually working and why; it must help build differentiated intellectual property — institutional knowledge and insight genuinely unique to a given organization, such as strong identity resolution enabling second-party data sharing in co-op marketing programs — that competitors can't simply replicate; and it must prepare marketers for continuous future change rather than requiring wholesale replatforming every time customer expectations or technology shift. Yet even in 2021, enterprise martech was already showing serious strain: 91% of surveyed enterprise martech users cited integration as the single most important feature of a marketing tool, but only 19% of surveyed Enterprise Marketing Suite customer references believed any single vendor could actually deliver every capability they needed — leaving marketers caught uncomfortably between "suite" and "best-of-breed" purchasing strategies, each with real drawbacks. The report's central prescription is to rethink martech priorities around genuine value creation rather than tactical feature accumulation: meeting modern consumer expectations requires eliminating any "digital divide" in the stack, since disconnected components actively harm rather than merely fail to help; reducing emphasis on commodity, tactical functionality (sending emails, buying display ads, building dashboards) in favor of capabilities that actually move core marketing KPIs, since knowing customers better than any competitor and reaching them with genuinely relevant interactions is what moves the needle, not incremental feature parity; and deliberately taking ownership of strategically important systems (customer analytics as a "system of insight," cross-channel campaign management as a "system of engagement") rather than delegating them, since these systems generate the proprietary institutional knowledge a customer-obsessed organization needs to control directly. To operationalize this strategic mission, the report proposes six mission-critical, deliberately interdependent capabilities for next-generation martech: a holistic customer data management strategy establishing accessible, consistent data as the stack's foundation; identity resolution to assemble a complete, unified picture of who a customer is across every touchpoint and interaction; customer experience optimization that evaluates and improves entire customer journeys rather than optimizing campaign-by-campaign or channel-by-channel; contextually relevant content infrastructure that can efficiently create, manage, locate, and repurpose creative elements for specific customer moments across any touchpoint or device; customer journey analytics that broaden measurement beyond simple interaction-level metrics (clicks) toward journey-level business KPIs like customer satisfaction and lifetime value; and unified orchestration that architects the entire customer journey within a single coordinated workflow rather than fragmenting it across channel-specific or lifecycle-stage-specific decisions. The report closes with concrete near-term steps marketers can take without waiting for vendors to deliver this future state: comprehensively inventorying the current martech stack (Forrester found 74% of surveyed users already had a marketing technology strategy their whole department follows, suggesting this documentation habit was becoming baseline practice); deliberately balancing buying criteria toward integration capability over standalone functionality (a regional retail bank, for instance, replatformed three martech tools simultaneously specifically to validate integration across all three candidates at once, rather than evaluating each in isolation); documenting a full identity graph mapping which consumer identifiers get used for which interactions and touchpoints; and benchmarking both campaign and broader business KPIs to establish the continuous-monitoring discipline customer journey analytics requires. <span class="src">The Moments-Based Evolution Of Enterprise Marketing Technology</span>

A follow-up 2022 report in the same research program, "A CMO's Guide to Marketing Technology," translates this strategic case directly into a mandate for the CMO personally. Its central argument is that there is no marketing without martech — marketing technology is what actually turns strategy into insights-driven, live customer engagement — and that despite growing organizational sophistication and rebounding post-pandemic martech investment, most companies still plan and deliver martech in a decentralized, ad hoc way, treating tools as one-off purchases rather than components of a deliberately architected system. Forrester's core prescription is that CMOs, having the single biggest organizational stake in martech's success, must take direct, hands-on ownership of its vision and ongoing management rather than delegating the function to IT or treating it as a background concern — one large mobile operator's CMO frames the required commitment concretely as roughly six months of four-to-eight-hour weekly self-education. A fully realized stack, per this report, must deliver five business capabilities beyond commoditized data management, insight generation, and campaign execution: engagement velocity (processing data and delivering interactions at the speed and volume real-time customer experience now demands); personalization (tailored content, sufficient cross-channel reach, and algorithms determining ideal channel, timing, and offer); cohesive customer journeys (breaking down channel silos through unified customer profiles visible to both insight-generating and engagement-executing systems); agility (architecting the stack as a swappable, upgradable platform via public cloud, low-code, and headless technology); and protection (native or integrated support for data security, preference management, consent management, and privacy management, given martech's direct proximity to consumer data). Structurally, the report organizes these capabilities across four architectural pillars: the data fabric (collecting, managing, and assembling data from internal and external sources into usable, unified customer profiles); customer understanding (the analysis layer — reporting, analytics, targeting, and testing); brand strategy (the planning, resource-management, content-management, and performance-management functionality connecting strategy to execution); and brand experience (the engagement layer that actually orchestrates and delivers customer-lifecycle interactions across marketing, advertising, and loyalty touchpoints). Years of tactical, siloed, reactive decision-making have left most companies' stacks fragmented — incomplete data access, inefficient workflows, underutilized functionality — meaning building a genuinely complete, cohesive stack from that starting point is a colossal undertaking few companies fully pull off, but a genuine source of competitive advantage for those who do. Forrester identifies three differentiators (plus a fourth, staffing) that separate the companies who succeed: enterprise martech strategy (a unified plan that also allocates resources for future innovation, not just today's requirements); integration (both the single most important vendor-selection criterion and martech's single biggest technical shortcoming, spanning unified customer profiles, cross-application workflows, and cross-touchpoint orchestration); data management and identity resolution (a documented inventory of current and planned data requirements backed by real collection, hygiene, and identity-resolution processes — one digital-bank CMO frames the underlying motivation as wanting "a real-time view of the data" without ceding control of it to an agency); and staffing as a distinct requirement in its own right, specifically hiring a dedicated marketing technologist to own martech strategy, define its roadmap, and manage vendor relationships — one clothing-brand CMO described hiring a director of martech as "breaking all standards" at a company used to centralized IT, concluding "I can't have a part-time person in this role." <span class="src">A CMO’s Guide to Marketing Technology</span>

Where the two earlier reports establish what martech should deliver and who should own it, "The Forrester B2C Marketing Technology Alignment Framework" (2023) supplies the operational process for keeping that ownership continuously effective, since technology alignment is explicitly framed as an evolving, iterative process — much like a go-to-market strategy itself — rather than a one-and-done project. The framework is designed to hold up under four recurring sources of misalignment: siloed decision-making, in which technology choices optimized for one department's needs create duplication and inefficiency for others who are also affected but weren't consulted; mergers and acquisitions, which routinely force together technology infrastructures with different procurement histories and cultures, producing duplication, political conflict over process ownership, and disrupted supplier relationships even in carefully planned deals; emerging technologies, whose rapid, continuous market arrival can quickly disrupt organizational alignment by impacting business processes, data, and existing infrastructure faster than governance can absorb; and new strategic initiatives, where a major go-to-market shift requires existing processes and technologies to change in ways that, without a coordinating framework, can inadvertently stall the very growth engine the initiative was meant to accelerate. The framework itself runs in two phases across eight stages. Phase one, discovery and analysis, begins with Initiate (obtaining cross-organizational agreement on what alignment actually means in terms of shared business objectives, including a cross-functional charter defining roles and a common martech reference model), proceeds through Audit (establishing a baseline of the current technology infrastructure by mapping tools to core capabilities, processes, and stakeholders, then scoring them on functionality and user adoption, repeated periodically as needs evolve), Analyze (comparing that audit baseline against a desired future state, conducting a gap analysis, and identifying whether gaps are best addressed with people, process, technology, or some combination rather than defaulting to a purely technology-led fix), and Propose (developing a formal business case and proposal for each project addressing an identified gap, estimating time, cost, benefit, and risk for each). Phase two, execution and optimization, runs through Prioritize (evaluating proposed projects against established guidelines and business objectives to select those with the greatest cross-organizational impact, then securing authorization and investment), Plan (building detailed roadmap plans with resource allocation, financial projections, milestones, and measurement criteria tied back to the original business case), Execute (implementing the plans, communicating purpose and benefit to affected stakeholders, and tracking measurable deliverables and milestones — highlighting interim wins along the way for phased implementations), and Govern (reviewing the updated infrastructure, establishing responsibility and accountability, capturing lessons learned at each stage, watching for new capability unlocks from strategic vendor partners' own innovation roadmaps, and determining which areas need continued focus using Forrester's broader martech maturity model). The framework's cyclical design is deliberate: Govern doesn't terminate the process but feeds directly back into a renewed Initiate stage as business needs, vendor capabilities, and market conditions continue to shift. <span class="src">The Forrester B2C Marketing Technology Alignment Framework</span>

Two later reports in the same research program turn specifically to generative AI, arriving roughly a year apart to track the shift from hype to operational reality. "Look Beyond The Hype To Evolve Martech With AI" (December 2023) opens by cautioning marketers against genAI overhype specifically because AI is not new to martech at all — predictive AI had already been a standard martech feature for more than a decade by the time genAI captured public attention, and 54% of surveyed US B2C marketing executives already said AI was contributing to marketing execution before genAI's public debut, even though only 15% had rated AI capability enhancement a top strategic priority as recently as mid-2022. The report's central analytical tool is a two-dimensional map for any AI capability, existing or emerging: autonomy (collaborative AI, where human and machine work in lockstep with the marketer retaining final say, versus independent AI, where the system runs inputs, self-optimizes, and acts largely unsupervised — "lights out" automation already exists today in areas like ad bidding and message send-time optimization) crossed with technique (predictive AI, which identifies statistically likely future outcomes from historical data and has been in marketing use for over a decade, versus generative AI, which creates genuinely original content from natural-language or other non-code prompts and remains a much newer marketing capability). Beyond creative content generation — where genAI excitement is currently concentrated — the report highlights more progressive emerging applications: dynamic insights (embedding measurement directly into every customer interaction to auto-optimize campaigns and journeys in real time, including generating synthetic data that mirrors live customer behavior to simulate a journey's projected impact while it's still in flight); collaborative workflows (genAI extending access to expansive analytical, creative, and orchestration capability previously reserved for specialists following rigid linear processes, shifting campaign targeting from a one-way analyst-to-marketer handoff toward genuinely coordinated, continuous collaboration); high-velocity customer engagement (compensating for slower legacy martech by running the full marketing-operations cycle of planning, creative development, analytics, and optimization faster while simultaneously compressing customer engagement cycle times); and anticipatory customer journeys (combining predictive and generative AI to move beyond manual, resource-constrained journey design toward automated customer-journey generation from prompts, integrated across the full range of digital touchpoints). Because rigorous technical planning remains a genuine deficit for many organizations (only half of surveyed AI decision-makers reported having a documented conceptual technical framework or architecture as part of their AI strategy), the report specifies four prerequisites marketers must satisfy before adopting genAI capabilities specifically: sufficient data and training (partnering with IT, enterprise architecture, and data science to gather and prepare domain- and company-specific data that tunes a public LLM toward on-brand, differentiated outputs, since data quality management was already cited by 31% of surveyed B2C marketing decision-makers as one of their biggest execution challenges); continuous monitoring of genAI outputs (testing throughout the AI lifecycle, running independent reviews and audits for bias, hallucination, and unintended consequences, and establishing an enterprisewide AI governance framework, since 64% of surveyed AI decision-makers were already concerned about genAI output misuse leading to organizational errors); measurement to justify ongoing investment (simultaneously tracking incremental marketing-KPI lift and internal efficiency gains, since genAI's promised time-savings should visibly fund continued investment in new resources, training, and skills — 57% of surveyed AI decision-makers had already documented AI-driven outcomes, KPIs, and ROI goals as part of a shareable AI strategy); and comprehensive privacy and security (adopting privacy-by-design principles specifically for the risk of inputting sensitive business and customer data into public LLMs, given data-privacy concerns cited as a top genAI adoption barrier, evolving regulatory action like a US presidential executive order and EU-level restrictions on some open-source genAI, and the added third-party risk of accessing open-source LLMs through third-party martech vendors). <span class="src">Look Beyond The Hype To Evolve Martech With AI</span>

A companion report published two months later, "Advance GenAI Marketing from Pilot Projects to Proficiency" (February 2024), shifts from "Look Beyond The Hype"'s capability-mapping framework to a maturity-segmentation lens, opening from the observation that "the genAI honeymoon is over" — more than a quarter of surveyed AI decision-makers already cited integrating genAI with existing infrastructure as the greatest adoption barrier, well past the initial-fascination phase. The report profiles four genAI marketing maturity segments among US B2C marketing executives: Integrators (26%, embedding genAI teamwide across go-to-market execution, often via centralized agency platforms like dentsu Connect or WPP Open, and increasingly focused on data quality and management as capabilities turn multimodal); Explorers (40%, piloting genAI in specific marketing tasks and advancing by adding performance use cases like media operations or content localization, while proactively addressing employee job-loss fears); Considerers (29%, intrigued but not yet experimenting, needing to identify and prioritize realistic use cases and assess technology/data/skills readiness before advancing); and Rejectors (4%, no intention to use genAI in marketing execution, typically due to heavy regulation or unconvinced leadership, who can still capture partial benefit by outsourcing to external genAI marketing partners). Advancing through these stages matters because genAI is projected to enhance 4.5 times more marketing jobs than it eliminates, reframing adoption as an augmentation and skills challenge rather than a replacement one. The report's GenAI Marketing Adoption Framework operationalizes that progression in three steps: establishing adoption as an explicit objective (identifying anticipated outcomes tied to business goals, securing a CMO-level executive sponsor to legitimize pilots and unlock funding, and building the investment case for AI provider spend); investing in a task force to build knowledge and expertise (identifying passionate internal "hand-raisers" as evangelists, institutionalizing a repeatable test-and-learn methodology with defined measurement checkpoints at 30/60/90-day intervals, and explicitly rewarding adaptability as pilot projects reveal what does and doesn't work); and creating a playbook for responsible execution (institutionalizing continuous genAI training beyond isolated pockets of expertise, building AI partner alliances for early access to new capabilities, and promoting clear, risk-tolerance-calibrated policies on tool selection and approved use cases). Read alongside "Look Beyond The Hype" and "Shift Generative AI In Martech," this report supplies the maturity-progression mechanics — how an organization actually advances from Considerer to Explorer to Integrator — that the earlier capability-mapping framework and the later production-scaling framework both implicitly assume but don't themselves detail. <span class="src">Advance GenAI Marketing from Pilot Projects to Proficiency</span>

A companion report published a month later, "The Future Of Marketing Technology" (March 2024), broadens the lens from genAI specifically back to the whole martech stack's structural evolution, arguing that legacy martech — built primarily to set up and deliver outbound, largely email-centric campaigns — must evolve to support the full range of devices, channels, and touchpoints where consumers now actually engage with a brand. Next-generation requirements the report identifies include customer-first data strategies (responding to ongoing data-deprecation pressure by shifting emphasis toward first- and zero-party data, identity resolution, and privacy-respecting profile-building — "any new tech investment needs to support our approach to data, consent, and privacy," in one marketing technology director's words); the enduring personalization mandate (41% of surveyed B2C marketing decision-makers still cited personalization as among their biggest execution challenges, a puzzle the report is explicit no single technology solves on its own, requiring multiple coordinated technologies working together instead); heightened expectations that martech investment demonstrably contribute to the business (66% of surveyed decision-makers planned to spend more on martech in 2023 even as 26% cited economic conditions as a top challenge, reflecting a shift from "marketers could buy new tech because it's cool" toward investments that "check all the boxes to rationalize" themselves, per one industry executive); and support for "invisible" experiences that proactively anticipate customer needs across the broader CX family of touchpoints (sales, service, digital), requiring high-performance systems built for real-time scalability and speed rather than systems retrofitted after the fact. The report finds legacy martech stacks structurally ill-equipped for these requirements: 91% of surveyed enterprise martech users still rated integration as the single most important tool feature, yet only 19% believed a single vendor's "end-to-end" enterprise marketing suite could cover their actual needs, leaving most organizations awkwardly straddling suite and best-of-breed strategies; automation and access remain inefficient, trapping marketers in a cycle of manually rebuilding the same data objects and moving data across disconnected systems for each new campaign ("we still need analysts to do manual laborious data work," in one product-management VP's words); and few stacks were originally engineered for today's real-time data volume and decisioning velocity (one AARP data leader described making "tens of millions of engagement decisions every day based on billions of pieces of information"). Forrester's proposed resolution is to redefine martech as an ecosystem — formally, "an interconnected system of data, applications, and services that enables consumer marketing strategy, insights, and engagement" — organized around three pillars (customer understanding, brand strategy, and brand experience, echoing the earlier CMO's Guide's four-pillar architecture) but distinguished by its emphasis on integration and modularity over monolithic, all-in-one platforms, letting marketers maximize flexibility (adapting to unresolved questions about data, privacy, and consumer preference without wholesale replatforming), develop capabilities incrementally over time rather than deploying an entire vendor's feature set simultaneously, reduce dependence on any single vendor's negotiating leverage, and tune capabilities precisely to company-specific needs rather than accepting a vendor's lowest-common-denominator functionality across every feature. The report identifies four strategic elements ecosystem investment should prioritize: interoperability (integration, workflow, and data connectivity that let varied modules function as one cohesive unit rather than a mere portfolio of separately-operating tools — "we're moving toward a plug-and-play environment that obfuscates individual solutions behind connectivity," in one martech leader's description); data (the underlying foundation for every insight, strategy, and engagement activity, requiring consolidated customer profiles, real-time data streaming, enrichment connectivity, and identity resolution — "whatever we put on top of data systems can be flexible and ad hoc; it's the data systems we need to be smart about," per one Google martech manager); orchestration (a unified facility for assembling audiences, communications, and triggered interactions across every touchpoint and lifecycle phase in a coordinated way, rather than siloed by channel); and generative AI specifically (already used in marketing by 26% of surveyed respondents, with a further 40% actively exploring use cases, expanding into automated customer insight, streamlined marketing operations, and generated customer journeys — "genAI is a forcing factor; our leadership is more motivated than ever before to consider new technology investments," per one AARP marketing leader). Realizing this ecosystem shift requires several organizational changes: de-emphasizing consolidation as a goal in itself (since no single vendor solution has proven able to deliver both technical efficiency and marketing efficacy simultaneously — vendor consolidation should be an outcome of good planning, not the plan itself); genuinely valuing strong technology partnerships across IT, enterprise architects, and data science rather than treating martech purely as marketing's own domain; building dynamic governance and planning processes suited to an inherently more design-flexible ecosystem than legacy packaged technology allowed; adopting a data strategy that structurally precedes and outlasts any specific technology choice; and deliberately expanding the circle of internal collaborators well beyond marketing and technical staff to include the CFO (for procurement, business-case development, and ongoing performance management) and CX counterparts (for coordinating the technology and strategy elements of increasingly "invisible" customer journeys). <span class="src">The Future Of Marketing Technology</span>

The most recent report in this research program, "Shift Generative AI In Martech From Theory To Reality" (November 2024), returns to genAI specifically to close the gap between the widespread urgency the earlier "Look Beyond The Hype" report documented and actual production deployment — finding only 18% of surveyed B2B marketing decision-makers had advanced genAI from experimentation to production roughly a year later, with most organizations still experimenting on historical data (54%), within live production workflows (53%), or in isolated sandbox environments (46%), typically starting with use cases layered onto existing internal processes rather than fundamentally new capabilities. The most common emerging genAI use cases the report documents are content generation (ideating, generating, and iterating text, image, or video content and variations — one cloud-computing marketer used genAI specifically to generate verticalized, industry-specific copy for account-based marketing campaigns), natural language interfaces (letting nontechnical users query martech systems in plain language rather than formal code, illustrated by ActionIQ's audience-segment creation feature, which returns both a natural-language explanation and the underlying structured query for validation), and application assistants (guided interactions that simplify traditionally complex tasks, like Adobe's cross-Experience-Cloud assistant answering questions such as "how do I create a new campaign brief?"). To advance beyond these relatively simple starting points toward more complex use cases, the report proposes a four-dimension operational framework — people, process, implementation, and measurement — that must be worked through systematically for every genAI use case rather than applied as a one-size-fits-all rollout. On people, genAI adoption is explicitly framed as a cross-functional group project spanning four distinct personas with different goals: marketers (accountable for investment, outcomes, and the appropriateness of customer-facing genAI output, and responsible for their team's organizational readiness); IT (extending its existing tech-and-data-ecosystem management role to cover rapidly evolving LLMs, coordinating infosec, enterprise architecture, engineering, and DevOps across every genAI-touched business function); data scientists (managing data complexity, explainability, and observability, evaluating and governing fast-changing LLMs, and validating model performance to inform future investment); and stewards (establishing governance policies and managing the data/model lifecycle across engineering, data science, security, and legal — particularly legal's authority on evolving data-privacy regulation, content-rights management, and IP risk). On process, the report proposes a five-step iterative cycle — ideate (determining which use cases genuinely support customer objectives and marketing strategy, aligning top-down enterprise goals with bottom-up marketing pain points), forecast (assessing existing tech ecosystem readiness, team capacity, cultural readiness, and both technical and ethical/legal risk before committing), prototype (putting a working model in employees' hands to trial, hone, and provide feedback on — GoDaddy's internal "GoCaaS" content-as-a-service platform lets every team experiment against both predictive and generative models through a shared intelligence/data layer), prioritize (evaluating feasibility, effort, and ROI against competing technology requests — Dell's chief AI officer used a three-criteria framework spanning customer experience, business growth, and business productivity to narrow 800 initial genAI use cases down to a final 36), and activate (deciding whether to continue, pivot, pause, or stop a prototype based on prioritization results and stakeholder input, then communicating that decision and, if continuing, setting realistic scaling timelines — one Adobe APAC customer began with an image-repurposing use case that saved $300 per image, then used that concrete proof point to win buy-in from IT, legal, and security for broader rollout). On implementation, the report lays out three access paths for any given use case, each with distinct tradeoffs: genAI tools embedded in third-party technology (easiest access and fastest deployment, but less customization, potential add-on costs, and vendor-introduced third-party data-privacy risk); public LLMs, open or closed (open models offer flexibility, control, and data privacy but require significant technical expertise and budget to host and maintain; closed, proprietary models integrate and scale more easily but carry higher access costs and less customization); and building a proprietary LLM entirely from scratch or from a foundational model (the highest control and customization, but requiring substantial investment in engineering talent, computational resources, training data, and ongoing maintenance to keep the model current as market conditions and marketing needs evolve). Finally, on measurement — a dimension the report notes few companies or vendors were proactively discussing amid the broader experimentation rush — genAI investment should be assessed on two simultaneous dimensions with clear KPIs for each: efficiency (productivity improvements and usage/journey/event analytics, illustrated by Persado's reduction in customer content-revision requests after replacing manual brand-guideline verification with a brand-trained LLM review-and-approval process) and effectiveness (incremental lift to existing marketing measurement frameworks spanning both CX metrics like customer satisfaction and business metrics like opens, clicks, conversions, attribution, revenue, acquisition cost, and lifetime value, benchmarked via traditional A/B testing against non-AI-assisted output — "generating a report or writing a campaign brief... is great for productivity, but CEOs should really care about how genAI impacts customer experience and revenue lift," in the words of one chief product officer). <span class="src">Shift Generative AI In Martech From Theory To Reality</span>

- [data leadership roles evolution](#/concept/data-leadership-roles-evolution) — the martech technologist and identity-resolution/data-fabric ownership questions raised throughout this concept connect directly to that concept's account of who within an organization should actually own data capability

## Open Questions

- None of the seven reports directly addresses how a company should sequence the earlier strategic/architectural work (six capabilities, five capabilities/four pillars, alignment process) against the later genAI-specific work — whether an organization needs a mature, aligned base stack before layering genAI capability onto it, or whether genAI adoption can proceed in parallel with unresolved foundational integration gaps.
- The reports' survey statistics on genAI adoption shift meaningfully across the roughly one-year gap between "Look Beyond The Hype" (late 2023) and "Shift Generative AI In Martech From Theory To Reality" (late 2024) — from 26% already using genAI with 40% exploring, to just 18% having reached production — but the sources don't reconcile whether this reflects genuinely slower-than-expected progress, a shift in what "production" means as expectations matured, or a change in the surveyed population (B2C in the earlier report, B2B cited specifically in the later one).
- The maturity-segmentation report doesn't specify what share of Integrators or Explorers eventually became one of the 18% who reached production per the November 2024 report, leaving the two reports' population overlap and progression rate unquantified.
