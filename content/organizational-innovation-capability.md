## Summary

Lasting innovation capability comes from treating innovation as a permanent organizational system and not as an occasional program or a single clever bet. Research finds that outsiders, who are unburdened by a field's norms, are well placed to connect distant ideas, but they gain traction only if they learn insiders' language and win at least one ally inside. Creative success tends to belong to people who straddle boundaries, and organizations such as Pixar and the National Science Foundation deliberately create that position. A study of 300 firms found that a novel business model predicts good performance only when paired with operational discipline, efficiency and fit with strategy, as Spotify showed and Clubhouse did not. Successful companies also have a dedicated strategic-innovation function, a playbook for scaling transformational projects and engagement with outside ecosystems, and they avoid traps involving identity, architecture and collaboration.

## Highlights

- Outsiders — individuals unburdened by a field's ingrained norms and expectations — are structurally positioned to connect disparate ideas and challenge the status quo, a pattern sociologists call "focused naivete," but the same distance that fuels their originality also makes it harder to win the backing and credibility needed to bring their ideas to fruition.
- A study of roughly 12,000 Hollywood artists' collaboration networks found the highest creative success (measured by major awards) belonged neither to those at the network's center nor its extreme fringes, but to people straddling both — grounded enough in the familiar to have legitimacy, exploratory enough at the edges to bring novelty; the same pattern held for film crews, where teams blending core and peripheral talent consistently outperformed teams that emphasized one or the other.
- Organizations can deliberately manufacture this "boundary-straddling" position: Pixar encourages animators to participate in outside professional associations and academic conferences; the National Science Foundation staffs roughly a third of its workforce with "rotators" — academic scientists on two-to-four-year loan — which research finds increases both the selection of novel projects and permanent staff's own ability to recognize novelty when they see it.
- A second ally, not universal buy-in, is usually what turns a marginal idea mainstream — insiders tend to instinctively support other insiders' ideas, but even one dissenting or supporting voice can shift a group's assessment, illustrated by venture capitalist Mike Markkula's early "yes" backing Steve Jobs when other investors passed.
- Outsiders can gain more traction by mastering insiders' own language rather than staying "authentically" plain-spoken — a controlled experiment found the identical pitch, delivered with insider jargon, rated 20% higher by expert panels than the same pitch delivered in plain language, and organizations lacking that fluency can instead deploy dedicated "outside-in translators" (Natura's cultural translators bridging cosmetic science and Indigenous ecological knowledge, IBM's "technology advocates," Philips's "cross-pollinators").
- A controlled experiment with life-sciences professionals found identical ideas rated higher when attributed to an insider under a "collective mindset" prime, but the ranking reversed when the same participants were primed toward independent thinking instead — a small nudge toward valuing individual judgment measurably reduces bias against outsiders' ideas, a mechanism Sequoia Capital operationalizes by having partners evaluate deals independently before convening to deliberate collectively.
- A 300-firm study found business-model novelty alone does not predict performance — top performers pair novelty with operational discipline, efficiency, strategy-fit, sustainability integration, correct sizing and timing, and durable value-capture mechanisms, a set of "seven essentials" rather than a single lever.
- Spotify paired novel subscription-bundling with strong operational discipline while Clubhouse's novel format lacked it and stalled, illustrating that novelty needs discipline to convert into performance.
- Shein and Tesla built efficiency itself into a strategic advantage rather than treating it as a background cost concern.
- Duolingo and Netflix show AI-driven business models becoming a distinct category of business-model innovation, where the system (not just the product) is redesigned around AI.
- Most companies default to incremental innovation or poorly-integrated M&A and venture investment rather than building genuine strategic-innovation capability, because neither approach secures the company's long-term future.
- IBM's Emerging Business Opportunity program generated more than $15 billion in revenue and launched five billion-dollar businesses in five years, then was shut down in 2009 because it was structured as a temporary "program" rather than a permanent function — a cautionary illustration of why permanency matters more than initial funding.
- A genuine strategic-innovation capability requires three distinct organizational competencies staffed by dedicated roles — discovery (generating and articulating opportunities), incubation (developing business concepts with evidence), and acceleration (scaling a concept into a business unit that can stand on its own).
- Scaling an already-validated transformational innovation inside a mature core business is a distinct challenge from developing it, requiring leaders to solve four problems in sequence — providing sufficient leadership attention, building the right team, mobilizing resources, and making big-bet decisions under uncertainty.
- P&G's CEO deliberately delegated most operating decisions in his own area of expertise to free up leadership bandwidth for the Oral-B iO transformational project, calling it "probably in some ways the most substantial choice I made."
- Transformational-innovation teams benefit from two complementary appointments beyond the product owner — an integration leader who surfaces organizational risk and coordinates stakeholders, and a commercial leader embedded from the start so the product isn't developed in a commercial vacuum, as Google Glass's launch illustrates when this step is skipped.
- Financial models built for conventional products are systematically biased against high-risk transformational bets, so leaders must supplement lagging indicators (sales, ROI) with forward-looking ones (shifting demographics, consumer attitudes) and validate demand through direct prototyping with consumers before committing capital.
- "Innovation ecosystems" — place-based hubs like Silicon Valley, Boston, London, or Tel Aviv, studied across more than 60 such locations on six continents — are structurally distinct from a company's own internal capability: they combine a research-intensive university, a high concentration of early-stage (often precommercial) activity, and dense concentrations of specialized shared resources (prototyping expertise, testing equipment, risk-tolerant investors) that no single organization could replicate on its own, making external engagement with them a complement to internal capability-building rather than a substitute for it.
- Innovation ecosystems are explicitly distinct from "corporate ecosystems" (supply chains, platform partnerships, and other commercially-anchored value-chain relationships): corporate ecosystems mainly benefit the incumbent anchor firm and support later-stage growth, while innovation ecosystems are interdependent communities of researchers, founders, and specialized service providers oriented toward the earliest, most precommercial stages of the innovation journey — meaning a company seeking genuine discovery-stage capability needs a different kind of external engagement strategy than a company optimizing its supply chain.
- A study spanning 601 interviews, 619 focus-group participants, and 231 reinvention workshops across nine sectors identifies three sequential "reinvention traps" that derail organizations even when leaders aren't naive about the threat: the identity trap (when "who we are" collapses into "what we do," making breakthrough innovation feel like a threat rather than an opportunity — Borders stayed tied to its brick-and-mortar bookstore identity and outsourced e-commerce to Amazon rather than build its own, while Moleskine's founders defined themselves as "creating tools for the creative mind" rather than as a notebook company, so employees embraced its later pivot into digital note-taking); the architecture trap (applying one organizational structure and incentive system to both early-stage exploration and mature-product exploitation, when each actually needs different team structures, autonomy levels, and rewards); and the collaboration trap (mature-product and breakthrough-innovation teams developing rivalry and distrust instead of mutual respect, especially when leaders visibly favor one group's resources or attention over the other's).

## Concept

Business-model innovation is a necessary but insufficient condition for company performance. <span class="src">Business Model Innovation: Seven Essentials</span> reports on a study of roughly 300 firms spanning the dot-com era and the 2010s digital economy, concluding that novelty in a business model does not by itself predict success. The study identifies seven essentials that separate top performers from firms that generate buzz without durable advantage: pairing novelty with operational discipline (Spotify's disciplined subscription bundling versus Clubhouse's undisciplined scaling of a novel format); treating efficiency itself as a strategic advantage rather than a cost afterthought (Shein, Tesla); ensuring strategic fit between the business model and the company's broader positioning (Warby Parker, Southwest); recognizing AI-driven business-model redesign as a distinct and increasingly central category (Duolingo, Netflix); integrating sustainability into the core model rather than bolting it on (Patagonia, Ørsted); matching the scale and timing of a bet to market readiness (OpenAI and Microsoft's rapid moves compared with Canva and Adobe's more measured ones); and recognizing that novelty is necessary but never sufficient for outlier success, since even well-known novel models (Airbnb, Square, Stripe, Nvidia) required a full supporting system — pricing, partnerships, lock-in — to convert novelty into lasting value. The article's overall framing is "think system, not sizzle": a business model succeeds as a coherent whole, not as one striking feature.

Where the seven-essentials framework addresses what makes any single business model succeed, <span class="src">The Eight Core Principles of Strategic Innovation</span> addresses how a company builds the organizational capability to generate and commercialize such models repeatedly. Most companies default to incremental innovation — improving existing product lines — or access outside-the-core innovation through acquisition and venture investment that is poorly integrated into the mainstream business; neither practice secures the company's future. The article defines eight practices, organized into three groups. The first three practices set direction and establish commitment: adopting a common language for the innovation landscape (for example, the McKinsey three-horizons framework), setting explicit domains of innovation intent that senior leadership commits to (illustrated by Grundfos identifying water-scarce cities as a domain), and treating strategic innovation as a permanent function governed by an innovation council rather than a temporary initiative — the IBM Emerging Business Opportunity program is cited as an example of what is gained by making a function permanent, and what is lost when it is not. The next three practices build the underlying capabilities: developing each domain into a portfolio of opportunities managed as experiments rather than a pipeline subjected to go/kill decisions; building the three organizational competencies of discovery, incubation, and acceleration as distinct staffed roles (with Sealed Air's six-month discovery sprints and Corning's two-person exploratory teams as examples); and clearly defining innovation career paths so that talent is not penalized for a venture's failure. The final two practices manage the innovation system's dynamics over time: proactively managing four types of uncertainty — technological, market, resourcing, and organizational, the last being identified as the most common cause of failure in mature companies — and tuning the function's scale to available resources during downturns rather than shutting it down entirely, since stop-start cycles destroy accumulated expertise.

<span class="src">Scaling Up Transformational Innovations</span> narrows the focus further, to the specific operational challenge of scaling one already-promising transformational innovation once it exists — the acceleration stage in the eight-principles framework, examined in depth through two Procter & Gamble case studies: Oral-B iO, a "smart" electric toothbrush, and Always Infinity, a menstrual pad that resolved a long-standing comfort-versus-protection trade-off. The authors present a four-challenge playbook. Providing sufficient leadership requires the CEO (or business unit president) to actively delegate operating decisions in their own areas of expertise, articulate a clear purpose for the innovation, and establish a regular cadence of stakeholder meetings to surface disagreements early. Building the right team requires two appointments beyond the product owner: an integration leader who is the CEO's eyes on the project, focused on organizational risk and stakeholder coordination, and a dedicated commercial leader embedded from early in development so the product is not designed in a commercial vacuum — the article cites Google Glass as a case where skipping this step led to a well-resourced technical success becoming a commercial failure. Unlocking resources means both tapping centralized corporate R&D and funding (which requires the business-unit CEO and senior R&D executive to personally initiate the relationship, since cultural and incentive differences otherwise block access) and redistributing incremental workloads elsewhere in the organization to free capacity for the transformational project. Making big-bet decisions requires combining conventional lagging financial indicators with forward-looking ones such as shifting consumer attitudes, since all standard financial-analysis methods are systematically biased against high-risk innovation; P&G validated Always Infinity's premium positioning by testing hand-assembled prototypes directly with consumers before committing to new manufacturing facilities.

<span class="src">What is an innovation ecosystem</span> (MIT SMR, April 2025, excerpted from Phil Budden and Fiona Murray's book "Accelerating Innovation: Competitive Advantage Through Ecosystem Engagement") supplies the external counterpart to the internal capability-building the other three sources describe. Drawing on a decade studying more than 60 innovation ecosystems across six continents, the authors define these place-based hubs — Silicon Valley and Boston most famously, but also London, Munich, Rio de Janeiro, Cairo, Singapore, and Tel Aviv — as sharing three attributes: an urban core (often not defined by traditional political boundaries), a research-intensive university oriented toward translating research into real-world impact, and a high concentration of early-stage, often precommercial innovative activity. What makes these places function as genuine ecosystems rather than mere geographic coincidences of talent is interdependency: researchers, founders, and specialized resource providers (prototyping expertise, testing equipment, production-method experience, risk-literate early-stage investors, government policy that supports rather than stifles new-to-the-world activity) rely on each other closely enough, and benefit enough from physical proximity to shared infrastructure like wet labs and maker spaces, that even communications-technology-enabled remote work hasn't eliminated the advantage of being physically present during a venture's earliest stages. The article draws a sharp distinction the other sources in this concept don't need to make, since they focus entirely on internal capability: innovation ecosystems differ fundamentally from "corporate ecosystems" (supply chains, platform partnerships, and other commercially structured value-chain relationships), which are typically dispersed globally, anchored around and mainly benefiting an established incumbent firm, and oriented toward later growth-stage support (cost reduction, efficiency, market reach) rather than the earliest, most precommercial stages of the innovation journey that place-based innovation ecosystems concentrate. For a company building the kind of "discovery" competency the eight-core-principles framework identifies as one of three essential organizational roles, this reframes discovery not as a purely internal capability to be staffed and funded, but as something that can be substantially accelerated by deliberate, sustained engagement with an external ecosystem a company doesn't control and shouldn't try to fully internalize. <span class="src">What is an innovation ecosystem</span>

<span class="src">The Three Traps That Stymie Reinvention</span> (MIT SMR, August 2024) supplies the failure-mode counterpart to the positive capability-building the other four sources describe: not what to build, but what specifically derails organizations even when leadership isn't naive about the threat they face. Drawing on research spanning 601 interviews, 619 focus-group participants, and 231 reinvention workshops across manufacturing, chemicals, technology, retail, luxury goods, media, food and beverage, nonprofits, and government, the author finds that companies rarely fail to reinvent themselves for lack of awareness — Kodak's own executives held the first digital-photography patents, and Swiss watchmakers invented the quartz movement that nearly destroyed their industry — but instead fall into one or more of three sequential traps. The identity trap strikes first, when "who we are" collapses into "what we do": Borders remained so tied to its identity as a brick-and-mortar bookseller that it outsourced e-commerce to Amazon rather than build its own capability, and by the time it reversed course seven years later, it was too late to recover, while independent bookstores that redefined themselves more broadly as community gathering spaces (rather than book retailers specifically) grew 72% in number from 2009 to 2024 despite facing the same Amazon threat; Moleskine avoided the trap entirely because its founders had always defined the company as "creating tools for the creative mind" rather than as a notebook maker, so employees experienced its pivot into Evernote- and Adobe-partnered digital note-taking as a natural extension rather than an identity threat. The architecture trap follows, when leaders apply one organizational structure and incentive system to both early-stage exploratory innovation and mature-product exploitation, when each actually requires different team autonomy, work processes, and rewards; Corning's countermeasure is a dedicated Emerging Innovations Group with autonomy and time to incubate ideas before handing commercially-validated ones to business-unit managers — the same organizational muscle that let Corning divert 300 people and critical resources from its profitable LCD business within days of Steve Jobs's 2007 request for a durable iPhone touchscreen, ultimately producing Gorilla Glass (now in 8 billion-plus devices across 45-plus brands) and, years later, the Valor Glass vaccine-vial technology that became essential to COVID-19 vaccine distribution after a decade and $200 million of sustained investment. Corning also treats incentive design explicitly, with an annual "Day of the Dead" ritual celebrating projects deliberately killed before they became too costly to unwind, rather than penalizing the teams that killed them. The collaboration trap emerges last, once separate architectures for exploration and exploitation exist: teams at different S-curve stages can develop rivalry and distrust, especially if leaders visibly favor one group's resources or visibility over the other's; Swatch Group overcame this at the industry level when CEO Nicolas Hayek (an advocate of mass-market quartz technology) and Jean-Claude Biver (an advocate of traditional mechanical craftsmanship) deliberately built a shared daily working relationship despite opposing visions, jointly reviving the Omega brand and ultimately making Swatch Group the world's largest watch company and largest supplier to the rest of the Swiss watch industry. The article's practical remedies include communicating a deliberately broader organizational identity well before a new product threatens to displace an old one, building genuinely distinct architectures (and matching incentives) for exploration versus exploitation rather than forcing one structure to serve both, and creating shared spaces — cross-functional workshops, temporary team-swap "stretch assignments" — that let people on both sides of the exploration/exploitation divide build the mutual trust that averts the collaboration trap before it takes hold. <span class="src">The Three Traps That Stymie Reinvention</span>

Read together, the five sources describe complementary layers of the same underlying problem: designing a single business model well (seven essentials), building the durable internal organizational machinery to generate a pipeline of such models (eight core principles), executing the specific operational challenges of taking one validated model to scale inside an existing mature business (the four-challenge playbook), deliberately engaging with the external, place-based innovation ecosystems where much of the earliest, highest-uncertainty discovery work that feeds all three internal processes actually originates, and — the layer the other four largely assume away — recognizing and preempting the identity, architecture, and collaboration traps that derail reinvention even in organizations that have every other piece of capability described above already in place.

<span class="src">Lessons Learned From Outside Innovators</span> (MIT SMR, Simone Ferriani and Gino Cattani, March 2025) supplies the individual-level counterpart to <span class="src">What is an innovation ecosystem</span>'s place-based external-engagement account, focusing on how a single outsider — a person, not a location — becomes a source of breakthrough innovation, and how organizations can be structured to actually benefit from that person's presence. The authors' central mechanism is "focused naivete": outsiders, unburdened by a field's ingrained norms and assumptions, are structurally positioned to connect disparate ideas and challenge the status quo in ways insiders rarely can, but that same distance makes it harder for them to win the credibility and backing needed to act on their ideas — a tension the authors trace through Katalin Karikó's decades-long, largely unsupported mRNA research before it became the technical foundation of rapid COVID-19 vaccine development and earned her a Nobel Prize. A decade-plus of the authors' own research, spanning a roughly 12,000-person map of Hollywood's collaboration network, finds the highest creative success (measured by major awards) belongs neither to those at a network's dense center nor its extreme periphery, but to people and teams straddling both — grounded enough in the familiar to carry legitimacy, exploratory enough at the edges to bring genuine novelty. Organizations can deliberately manufacture this boundary-straddling position rather than leaving it to chance: Pixar sends its own animators out to professional associations, academic conferences, and outside art projects, while the National Science Foundation staffs roughly a third of its own workforce with "rotators" — academic scientists on two-to-four-year loan — a practice research finds measurably increases both the selection of novel projects and permanent staff's own ability to recognize novelty when it appears. Even inside a receptive organization, an outsider's idea typically needs a specific catalyst to gain traction: a single ally, since insiders tend to instinctively support other insiders' ideas by default, but even one dissenting or supporting voice can shift the majority's assessment (illustrated by venture capitalist Mike Markkula's early "yes" to Steve Jobs when other investors passed); fluency in insiders' own language, since a controlled experiment found an identical pitch delivered with field-appropriate jargon rated 20% higher by expert panels than the same pitch delivered plainly, a gap organizations can bridge institutionally with dedicated "outside-in translator" roles (Natura's cultural translators connecting cosmetic science with Indigenous ecological knowledge, IBM's "technology advocates," Philips's "cross-pollinators"); a deliberate shift toward independent (rather than collective) evaluation, since a controlled experiment with life-sciences professionals found identical ideas rated higher when attributed to an insider under a collective-mindset prime, but the ranking reversed when participants were primed to think independently instead — a pattern Sequoia Capital operationalizes by having partners evaluate deals independently before convening to deliberate as a group; and, most powerfully, an organizational "inflection point" — a moment of genuine pressure or crisis that makes even the most closed systems newly receptive to ideas they had previously dismissed, as COVID-19 did for Karikó's mRNA research and for BioNTech's subsequent partnership with Pfizer. Read alongside this concept's place-based innovation-ecosystem source, outside innovators and innovation ecosystems represent two distinct but complementary channels for the same underlying need: a genuine discovery capability requires deliberately importing perspectives an organization cannot generate purely from within, whether through sustained engagement with an external geographic hub or through individual outsiders embedded, translated, and allied with inside the organization itself. <span class="src">Lessons Learned From Outside Innovators</span>

## Open Questions

- None of the three original sources address how strategic-innovation capability-building interacts with AI-native product development specifically, beyond citing Duolingo and Netflix as examples of AI-driven business models; the mechanics of that intersection are not detailed.
- The eight-principles article does not specify typical team sizes or budget ranges for the discovery, incubation, and acceleration competencies, only qualitative examples (Sealed Air, Corning).
- The outside-innovators source doesn't address how an organization should decide between investing in place-based innovation-ecosystem engagement versus individual-outsider practices (rotators, translators, allies) when resources are limited, or whether the two approaches substitute for or complement each other in practice.
