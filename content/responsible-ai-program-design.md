## Summary

Why responsible AI programs stall between principle and practice — three recurring gaps (accountability, strategy, resourcing) diagnosed from 20+ executive interviews, the SHARP framework for closing them, and an eight-question readiness checklist for designing an enterprise-wide RAI program before rushing to implement it. A parallel study of emerging-technology commercialization generally finds the same gaps and proposes a complementary four-principle framework — anticipation, reflexivity, inclusion, responsiveness — and Scotiabank's award-winning "Ethics Assistant" shows what closing those gaps looks like in practice.

## Concept

Two pieces of research address the same underlying failure from complementary angles: organizations widely embrace the language of responsible AI (RAI) — fairness, accountability, transparency — yet implementation consistently lags behind stated ambition, producing biased outcomes, unexplainable decisions, and public backlash even at organizations that clearly understand the risks in principle. New York City's experience is a vivid, public illustration: its 2023 AI action plan publicly committed to exactly these principles and created a dedicated oversight role, the algorithm management and policy officer, yet within months a chatbot the city deployed to give small-business owners regulatory guidance was found misinforming users about labor laws and licensing requirements in ways that could actually lead them into violations — a failure that raised questions not just about the chatbot's technical accuracy but about the city's governance protocols and deployment process more broadly, offering a cautionary tale for any organization deploying AI at meaningful scale, not merely public institutions. Interviews with more than 20 AI leaders, ethics officers, and senior executives across technology, financial services, healthcare, and the public sector surfaced why this pattern recurs, identifying three specific, structural gaps rather than a simple lack of good intentions. The accountability gap is the most stubborn: even when organizations publish RAI principles, few define who is actually responsible for embedding them into day-to-day work or how that responsibility should be exercised in practice, so responsibility ends up widely shared but rarely genuinely owned. This often starts with how principles get written in the first place — one adviser observed that "companies define principles based on what they're already doing. It legitimizes current practices. It doesn't ask them to change much" — and persists through execution, where fairness assessments are sometimes run by the very teams building the models being assessed, biases embedded in historical training data go unaddressed for lack of a defined fix-it process, and even organizations with mature financial-audit cultures find themselves without an equivalent standard for algorithmic fairness (one executive put it directly: "we know how to audit financial statements. But algorithmic systems? There's no GAAP for fairness"). Compounding this, incentive systems rarely reward RAI practice at all — most practitioners are evaluated on speed, accuracy, and delivery, not on whether their models are fair or explainable, leaving RAI efforts to drift toward symbolic compliance: policies published, values declared, teams reminded to "be ethical," but without the systematic follow-through dedicated governance actually requires. The strategy gap describes a related but distinct failure: AI ethics considerations often remain disconnected from how an organization actually creates value or measures success, because RAI functions are frequently located within compliance, privacy, or risk departments that operate downstream of product and business decisions rather than informing them from the start — "we're consulted only after the product is built, not while it's being designed," as one RAI lead put it. In organizations that reward rapid innovation, this positions ethics as a speed bump rather than a strategic asset, and pilots sometimes stall not because they fail technically but because they raise usage questions no one thought to discuss upfront; without RAI structurally connected to strategy, product decisions lack real guardrails and governance programs lose organizational influence. The resource gap describes the final piece: even where genuine commitment to ethical AI exists, organizations' actual structures, staffing, and tools frequently don't match their stated aspirations — fairness reviews remain technically feasible but rarely properly resourced, AI teams often lack access to staff trained in bias mitigation, and well-meaning governance boards fade into irrelevance once competing demands crowd out the time needed to sustain them; even available technology often goes unused, as one financial-institution data scientist described having access to an open-source fairness toolkit with no integration into their actual workflow and no guidance on when to use it — "so we don't." <span class="src">The Three Obstacles Slowing Responsible AI</span>

The research proposes a five-part framework, SHARP, for closing these three gaps, describing not a rigid linear sequence but different ways organizations have successfully reconfigured their structures, incentives, and routines. Structuring ownership at the project level means assigning a named RAI lead to each high-impact AI initiative, embedded directly within development teams rather than confined solely to legal or compliance — a global manufacturing company's digital ethics team illustrates this by operating across the whole organization with real influence over decision-making, not merely symbolic gestures, while a financial services company found that naming a specific individual eliminated the diffusion of responsibility that comes from assuming "someone else" is watching for potential harms ("if it's everyone's job, it's no one's job"). Hardwiring ethics into everyday procedures means going beyond checklists to embed ethical review directly into existing product-development workflows — a healthcare analytics company built an ethics checklist requiring training-data validation, stakeholder consultation, and documented harm assessment directly into its DevOps pipeline alongside automated tests and deployment protocols specifically so it wouldn't feel like an externally imposed hurdle: "it had to feel like something we do — not something someone else makes us do." Aligning ethical risk with business risk means reframing AI ethics in terms executives already track and act on — reputation, regulatory exposure, operational disruption — rather than leaving it as an abstract values conversation; a European insurance company modeled the concrete financial and reputational cost of an algorithmic-bias failure reaching the media, tracing cascading costs from client churn through brand damage, and found the conversation genuinely changed once leaders could see the downstream risk quantified, while another organization began reporting RAI indicators alongside cybersecurity and operational-integrity metrics on its enterprise risk dashboard, repositioning AI ethics as a material risk-management concern rather than a values issue and prompting product teams to anticipate ethical risk earlier rather than waiting for downstream review. Rewarding responsible behavior means explicitly incentivizing the behaviors organizations say they want, since ethical diligence often directly competes with pressure to ship fast — a Nordic energy company added an "impact awareness" dimension to performance reviews for its AI and data teams, recognizing engagement with fairness audits and transparency work not by "docking points" for problems but by actively signaling what the organization values, while a European telecommunications firm created an internal award specifically for responsible innovation, honoring teams that shelved a promising model over fairness concerns or prioritized transparency over raw performance. Practicing ethical judgment, not just compliance, means recognizing that no process can fully substitute for human judgment in genuinely novel or subtle ethical situations — one public agency introduced recurring "AI ethics labs," informal, facilitated cross-functional sessions built less to produce specific decisions than to develop a shared vocabulary and the confidence to raise concerns, while a logistics firm adapted its agile sprint retrospectives to include rotating prompts about data use, consent, or unintended consequences, normalizing ethical inquiry as a routine part of already-existing workflows rather than a separate, occasional exercise — both organizations aiming to keep employees active moral agents rather than passive recipients of algorithmic suggestions, since overreliance on AI-based decision-making can otherwise crowd out the habits of ethical reflection that keep human judgment sharp over time. The overarching finding is that closing the gap between RAI aspiration and practice isn't primarily a technical or philosophical challenge — it's an organizational one, requiring genuine investment in the people, processes, incentives, and routines that make ethical intent sustainable, with executive leadership positioned not as the sole owner of responsible AI but as the enabler of the cultural and structural conditions that let it actually take root across the organization. <span class="src">The Three Obstacles Slowing Responsible AI</span>

The second piece of research addresses a specific, common failure mode within this broader picture: organizations rushing to implement RAI programs before they've actually finished designing them, producing predictably inefficient, hard-to-scale efforts at managing AI's ethical, reputational, and legal risk, wasted resources, and slowed innovation as a result. The pattern is understandable — leaders reasonably want to confirm an AI use case actually works before investing heavily in the guardrails around it (no one wants to install seat belts before knowing whether the car can move), but once a solution proves its ROI, the risks that were set aside become urgent all at once, and teams end up improvising an RAI program on the fly because they never had a clear model of what a genuinely robust one looks like. A common intermediate step — building a narrow "mini" RAI program scoped to just the specific solution being deployed, with use-case-specific policies for usage, risk assessment, and metrics — is a reasonable stopgap, but becomes unwieldy and inefficient as more AI use cases accumulate, at which point designing a genuine enterprise-wide program becomes unavoidable, and arguably should have come first. The recommended readiness checklist poses eight questions an organization should be able to answer "yes" to before implementation, not after. First, has the organization determined its RAI program's actual strategic objectives — aiming to be best-in-class, to meet existing regulatory minimums, or to get ahead of pending regulation — since the answer shapes everything downstream, including whether the program should be rolled out consistently across every market or tailored market by market (with correspondingly different thresholds for escalation and go/no-go decisions in each case). Second, are the program's stated RAI values genuinely connected to concrete procedures, since abstract commitments to "fairness," "safety," and "transparency" mean different things to different stakeholders and create only the illusion of internal alignment until an organization actually tries to implement a program across a misaligned staff — a genuine commitment to fairness, for instance, needs to translate into specifics like identifying which stakeholders could be discriminated against at each stage of an AI system's lifecycle, and what happens when that risk crosses an acceptable threshold. Third, has the organization designed genuine RAI metrics (KPIs and OKRs) distinguishing between measuring the program itself (employee awareness, compliance, program impact, and whether improvement efforts are working) and measuring individual AI models or solutions — a distinction many organizations conflate, with real complexity hiding in decisions about what specifically to measure, who's responsible for collecting and evaluating it, how frequently, and what benchmarks and thresholds actually apply. Fourth, has the organization trained the cross-functional people responsible for overseeing the program's implementation and impact, since an RAI committee's members typically bring deep expertise in their own specific risk domain but correspondingly limited visibility into risks outside it, and none of them are automatically experts at actually rolling out and overseeing an enterprise program — meaning the committee itself needs dedicated training and alignment on priorities, risk sources, and thresholds, not just individual subject-matter knowledge. Fifth, does the organization have (or a credible plan to acquire) the personnel actually needed to scale and maintain the program over time, since existing QA, quality-improvement, and audit staff typically aren't equipped for this work by default even when well-intentioned — training existing personnel is usually less expensive and disruptive than hiring from scratch, though organizations should expect eventually to need dedicated new hires as their AI footprint grows. Sixth, has the organization mapped how its RAI program will interact with other enterprise priorities — cybersecurity, privacy, multi-cloud and other IT optimization programs — asking specifically whether RAI interferes with those programs (unnecessarily stifling innovation, for instance) or augments them, and what needs adjusting (workflows, staffing, budget) for everything to work in harmony rather than in conflict. Seventh, has the organization developed a genuine strategic roadmap covering execution from design through implementation, monitoring, quality assurance, and audit, including decisions about deployment cadence (enterprise-wide and simultaneous, versus sequential department by department, with defined milestones gating progression) and which stakeholders — vendors, suppliers, strategic partners, board members — get included in scope. Eighth, has the organization designed an actual implementation playbook translating that roadmap into concrete, department-specific guidance — how workflows get customized per team, how each department will identify and mitigate its own specific risks, and how vendors and partners get incorporated — including the checklists and tutorials needed to ensure people can actually follow the documented procedures rather than merely knowing they exist. The article's closing distinction matters as much as the checklist itself: "baby steps" belong squarely in the implementation of an RAI program, where a phased rollout by department or region is genuinely sensible given competing organizational priorities and finite resources — but not in the design of the program, which should be as complete as possible before any phase of rollout begins. The analogy offered is a building with four planned wings: it's better to complete the design of the full building and then construct it wing by wing than to build a single wing first and improvise additions to it later — and fortunately, completing an RAI program's design generally requires far fewer resources than actually implementing it, making thorough upfront design a comparatively cheap investment against the alternative of costly, difficult-to-unwind course correction down the line. <span class="src">Designing a Responsible AI Program Start with this Checklist</span>

<span class="src">Avoiding Harm in Technology Innovation</span> (MIT SMR, September 2024), by an author team including Gina Colarelli O'Connor (whose separate research on strategic-innovation function design also appears elsewhere in this vault), generalizes the same underlying failure this concept's two AI-specific sources diagnose to the commercialization of any emerging technology, not AI alone. Interviewing executives and managers across technology scouting, adoption, and commercialization roles about how their organizations weigh potential harms to health, the environment, and society, the authors found essentially the same pattern SHARP's three gaps describe: business imperatives dominate early-stage decision-making, legal and regulatory compliance dominate later stages, and almost no interviewee could point to a systematic process their organization expected them to follow for evaluating a technology's broader implications before releasing it — leaving individuals to navigate tensions among compliance, financial return, and personal ethical judgment largely alone, with the organization nonetheless bearing the consequences when that individual judgment proves wrong. The opening case study — Air Canada's customer-service chatbot fabricating bereavement-fare policy, leading a customer to sue and win, with lasting reputational damage — is offered as a vivid illustration of exactly the accountability and strategy gaps SHARP identifies, playing out with a real AI deployment even though the broader study's scope is not AI-specific. The article also documents a structural evasion pattern this concept's other sources don't cover: rather than avoiding harm outright, some companies offload liability instead — one formed legally separate entities to develop riskier technologies, another took a startup-board observer seat specifically to influence direction without assuming liability for outcomes — a workaround the article's proposed framework is designed to close. That framework, "Responsible Innovation and Commercialization" (RIC), adapts the academic Responsible Research and Innovation tradition into four business-relevant principles that function as a more general, non-AI-specific counterpart to SHARP: anticipation (systematically imagining a technology's possible uses and consequences, both for the company and for affected stakeholder groups, before committing to commercialize it — illustrated by a corporate venture capital team declining to invest in an engineered microbe technology after two staff chemists raised unresolved questions about accidental environmental dispersal); reflexivity (surfacing a team's own unexamined industry, professional, and organizational biases and assumptions, illustrated by a chemical-company venture manager rejecting an ocean-biodegradable-plastic technology specifically because it only met an industry standard that still tolerated 30% of the treated plastic ending up on the ocean floor); inclusion (deliberately engaging affected external stakeholders — not to build consensus, but to surface perspectives an organization's internal assumptions would otherwise miss — illustrated by a company that, after facing public backlash and a forced product withdrawal from skipping stakeholder engagement on a GMO food product, now proactively engages the advocacy groups it once avoided); and responsiveness (accepting accountability for a technology's consequences and having a concrete response plan, up to and including market withdrawal, ready before those consequences surface — illustrated by a company that discovered early safety-literature signals about nanoparticle inhalation risk during small-scale pre-launch testing of a cleaning-spray application and pivoted to a different use case rather than proceeding). The article closes by naming the same practical obstacles this concept's other sources identify for SHARP and the eight-question checklist — competitive pressure to prioritize speed and profit, vague mission statements that produce disagreement about what "ethical" even means in context, and under-resourcing — while noting that individual managers can meaningfully apply RIC's principles to their own projects even without full organizational buy-in, sometimes building the track record that leads to broader adoption over time. <span class="src">Avoiding Harm in Technology Innovation</span>

<span class="src">How Scotiabank Built an Ethical, Engaged AI Culture</span> (MIT SMR, July 2024) supplies a concrete, award-winning real-world instance of the SHARP framework's "hardwire ethics into everyday procedures" component, at a bank that won both a Most Innovative Use of AI award and a Best Responsible AI Program award at the same industry event. Scotiabank developed, jointly with Deloitte Canada, an "Ethics Assistant" application that evaluates the ethical impact of every new AI or machine-learning use case as the mandatory first step of project development — early enough in the process that a use case flagged with ethics issues can still be redesigned before deployment, rather than being caught only after the fact. This sits alongside a formal data ethics policy folded directly into the bank's code of conduct (which every employee must re-attest to annually) and a mandatory data ethics education program for anyone in the bank's analytics organization or doing advanced analytics work elsewhere — together the specific combination of structural, procedural, and cultural commitments that distinguished Scotiabank's program from other financial institutions' in the award judging. The article frames responsible AI and effective AI as arising from the same underlying cultural shift rather than as competing priorities: the chatbot that won the innovation award (whose accuracy rose from 35% to 90% since its late-2022 launch, now resolving more than 40% of customer questions without human intervention, and whose LLM-generated conversation summaries cut human-agent ramp-up time 60 to 70 percent on escalated cases) improved specifically because contact-center staff, data and analytics, product design, and engineering teams took collaborative ownership of curating the underlying knowledge base — the same participative, cross-functional engagement pattern that made the bank's ethics program credible rather than symbolic. The bank's chief data and analytics officer, Grace Lee, connects this improvement directly to generative AI's broader accessibility: in the authors' own executive survey, the share of organizations reporting an established data-and-analytics culture doubled from 21% to 43% in a single year, a shift they attribute to generative AI making data quality problems newly visible and newly worth fixing to everyday business users, not just data specialists. Scotiabank's parallel effort to extend data ownership from structured data (consolidated into a single cloud-hosted enterprise data model) to unstructured data — treating knowledge-base curation as a genuine data-management responsibility rather than a one-off content task, with plans to extend the contact center's and payments business's early wins progressively across the rest of the bank — illustrates concretely what "hardwiring ethics into everyday procedures" looks like when it's actually resourced rather than merely declared, addressing this concept's resource gap directly rather than only its accountability and strategy gaps. <span class="src">How Scotiabank Built an Ethical, Engaged AI Culture</span>
