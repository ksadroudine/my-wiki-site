## Summary

Traditional cybersecurity incident-response playbooks don't fit AI failures, because AI systems are probabilistic and can cause harm without any malicious actor involved. Organizations need AI-specific incident-response policies covering definition, harm identification, containment, and eradication — and a concrete case study of one specific incident category, deepfake-enabled executive impersonation fraud, shows what defending against a real AI-driven attack actually looks like.

## Highlights

- Traditional cybersecurity incident definitions (NIST's included) assume an incident stems from a bad actor violating security policy, but AI systems can cause serious harm with no malicious intent at all, simply because they are probabilistic and guaranteed to be wrong some percentage of the time — Google's AI image tool that labeled Black people as gorillas is a canonical example with no attacker involved.
- An AI incident-response policy needs a clear definition of what counts as "AI" and, just as importantly, what doesn't — since AI is frequently embedded within larger traditional software systems (an AI chatbot inside a broader platform), and knowing where the boundary sits determines which response playbook and team applies.
- A short-term containment plan should exist before a model is even deployed, since diagnosing the actual root cause of an AI failure can take weeks or months; one company without such a plan discovered its AI-based HR screening tool was giving preferential treatment to a demographic group and, lacking any way to scale back or modify the tool quickly, had no option but to shut it down entirely for over a year.
- AI incidents can be surfaced through three complementary channels: "appeal and override" mechanisms that let end users flag undesirable outputs directly, automated model-monitoring systems with anomaly alerts, and pre-deployment "red teaming" where an independent team deliberately tries to make the system misbehave.
- Fixing problematic AI behavior falls into three technical categories with very different costs: pre-processing (retraining on more representative data), in-processing (rebuilding the model's weights or architecture — rarely used in practice because it's so resource-intensive), and post-processing (adding rule-based output filters after the fact, the approach Google used to fix its gorilla-labeling incident by simply disallowing gorilla-related outputs altogether).
- Deloitte's Center for Financial Services projects generative-AI-enabled fraud losses in the U.S. will reach $40 billion by 2027, up from $12.3 billion in 2023, driven substantially by deepfakes — GAN-generated video, image, text, or voice content realistic enough to impersonate real people, built from photos, audio, and video of the target (making celebrities, politicians, and public figures with large media footprints disproportionately easy to impersonate).
- An attempted deepfake fraud against Ferrari — a scammer impersonating CEO Benedetto Vigna via WhatsApp voice and video, urging an executive to sign an NDA for a fabricated acquisition — was foiled when the targeted executive asked a question only the real CEO could answer (the title of a book Vigna had recently recommended); the scammer couldn't answer and ended the call.
- Deepfake scams succeed by exploiting specific cognitive biases rather than just technical realism: trust bias (deference to familiar/authoritative sources), manufactured time pressure that short-circuits careful verification, confirmation bias (a deepfake that matches what a target already expects to hear slips through more easily), and the deep-seated human assumption that audiovisual content is inherently trustworthy.
- Seven concrete organizational defenses against deepfake-driven fraud: employee vigilance training and simulation drills, multistep identity-verification protocols for sensitive communications, ongoing digital-literacy and deepfake-detection training, explicit cognitive-bias-awareness education, migrating sensitive communications off consumer apps like WhatsApp onto encrypted enterprise platforms, layering technological safeguards (biometrics, multifactor authentication, fraud-detection monitoring) with mandatory human dual-review of high-risk requests, and continuously updated fraud-detection systems paired with red-teaming exercises.

## Concept

<span class="src">How to Prepare Your Company for AI Incidents</span> (HBR, December 2024), written by a lawyer whose firm focuses exclusively on managing AI liabilities, argues that the mature, widely accepted playbook for traditional software incident response doesn't transfer to AI failures, because the underlying definition of an "incident" doesn't fit. Standard cybersecurity frameworks (including NIST's) define an incident around a bad actor violating security policy, but AI systems are probabilistic — guaranteed to be wrong some percentage of the time — and can cause serious harm with no malicious actor involved at all, as when Google's AI photo-labeling tool consistently mislabeled Black people as gorillas. The article lays out what an AI-specific incident-response policy needs: a clear definition of what counts as AI (and what doesn't, since AI is frequently embedded inside larger conventional software systems, making the boundary between applicable playbooks unclear without an explicit definition); identification of which harms matter most for the organization's context (physical harm for robotics or transportation companies, economic-opportunity or fairness harms for financial services and healthcare); designated incident responders spanning IT, cybersecurity, legal, communications, and domain experts, potentially supplemented by external AI-incident specialists; and — critically — a short-term containment plan developed before the model is even deployed, since diagnosing an AI failure's actual root cause can take weeks or months. The costs of skipping this last step are illustrated by an anonymized client whose AI-based HR screening tool was found to be giving preferential treatment to a specific demographic group; lacking any way to quickly scale back or adjust the tool, the company's only option was a full shutdown that lasted more than a year. The article details three ways to surface incidents (user-facing "appeal and override" reporting channels, automated model monitoring with anomaly alerts, and pre-deployment red-teaming) and three technical remedy categories once a cause is identified: pre-processing (retraining on more representative data), in-processing (rebuilding model weights or architecture, rarely used in practice given the resources required), and post-processing (rule-based output filters applied after the fact — the approach Google used to fix its gorilla-labeling failure by simply disallowing gorilla-related labels). The article closes by insisting that disciplined incident response accelerates rather than slows AI adoption, using the analogy that it's brakes, not engines, that let cars go fast, since confidence in the ability to stop is what allows organizations to accelerate in the first place.

<span class="src">How Ferrari Hit the Brakes on a Deepfake CEO</span> (MIT SMR, January 2025) supplies a concrete, real-world case study of exactly the kind of incident category the HBR framework is designed to prepare organizations for, focused specifically on deepfake-enabled executive-impersonation fraud. In July 2024, a Ferrari executive received WhatsApp messages and calls that appeared to come from CEO Benedetto Vigna — complete with a matching profile photo and a voice mimicking Vigna's Southern Italian accent — urging immediate signature of an NDA for a fabricated acquisition, with the scammer claiming Italian regulators had already been informed. Sensing something was off despite the convincing audiovisual detail, the executive asked a verification question only the real Vigna could answer (the title of a book Vigna had recently recommended); the scammer, unable to answer, ended the call. The article situates this incident within a fast-growing threat category — Deloitte projects generative-AI-enabled fraud losses in the U.S. will reach $40 billion by 2027, more than triple 2023's $12.3 billion — created by GAN (generative adversarial network) technology, in which a generator network produces fake media while a discriminator network evaluates its realism, iterating until the discriminator can no longer tell the difference. The article's more distinctive contribution is a psychological analysis of why deepfake scams succeed: they exploit trust bias (deference to familiar, authoritative sources like a CEO), manufactured urgency that short-circuits careful verification, confirmation bias (fabricated content that matches what a target already expects to hear, like news of an impending acquisition, slips through more easily), and the deep human assumption that audiovisual content is inherently trustworthy. From this analysis, the article derives seven organizational defenses: employee vigilance training and simulated phishing/deepfake drills; multistep identity-verification protocols for sensitive communications (the Ferrari executive's "only the real CEO would know" question is offered as a simple, effective template); ongoing digital-literacy and deepfake-detection training, especially for executives who are disproportionately targeted; explicit education about the specific cognitive biases deepfakes exploit; migrating sensitive corporate communications off consumer platforms like WhatsApp onto encrypted, enterprise-grade alternatives; a multilayered defense combining technological safeguards (biometrics, multifactor authentication, fraud-detection monitoring) with mandatory human dual-review of high-risk requests; and continuously updated fraud-detection systems paired with ongoing red-teaming exercises.

Read together, the two sources operate at complementary levels of generality: the HBR piece supplies the organizational policy architecture for responding to AI incidents of any kind — how to define them, who responds, how to contain and eradicate harm, and how to learn from what happened — while the MIT SMR piece works through one specific, fast-growing incident category (deepfake-driven social-engineering fraud) in enough real-world detail to show what disciplined incident awareness and containment actually looks like when a live attack is underway. The Ferrari case is, in effect, the HBR framework's "appeal and override" and vigilance-based detection layer working exactly as intended: a single employee's willingness to question a seemingly authoritative, urgent communication caught an incident before any harm occurred, illustrating why both sources treat human judgment as an essential layer of defense rather than a stopgap to be engineered away.

## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses how AI incident response should be coordinated with traditional cybersecurity incident response when both playbooks apply simultaneously — for instance, a deepfake attack (covered by the Ferrari case) is itself a form of AI-enabled incident that the general HBR framework's harm-identification and containment steps would presumably need to cover, but the two articles don't explicitly cross-reference each other's frameworks.
- The Ferrari case succeeded because one executive happened to ask the right verification question; neither source addresses how consistently effective ad hoc human vigilance is likely to be at scale as deepfake quality continues to improve, versus how quickly organizations should expect to need to rely primarily on the technological and procedural safeguards described.
