## Summary

Getting real value from generative AI depends more on the user's own discipline than on the model itself — a structured self-audit across five habits (set up, refine, verify, own, systematize) and a "virtual personal board of directors" of AI personas both show how deliberate practice turns AI sessions into compounding personal capability rather than one-off chats. A companion how-to guide shows the concrete mechanics of building persistent custom AI assistants, the practical vehicle for turning that discipline into a reusable tool.

## Highlights

- A field experiment randomly assigning 250 employees at a Chinese technology consulting firm to use ChatGPT or not found AI access made them significantly more creative by supervisor and outside-evaluator judgment — but only among employees with strong metacognitive strategies (reflecting on their own thinking, recognizing knowledge gaps, refining their approach), establishing metacognition as the missing link between merely using AI and using it well.
- A five-goal self-audit (set up, refine, verify, own, systematize) organizes 30 specific habits for getting more from any significant AI session, run as a single reusable "macro-prompt" pasted into an existing conversation that scores the session and identifies exactly which habits were missed.
- "Set up" means passing the "intern test" — providing role, context, constraints, and materials as if briefing a brand-new hire — with negative constraints (explicitly stating what NOT to produce, e.g. "no generic SWOT, no hedging") identified as the single highest-leverage, most commonly skipped habit.
- "Refine" means iterating on your own instructions rather than just re-asking for a better answer — the single most valuable habit identified is asking the AI directly, "What question should I be asking that I am not currently asking?"
- "Verify" means treating fluent AI output as a new category of quality risk distinct from obviously bad output: a request to summarize a familiar regulatory history came back "beautifully written, logically structured, and completely wrong" on two key points, nearly slipping into a committee meeting undetected — countered with habits like a temporal-awareness check ("what is the date of your most recent information?") and a confidence stress-test ("rate your confidence in each factual claim").
- "Own" means actively imposing a personal voice and position rather than accepting AI's default hedged, generically competent output — one technique for this is consulting a "virtual personal board of directors," a set of AI personas modeled on real or archetypal figures (a tactical-power-dynamics persona, a commercial-realism persona, an operational-rigor persona) that stress-test a draft before it's finalized.
- "Systematize" means converting a one-off successful session into a reusable "macro-prompt" template (with [variable] placeholders) rather than closing the chat and losing all the accumulated context and refinement — turning individual expertise into a transferable organizational capability.
- A virtual personal board of directors — AI personas modeled on iconic leaders and thinkers (a moral-reconciler persona modeled on Nelson Mandela, a visionary-disruptor persona modeled on Steve Jobs, a systems-builder persona modeled on Meg Whitman) consulted through structured prompts — offers timeless/global perspective, always-on availability, deliberately designed cognitive diversity, and psychological safety for testing unpopular ideas, without replacing real human advisers.
- A real build-versus-buy decision (acquire a fintech company's AI-native loan-servicing platform versus build one internally) worked through a virtual board surfaced considerations a single human perspective would likely have missed: a tactical-power-dynamics lens on who benefits long-term from the deal structure, an operational-rigor lens on underestimated post-acquisition integration costs, and a psychologically-reflective lens questioning whether nostalgia for the legacy system was clouding the decision.
- The two practices converge on the same underlying claim: AI widens the gap between disciplined and undisciplined users rather than leveling it, since people who skip deliberate practice "generate more volume without more insight" (a pattern researchers have called "unsustainable intensity"), while those who build reusable habits and structures compound their capability with every session.
- Custom AI assistants — what ChatGPT calls a "custom GPT," Claude calls a "Project," and Google Gemini calls a "Gem" — store the background files and recurring instructions a frequent user would otherwise re-upload and re-type for every similar task, directly operationalizing this concept's "systematize" habit as a concrete, no-code product feature rather than an abstract discipline.
- Building an effective custom assistant follows four steps: choose a platform based on specific needs (ChatGPT for voice conversation, Claude for writing style, Gemini for integrating Google Docs/Gmail); experiment in a normal chat session first and synthesize what worked into draft instructions; write custom instructions in the second person that specify persona, desired outputs, goals, and tone; and provide reference files the assistant can draw on as examples or context — most taking under 20 minutes to build.
- Four major use-case categories for custom assistants recur: writing/marketing/communications (drafting content with reusable guidelines and examples), how-tos/troubleshooting (personalized tech support, financial/tax helpers, equipment manuals), productivity/project management (sorting tasks, extracting action items from meeting transcripts), and strategic advice/coaching/training (an always-on sounding board with configurable tone, from strict taskmaster to encouraging cheerleader) — directly paralleling this concept's own examples spanning due-diligence analysis, board-of-directors consultation, and habit-audit macro-prompts.
- A custom assistant is never perfect on the first try — like this concept's "refine" habit, it requires iterating: giving explicit feedback on what's wrong, updating instructions, and adding files, with the assistant even able to draft its own revised instructions based on how a session unfolded, rather than repeatedly padding out a single long chat thread that eventually starts "forgetting" earlier context.

## Concept

<span class="src">Audit Yourself to Get More From GenAI</span> (MIT SMR, April 2026) opens from a personal problem: after more than a year of daily generative AI use, the author had no benchmark or feedback loop for whether his own sessions were any good, so he built one — a single reusable "macro-prompt" that audits any significant AI session against 30 specific habits organized under five goals. The article grounds this in a randomized field experiment (a research team including MIT Sloan's Jackson Lu, assigning 250 employees at a Chinese technology consulting firm to use ChatGPT or not) that found AI access made employees significantly more creative by both supervisor and outside-evaluator judgment, but only among those with strong metacognitive strategies — the ability to reflect on their own thinking, recognize gaps, and adjust. This finding reframes the self-audit's purpose: it isn't a way to judge AI's output, but a way to install metacognition where it doesn't naturally occur. The five goals are: set up (preparing the AI with role, context, constraints, and materials — the "intern test" — with explicit negative constraints as the single highest-leverage, most-skipped habit); refine (iterating on your own instructions rather than re-asking for a better answer, epitomized by the prompt "What question should I be asking that I am not currently asking?"); verify (treating fluent-sounding AI output as a distinct new category of risk, illustrated by a near-miss where a beautifully written but factually wrong regulatory summary almost reached a committee meeting, countered by temporal-awareness and confidence-stress-test habits); own (actively imposing a personal voice and position on output that otherwise defaults to hedged, generically competent prose, using techniques including consultation with a "virtual personal board of directors"); and systematize (converting a successful one-off session into a reusable macro-prompt template rather than losing the accumulated context when the chat closes). Applied to a real $5 billion competitive due-diligence analysis, these habits surfaced a material discrepancy between a competitor's public narrative and its internal employee-sentiment data, and independently caught a $42 million enforcement action the AI's own output had understated — concrete evidence the discipline, not the model, produced the value. <span class="src">Audit Yourself to Get More From GenAI</span>

<span class="src">How I Built a Personal Board of Directors With GenAI</span> (MIT SMR, July 2025), by the same author and explicitly cited within "Audit Yourself" as the source of its "own"-stage board-of-directors technique, develops one specific practice in much greater depth: building a "virtual personal board of directors" (MVP Board) — a set of AI personas modeled on real and archetypal leaders, each with a distinct leadership lens, consulted through structured multi-persona prompts. The author's board spans specific domains deliberately: a tactical persona modeled on Sun Tzu for power dynamics, an empathetic-strategist persona modeled on former PepsiCo CEO Indra Nooyi, a bold-realist persona modeled on Mark Cuban, a systems-builder persona modeled on Meg Whitman, a visionary-disrupter persona modeled on Steve Jobs, and others spanning ethics, storytelling, and even a family member's engineering perspective. The article frames this not as a replacement for real human advisers but as a complementary "hybrid brain trust": real advisers contribute trust, depth, shared history, and emotional intelligence that no algorithm can replicate, while the virtual board contributes breadth, always-on availability, deliberately engineered cognitive diversity, and psychological safety for testing ideas too early or unpopular to share with real people yet. A worked example — a build-versus-buy decision about whether to acquire a fintech company's AI-native loan-servicing platform or build one internally — shows the board surfacing considerations a single perspective would likely miss: post-acquisition integration costs, IP ownership and roadmap control, the human experience of organizational change, and whether nostalgia for the legacy system (rather than sound judgment) was driving the instinct to build rather than buy. The article's practical guidance is to start small (three to five personas), treat the board as a living system that evolves with context, and explicitly seek perspective rather than perfection. <span class="src">How I Built a Personal Board of Directors With GenAI</span>

Read together, the two articles describe a single author's evolving personal practice, with the later piece formalizing and generalizing a technique the earlier piece introduced as one tactic among several. Both converge on the same underlying claim about who benefits from generative AI: not simply people with access to the best models, but people who bring deliberate, reflective structure to how they use any model, since the gap between disciplined and undisciplined use — not the gap between good and bad AI — is what actually determines whether a session produces compounding personal capability or just more words.

<span class="src">How to Build Your Own AI Assistant</span> (HBR, Alexandra Samuel, March 2025) supplies the concrete product mechanics underneath what this concept's other sources treat mostly as an abstract discipline — specifically, the "systematize" habit's practical vehicle. Most major generative AI platforms let a user create a persistent custom assistant (ChatGPT's "custom GPT," Claude's "Project," Google Gemini's "Gem") that stores background files and recurring instructions so they don't need to be re-uploaded and re-typed for every similar task; the author reports having built assistants for marketing content, a strategy sounding board, tech support, research summarization, and project tracking, most taking under 20 minutes each and saving hours of repeated work since. Building one follows four steps: choosing a platform based on its actual strengths for the task at hand (ChatGPT for voice-based conversation, Claude for writing style, Gemini for integrating Google Docs and Gmail directly); experimenting first in an ordinary chat session and synthesizing what worked into draft custom instructions (even asking the AI itself to write those instructions from the session's feedback); writing the instructions in second person, specifying persona, desired output format, goals, and tone; and supplying reference files the assistant can draw on as examples or context. The article organizes assistant use cases into four categories that map closely onto this concept's own examples: writing, marketing, and communications; how-tos and troubleshooting (personalized technical support, financial or tax helpers, equipment manuals); productivity and project management (sorting tasks, extracting action items from meeting transcripts); and strategic advice, coaching, and training — an always-on sounding board with a configurable tone, directly paralleling this concept's "virtual personal board of directors" technique, though implemented here as a single configurable persona rather than a multi-persona board. Consistent with this concept's "refine" habit, the article stresses that a custom assistant is never right on the first attempt: it improves through explicit feedback on what's wrong, updated instructions, and additional files — with the practical advantage over a single long-running chat thread that a well-built assistant starts each new work session already loaded with its accumulated context, rather than requiring the same background to be re-explained repeatedly or risking a long thread "forgetting" earlier instructions. <span class="src">How to Build Your Own AI Assistant</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses how these individual habits should be taught or enforced at team or organizational scale beyond "share it with your team" — there's no discussion of how a manager would verify that reports are actually applying the five-goal audit rather than just claiming to.
- The virtual-board technique relies entirely on the AI's ability to accurately simulate a named real person's judgment from public information about them; neither source addresses how faithfully an AI persona actually represents the reasoning style of the person it's modeled on, versus a generic stereotype of that person's public reputation.
- The custom-assistant source doesn't address data privacy or governance implications of uploading potentially sensitive background files and instructions to a third-party platform's persistent assistant feature, an omission notable given this concept's other sources' emphasis on verification and organizational risk.
