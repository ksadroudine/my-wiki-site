## Summary

A three-layer AI governance framework matched to system type and risk scope — rules-based controls for narrow, static systems; ex post alignment controls for opaque, adaptive systems; and propagation-risk controls for interconnected, agentic systems. Grounded in interviews with AI governance leaders at Microsoft, Nasdaq, and major banks, plus three practices for embedding these controls into how organizations actually operate. BCG’s 2026 survey adds six enterprise agent controls that, deployed together, are associated with about three times the agentic value.

## Concept

Based on interviews conducted from 2022 to 2025 with senior AI governance, risk, compliance, data, and product leaders at Microsoft, Barclays, Kyriba, Nasdaq, Lloyds Bank, Danske Bank, and the Abu Dhabi Department of Finance — validated against public disclosures and regulatory filings from more than 40 additional financial institutions — this research addresses a specific gap: government and regulatory frameworks already articulate important AI governance principles (transparency, fairness, accountability), but organizations consistently struggle to translate those principles into actual day-to-day workflows and decisions. The proposed fix reframes AI governance from a compliance obligation into a strategic, adaptive capability that evolves as AI systems scale, use cases multiply, and risk profiles shift — built on two organizing principles: matching governance controls to the specific type of AI system and risk involved, and embedding those controls directly into workflows, decision rights, and accountability structures rather than layering them on as a separate oversight function. Understanding which controls actually apply starts with recognizing that AI risk emerges at two structurally different moments requiring different responses. Development risk covers problems introduced before a system ever goes live — biased or incomplete training data, models inadequately aligned to the actual task, insufficient validation processes — illustrated by an early credit-limit-increase model at one bank studied, where small changes to input data produced unexpectedly large shifts in the model's output decisions. Deployment risk covers problems that emerge only once a model interacts with a dynamic environment and real human operators: data drift, where model quality degrades over time as the statistical properties of live input data diverge from training data; models generating plausible-sounding but false outputs; and users over-trusting outputs they lack the means to independently verify. Nasdaq's AI-driven market-surveillance systems, which generate hundreds of alerts per second monitoring trading activity for suspicious patterns, illustrate deployment risk concretely: the boundary between abnormal and genuinely illicit behavior is often deliberately blurred, since illegitimate trading activity may be specifically engineered to exploit a model's learned patterns and pass as compliant. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

Beyond timing, the specific controls an organization needs depend on two further, independent dimensions of the AI system itself. The first is whether the system is bounded-learning (static) or adaptive. Bounded-learning systems operate within a fixed set of rules and parameters, and their performance improves by optimizing how those existing rules are applied rather than by changing the rules themselves — credit-scoring models refining risk estimates from income or payment history without altering how those variables relate to each other, or most pretrained generative AI models that don't update during actual use, are both examples. Adaptive learning systems, by contrast, evolve continuously by incorporating live production data back into their own training and updating their internal variable relationships over time — algorithmic trading platforms and dynamic fraud-detection systems are the clearest examples. The second dimension is scope: whether a system's errors stay contained within a specific function or task (a narrow-scope system, like anomaly detection within a single transaction stream) or propagate across multiple functions, geographies, or even industries (a wide-scope system, like a cross-border supply-chain optimization platform) — a difference the research characterizes as exponential rather than incremental, since as a system's reach expands, small errors don't simply add up but interact, propagate, and amplify into second-order effects that were never present in any single component. Crossing these two dimensions with timing produces three layered types of control, each necessary but insufficient on its own, and each building on the one before it. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

Rules-based controls form the baseline layer, most effective for narrow, static systems where decision logic is explicit and outcomes are auditable — credit scoring, fraud detection, customer-service chatbots. They work by embedding relevant norms (ethical guidelines, industry standards) and compliance requirements directly into model design as constraints, supplemented by processes like validation testing and anomaly monitoring. A bank studied illustrates this in practice: its credit-limit-increase model deliberately uses a statistical rather than deep-learning approach specifically so decisions remain interpretable, and before any new model deploys, the analytics team produces a "model card" covering three things — data checks (is training data complete, recent, and balanced, and how will the team detect data drift going forward), decision-logic and edge-case review (how do scores translate into approve/deny decisions, with explicit analysis of the threshold "gray zone" where a customer tips from no increase to an increase, ensuring those borderline cases aren't treated unfairly), and bias/discrimination testing (does the model overfit to particular customer profiles or systematically disadvantage specific groups). This model card then undergoes independent quality-assurance review by a model-risk unit with credit-domain and regulatory expertise, followed by internal audit verification, before the model is allowed to go live — and even in this rules-based setting, human judgment remains central: one organization ran each new midmarket lending model against 100 sampled existing client files, having relationship managers compare their own assessments against the model's recommendations, investigating any divergence to determine whether the model had uncovered a genuine insight or was simply overfitting to data idiosyncrasies, with periodic sample reviews continuing as a standard part of the ongoing risk-and-control cycle after launch. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

Ex post alignment controls form the second layer, necessary once systems grow complex enough — particularly deep neural networks — that traditional traceability and explainability methods lose their effectiveness, since rules-based controls fundamentally depend on being able to specify decision logic explicitly, and that logic becomes progressively more opaque as models grow more sophisticated. Generative AI compounds this difficulty further through its inherently stochastic behavior, where an identical prompt can yield different outputs on different occasions. Rather than trying to explain how a decision was reached, ex post alignment controls instead test whether a system's outcomes remain legitimate — assessed against ethical, regulatory, and domain-specific standards, with the emphasis shifting from preventing errors upfront to detecting misalignment as systems operate, learn, and scale in the real world. Microsoft's approach illustrates this operationally: a structured evaluation pipeline assesses high-stakes models against libraries of expert-defined policies articulating what constitutes a "fair" or "acceptable" outcome, with evaluators annotating model outputs against these policies and independent reviewers validating where a system falls short — in some cases partially automated, with continuous monitoring against predefined policy benchmarks and automated flagging of deviations for human review. Algorithmic auditing is a core mechanism within this layer: after deployment, a model's behavior is systematically examined in two steps, first identifying plausible failure scenarios and defining the full use case (who the system serves, who is affected by its decisions, and for what purpose), then monitoring those specific risks by assessing decision outputs, input data, and internal logic against predefined criteria — helping organizations surface unintended disparate impacts, document recurring risk patterns, and trigger corrective action before harm compounds further. A critical, easily overlooked component of ex post alignment is training people not to treat AI outputs as unquestionable truth: since many AI recommendations are inherently probabilistic, users need to learn to interpret them as informed signals rather than final decisions, understanding when to rely on the system, when to challenge it, and how to spot unexpected or biased output. Nasdaq's AI-driven market surveillance illustrates the scale challenge this layer has to manage: with hundreds of high-risk alerts generated per second, cross-functional teams of compliance officers, data scientists, and domain experts review flagged activity through structured case workflows, documenting the rationale behind each conclusion and feeding those outcomes back to model developers to recalibrate detection thresholds — with escalation committees intervening specifically when investigations suggest coordinated bad actors or broader systemic risk, and audit trails capturing the full chain from original alert through human decision to any resulting model adjustment. A separate global bank's fraud-detection experience shows that misalignment problems often stem from workflow breakdown rather than model error itself: alerts were sometimes routed to the wrong team, duplicated across units, or simply left unresolved because no group clearly owned the next step, while customer-service staff occasionally contacted clients based on alerts the fraud team hadn't yet validated — a problem the bank fixed not by improving the underlying model but by mapping the alert workflow step by step, reassigning clear ownership at each decision point, and standardizing explicit rules for when an alert should be suppressed, investigated further, or escalated. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

Propagation-risk controls form the third and most demanding layer, addressing a limitation rules-based and ex post alignment controls share despite operating at different points in a system's lifecycle: both fundamentally treat risk as confined to a single system, focused on discrete errors or individual outputs — an approach that works reasonably well when AI systems operate in relative isolation, but produces genuinely incomplete governance once systems become interconnected through real-time data flows, APIs, and automated decision-making. The rise of agentic AI makes this gap increasingly consequential: as AI systems increasingly initiate actions autonomously, coordinate with other systems, and pursue objectives spanning multiple domains, an error or misalignment originating in one system can propagate into others in ways traditional, single-system-focused controls simply don't anticipate. Regulators are already responding to this shift: the Bank of England has specifically flagged the risks posed by "deep trading agents" — AI-driven strategies that could amplify external market shocks or coordinate with each other in ways that evade human detection entirely — while parallel dynamics show up in healthcare (biased diagnostic models spreading flawed heuristics across hospitals and insurers that share them) and supply chains (algorithmic procurement platforms amplifying pricing errors across entire supplier networks). What makes propagation risk distinctively hard to govern is its structural invisibility: failures travel laterally through hidden interdependencies that often become apparent only once a disruption has already occurred — a minor logistics API error might be harmless in isolation, but combined with an unrelated cyber incident affecting a payment gateway, could contribute to genuine systemic breakdown. Because these risks unfold across interconnected systems that sit beyond the visibility or control of any single organization, managing them requires a fundamental shift from a company-centric to an ecosystem-aware perspective, built on three complementary activities: mapping interdependencies across organizational boundaries, monitoring shared infrastructure for cross-organization vulnerabilities, and institutionalizing genuinely anticipatory (not merely reactive) oversight. The European Central Bank's sectorwide cyber-resilience stress tests illustrate what this looks like at scale — mapping interdependencies across clearinghouses, payment systems, and financial institutions; monitoring shared infrastructure for vulnerabilities that span multiple organizations; and simulating how a localized disruption could cascade through the broader financial system — practices the research argues generalize well beyond financial regulation to any sufficiently interconnected environment. Enacting propagation-risk controls in practice requires redistributing visibility, accountability, and decision rights across an entire ecosystem rather than relying solely on organization-level rules or after-the-fact interventions, which in turn often demands genuine cultural shifts toward data sharing, coordinated standards, and co-investment in shared oversight infrastructure with partners, competitors, regulators, and sometimes even open-source communities — since no single company can achieve resilience entirely on its own when that resilience is fundamentally a property of the broader system it depends on. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

Identifying the right controls is necessary but not sufficient; the research identifies three practices required to actually implement adaptive governance day to day. First, risk-control protocols need to be hardwired directly into operational workflows, accountability structures, and incentives, with oversight flowing into regular planning, audit, and leadership-review cycles rather than sitting on a separate compliance layer disconnected from actual decision-making. One bank studied embeds this directly into its lending workflow: for every approved AI use case, an AI use-case committee documents the risk tier (based on customer impact, regulatory impact, data sensitivity, and model type), the mandatory controls tied to that tier (independent model validation, relationship-manager sample testing, post-deployment review frequency), and the specific decision rights (who can approve model changes and under what conditions) — encoded directly into the credit-approval system itself, so relationship managers cannot bypass validation or review steps without explicit sign-off from both business and risk management. Second, organizations need mechanisms that enable conclusive judgment across genuinely heterogeneous expertise, methods, and risk tolerances — not consensus or homogenized judgment, since differences across domains reflect real, legitimate differences in expertise and risk appetite rather than a flaw to be engineered away. The practical challenge is that siloed knowledge undermines rules-based controls when domain experts lack a shared frame (addressed through joint model reviews and shared model-card documentation), while ex post alignment controls break down specifically when teams operate with misaligned risk thresholds or rely on incompatible validation methods to reconcile expected versus observed outcomes — addressed not by "building trust" in AI recommendations abstractly, but by establishing shared evaluative routines (incident and near-miss analysis, performance-drift monitoring, structured comparison of intended use cases against actual outcomes) that create common reference points letting analytically-driven teams, experimentation-oriented groups, and use-case owners jointly assess whether a model is actually performing as intended, avoiding both premature shutdown from excessive caution and paralysis from unresolved methodological disagreement. The same conclusive-judgment challenge extends to propagation-risk governance, where coordination tends to break down when accountability is diffuse, incentives stay locally optimized within each organization, and no single actor is explicitly responsible for orchestrating cross-boundary trade-offs — meaning ecosystem-level governance needs not just visibility into interdependencies but genuine shared rules of engagement, escalation rights, and decision authority that persist over time, or interdependency mapping risks becoming a one-off analytical exercise rather than a sustained governance capability. Third, governance itself needs to be institutionalized as a living learning system rather than a static set of protocols, since AI risks mutate continuously and controls that don't evolve alongside them inevitably fall behind — requiring explicit assigned responsibility for reviewing incidents and near-misses, systematically documenting what actually went wrong, and translating those lessons into revised policies, recalibrated thresholds, and strengthened controls on an ongoing basis. The overarching argument is that competitive advantage in an AI-driven environment will come not from adopting AI faster than competitors, but from governing it better — embedding oversight precisely where decisions actually get made, where risk actually propagates, and where value is actually created, rather than treating governance as a constraint layered on top of AI adoption after the fact. <span class="src">Scaling AI With Adaptive Governance</span>

BCG's Applied AI Index report treats governance as an enabler of value rather than a brake. It recommends that boards articulate and review AI risk appetite, keep an enterprise-wide inventory of agents and have transparency into the most consequential ones, and notes that agents combining data, tools, permissions, memory and other agents can cause risks to emerge dynamically and compound at machine speed. A large business-process outsourcer is cited using an autonomy ladder from insight to copilot to autopilot set at process rather than tool level. <span class="src">The Formula for Agentic AI Value (BCG)</span>

## Open Questions

- The research doesn't address how smaller organizations without dedicated model-risk units, AI use-case committees, or the resources for algorithmic auditing at scale should adapt this framework — the case studies are drawn entirely from large banks, Microsoft, and Nasdaq.
- The finding that controls triple agentic value is correlational and based on executive self-reports, as noted in the report's methodology.
