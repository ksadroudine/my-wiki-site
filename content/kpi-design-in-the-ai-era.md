## Summary

Organizations chasing a single perfect KPI keep falling prey to Goodhart's Law — the metric gets gamed until it stops reflecting the goal it was meant to track — and machine learning research offers four concrete, borrowed anti-overfitting techniques to fight that pattern, while Agoda's five-year, still-unfinished search for one comprehensive KPI for its business development team shows both why AI itself is often required to build a KPI robust enough to survive real-world noise, and why the search for the "one KPI to rule them all" usually ends in three.

## Concept

MIT SMR's research on organizational metric design starts from Goodhart's Law — "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — and its most vivid recent illustration, Wells Fargo's 2016 scandal, in which employees, incentivized to sell eight financial products per customer, opened millions of unauthorized accounts to hit that target, harming customers and the bank alike. The article's core argument is that traditional solutions to this problem — balanced scorecards, conventional KPIs — routinely fail precisely because they remain vulnerable to narrow optimization and gaming absent careful, ongoing oversight, and that a more sophisticated approach is needed. That more sophisticated approach, the piece argues, already exists — inside machine learning research, which has spent decades studying a structurally identical problem under a different name: overfitting, where a model performs flawlessly on its training data but fails on real-world data because it learned incidental patterns in that training set (a background detail correlated with the label, say) rather than the genuine underlying signal. AI researcher Jascha Sohl-Dickstein's observation that "increased efficiency can sometimes, counterintuitively, lead to worse outcomes" — true "almost everywhere," in his words, whether in machine learning or organizational settings — frames the article's central translation project: taking four established anti-overfitting techniques from ML research and mapping each onto a concrete organizational-metric-design strategy. The first, early stopping, addresses overoptimization directly: ML researchers monitor a model's performance on data separate from its training set and halt training the moment validation performance peaks, even though training performance alone might still be improving — a warning sign of overfitting in progress. Organizationally, this maps to periodically reassessing metrics against true objectives rather than pursuing a fixed metric indefinitely: Amazon shifted its primary internal metric from profit margin to free cash flow per share in 2004 specifically to prevent any single metric from being over-optimized past its useful point; Intel, having long emphasized processor clock speed as its central performance metric, discovered continued speed optimization was producing excessive power consumption, overheating, and diminishing real computing-performance returns, prompting a shift toward a more balanced set of metrics including energy efficiency; and Microsoft under Satya Nadella moved from rigid annual targets to quarterly business reviews specifically so metrics and goals could be adjusted as market conditions and understanding evolved, rather than being pursued past the point of usefulness. A related early-stopping variant is setting minimum rather than maximization targets — unlike Wells Fargo's aggressive "eight products per customer" ceiling-chasing target, a genuine minimum target (a customer service department resolving at least 85% of inquiries within 24 hours, for instance) establishes an acceptable performance threshold without rewarding indefinite over-optimization once that threshold is met. The second technique, noise injection, addresses a related overfitting mechanism: when ML models rely too heavily on specific, predictable patterns in training data, they can memorize incidental quirks rather than learning genuine underlying relationships, so researchers deliberately introduce controlled randomness (randomly disabling some model connections during training, rotating or perturbing training examples) to force the model toward more robust, generalizable learning. Organizations can apply the same logic through random audits, rotated team compositions, and unpredictable evaluation timing, preventing people from learning and exploiting predictable assessment patterns: JPMorgan Chase uses random compliance audits specifically to discourage employees from developing gaming workarounds calibrated to predictable review schedules; the IRS's random-audit program is empirically documented to generate up to $6 in additional revenue for every dollar invested, purely through deterrence, with compliance gains among randomly audited taxpayers persisting for at least five years post-audit; the FDA's unannounced pharmaceutical-manufacturer inspection program (expanded further in 2025 specifically because of its effectiveness) consistently surfaces compliance problems that scheduled, predictable inspections would have missed as facilities temporarily optimized for evaluation day; and the TSA rotates screening personnel across checkpoints and functions specifically to prevent bad actors from studying and exploiting predictable individual screener patterns. The third technique, capacity alignment, addresses the mismatch between model sophistication and available training/validation resources: ML researchers deploy complex, many-parameter models only when they have enough training data and strong validation methods to support that complexity safely, defaulting to simpler models when data or oversight capacity is limited, since overly complex models trained on insufficient data collapse quickly, especially when the same small validation set gets reused repeatedly. Organizationally, this means matching metric complexity to an organization's actual analytical and oversight capabilities — a company with genuinely advanced analytics infrastructure and skilled data scientists can safely implement complex, multidimensional metrics, while an organization with limited oversight capacity should favor simpler, more robust metrics even at the cost of some lost nuance, since the real danger zone is metric complexity that outpaces the organization's actual capacity to validate and monitor it. WeWork's notorious "community adjusted EBITDA" — a metric so complex it excluded not just standard EBITDA adjustments but even basic operating expenses like marketing and administrative costs — illustrates the danger directly: the metric was sophisticated enough to obscure the company's real financial position from investors who lacked the expertise to properly dissect it, yet it wasn't a genuinely sophisticated measure of real business performance, and it helped attract billions in investment before the SEC intervened and the company's underlying business model ultimately collapsed into bankruptcy. JPMorgan Chase's 2012 "London Whale" trading incident shows the same dynamic inside a highly analytically sophisticated organization: a Value-at-Risk model fatally undermined by spreadsheet errors that understated risk by 50%, complex enough that traders could manipulate price inputs within technically acceptable ranges while risk managers lacked the analytical infrastructure to detect either the underlying errors or the deliberate gaming — a combination that produced over $6 billion in losses. Startups, by contrast, tend to naturally practice good capacity alignment by focusing on simple, stage-appropriate metrics (customer acquisition cost, monthly recurring revenue, retention) directly tied to product-market fit and cash flow, only expanding toward more sophisticated unit economics and predictive analytics as their own analytical maturity genuinely grows. The fourth technique, regularization, addresses the core mechanism behind gaming most directly: in ML, regularization penalizes excessive model complexity to prevent a model from becoming so finely tuned to its training data's idiosyncrasies that it performs poorly on new data — forcing the model toward simpler, more generalizable patterns instead. Organizationally, regularization means adding deliberate constraints that make gaming a metric more complex and costly than simply performing well genuinely: Amazon's seller-rating algorithm actively detects suspicious review patterns (sudden five-star review spikes from low-purchase-history accounts, clusters of generic reviews posted in tight time windows) that indicate solicited reviews, penalizing the practice specifically to keep star ratings aligned with real seller quality rather than gaming activity; Netflix, recognizing that optimizing purely for hours-viewed risked incentivizing attention-capturing but ultimately unsatisfying content, shifted in 2023 to a "views" metric (hours viewed divided by content runtime) that the company explicitly called "our best measure of member satisfaction," using multiple complementary metrics that balance each other in a way analogous to ML regularization; Unilever's Sustainable Living Plan added explicit sustainability constraints on pure profit-seeking after discovering unconstrained profit maximization was producing environmental costs the company hadn't been measuring; and Whole Foods (pre-Amazon-acquisition) tied executive compensation caps to average worker pay specifically as a structural constraint against optimizing purely for leadership enrichment. The article's closing framing treats these four techniques as most powerful when applied together rather than in isolation, and stresses that successful implementation typically involves the people who will actually be measured directly in designing the measurement system — producing both better-calibrated metrics and greater genuine buy-in than a top-down metric design process tends to achieve. <span class="src">What AI Can Teach Us About Designing Better KPIs</span>

Agoda's own multiyear account of its search for a single comprehensive KPI illustrates several of these same dynamics playing out in a real, high-stakes operational context, while adding a distinct complication the abstract framework doesn't fully address: sheer combinatorial complexity that makes AI-assisted prediction close to mandatory rather than merely helpful. Agoda's business development team supports hotel, airline, and attraction partners in maximizing their sales on the platform, and the underlying decision space each business developer must navigate is genuinely enormous — a typical portfolio of 100 hotels, each with a conservative estimate of 125 availability/price permutations (room type, room features, pricing segment) per future check-in date, works out to 12,500 permutations per date and more than a million permutations across just the next 90 check-in days for a single business developer. Without some form of comprehensive KPI capable of guiding decisions across that space, Agoda argues the job would be genuinely impossible to perform well. The company's search, chronicled as a continuation of an earlier 2022 MIT SMR piece on the same quest, converged on five desired attributes for a workable KPI: high measurability with low "noise" (natural fluctuation should be much smaller than the effect of deliberate actions, described as "controlling your own destiny"); alignment with overall business value (tying to P&L, though closer P&L alignment tends to increase noise, requiring a deliberate balance); understandability (the easier the underlying logic is to explain, the easier adoption and organizational buy-in become); a comparable currency across individuals and teams (enabling fair comparison and compensation); and minimized communication costs (clarity reduces time spent debating the metric itself rather than acting on it). These attributes map directly onto a fundamental tension between output KPIs and input KPIs that Agoda found itself repeatedly navigating. Output KPIs (bookings, revenue) correlate tightly with actual business value and support genuine ROI measurement and cross-team comparison, but are heavily distorted by externalities entirely outside a business developer's control — an unpredictable Taylor Swift concert announcement, for instance, spiked travel interest in Singapore in a way no analyst could have forecast, meaning a business developer whose portfolio happened to include affected hotels would be credited with strong performance driven by pure luck rather than skill. Input KPIs (like the percentage of a portfolio's hotels enrolled in a specific promotional program) are directly actionable, controllable, and quick to execute, requiring little upfront analysis — but don't reliably indicate whether the underlying action actually generated real value, and tend to apply unevenly across different market contexts (a Black Friday promotion works well as an input KPI for hotels serving European and US customers, for whom Black Friday drives impulse purchases, but is a poor indicator for hotels serving predominantly Southeast Asian customers). Beyond this core tradeoff, Agoda's KPI search had to account for a wide range of genuine externalities entirely beyond the business development team's control — market growth, new flight routes, geopolitical and macroeconomic shifts, competitor and partner activity, and idiosyncratic events like COVID-19 recovery patterns varying sharply by market, or Japan's 2015 visa-requirement relaxation producing a travel boom that flattered the Japan team's output KPIs for reasons having nothing to do with the team's own decision-making — plus internal confounds like company-run A/B experiments that could distort a subset of business developers' metrics for reasons entirely outside their control. After considering and rejecting several simpler candidate metrics (raw revenue, too noisy and externally driven; pricing competitiveness, too fragmented across millions of individual price points and complicated by the gap between public and member rates; single-program-adoption rate, too uneven in value across different programs and hotel tiers), Agoda eventually developed what it calls Supply Equity Points (SEP): a machine-learning-modeled expected-margin value assigned to every possible business-developer action (activating a promotional program, negotiating a price, securing room availability for a given date), refreshed daily, expressed in a common unit that makes global comparison across business developers finally possible. Building SEP required substantial machine learning investment specifically to model millions of scenario permutations, accounting for the depth of potential value a given action might unlock, how that action interacts with a hotel's existing live products, how long the resulting value is expected to persist, and what behavioral changes (increased occupancy leading to higher prices, for instance) might follow a given action going live — work Agoda explicitly states "would likely have failed without the benefit of reliable prediction models," since the noise-reduction and confound-removal SEP performs is precisely the kind of computational problem machine learning is well suited to and traditional manual analysis isn't. Even after roughly five years of continued development, SEP remains imperfect in three specific, ongoing ways: predictability continues to lag accuracy, since internal and external factors that shift quickly create real gaps between predicted and realized SEP values, frustrating business developers who feel disempowered when outcomes diverge from projections; SEP structurally misses some genuinely valuable but hard-to-quantify actions, such as relationship-building, face-to-face meetings with hotel partners, whose incremental value the underlying machine learning model simply can't isolate, forcing Agoda to encourage such activity through separate, non-KPI-based means; and SEP, like most KPIs, can discourage longer-term entrepreneurial bets in favor of short-term optimization, illustrated by Agoda's Japan team pursuing acquisition of ryokans (traditional Japanese inns) that scored poorly under SEP due to insufficient historical data, requiring the team to add ryokan onboarding as a separate input KPI entirely outside the SEP framework to protect the strategic bet from being starved by short-term metric pressure. To manage these gaps, Agoda ultimately settled not on the single comprehensive KPI it originally set out to build, but on three complementary measures: SEP itself as the primary, machine-learning-derived input KPI; a Supply Health Score (SHS), a noisier but genuinely output-oriented price-competitiveness metric that captures value SEP structurally misses and preserves business developers' sense of entrepreneurial ownership over their portfolio; and locally-defined "innovation KPIs" that let regional management teams pursue strategic, longer-horizon bets (like ryokan acquisition) that wouldn't survive evaluation under SEP alone. Agoda's own distilled lessons for other organizations pursuing a similar KPI search echo the ML-derived framework directly: the greater the influence of uncontrollable external factors on a given output goal, the more an organization needs to lean on input KPIs to reduce noise and evaluate performance fairly (a real estate agent's commission, largely insulated from major external confounds, needs far less of this correction than Agoda's booking-dependent business developers do); the greater an organization's own analytical capability, the more control it gains over KPI fluctuation — machine learning and AI, in Agoda's own words, "helped us remove fluctuations that might affect performance evaluation," and without that capability the company's KPI search would likely have failed outright; and complexity itself carries a real, ongoing cost, since a KPI too complex to be understood, or one perceived as an opaque black box, consumes organizational time defending and explaining the metric rather than acting on the business performance it's meant to represent. Agoda's closing reflection frames its multiyear search not as a solved problem but as an ongoing one specifically because AI capability keeps advancing: the team explicitly expects AI to keep "rewriting much of the KPI playbook" by enabling ever more accurate predictions and more flexible, real-time adjustment to what actually matters in a genuinely dynamic operating environment — meaning, in the company's own assessment, this won't be its last search for a better KPI. <span class="src">Building One KPI to Rule Them All</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither source addresses how an organization should decide, in advance, which of the four ML-derived techniques (early stopping, noise injection, capacity alignment, regularization) is most urgently needed for a given metric-design problem — the article presents all four as generally applicable but doesn't offer a diagnostic framework for prioritizing among them under real resource constraints.
- Agoda's account doesn't fully reconcile its own SEP metric's five-year, still-unfinished development timeline with the "capacity alignment" principle from the companion research — it's unclear whether Agoda's continued investment in an imperfect, complex metric reflects appropriately matched analytical capacity, or the kind of complexity-outpacing-oversight risk the WeWork and JPMorgan Chase examples warn against, since the piece reports ongoing predictability problems without fully assessing whether SEP's complexity has in fact outpaced Agoda's ability to validate it.
