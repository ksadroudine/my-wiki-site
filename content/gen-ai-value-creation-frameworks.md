## Summary

Most organizations adopt generative AI broadly but capture little strategic value from it, and many research teams propose complementary ways to close that gap. The sources cover task-level use-case selection, organizational structure, staged risk management, capability maturity, learning loops, testing and ways to measure return on investment, and survey evidence shows that strategic clarity combined with applied AI yields about five times the value.

## Concept

MIT Sloan Management Review's three-year study (2022-2025) of 23 Swiss companies across banking, insurance, healthcare, energy, law, manufacturing, and postal services — drawing on more than 10 workshops and 87 in-depth interviews with practitioners ranging from frontline employees to C-suite executives — found that leaders who successfully scale gen AI value share three practices: expanding use cases across processes rather than staying confined to a single task, treating each use case as a permanent work in progress rather than a one-and-done deployment, and quickly abandoning use cases that fail to demonstrate measurable value. Most traditional companies, organized as multidivisional structures with duplicated functions and limited cross-unit information flow, aren't structured to institutionalize these practices — and the researchers found that of the 23 organizations studied, only 2 managed to scale gen AI into a genuine strategic capability. Both had independently converged on the same organizational innovation, which the researchers call the "AI spine": a cross-functional structure that, unlike a classical hub-and-spoke AI center of excellence (which centralizes technical expertise and deploys technologists out into business units), instead pulls people with deep business-process domain knowledge into a central, self-contained team alongside AI developers, a permanent risk/compliance officer, and a technology owner responsible for preventing data and tooling fragmentation — all overseen by a C-suite leader who keeps the effort aligned with strategic objectives. Critically, the spine is independently funded by top management and receives a cut of the revenue or cost savings its applications generate, which forces rigorous ROI measurement and prevents drift toward low-impact "convenience" use cases. At a Swiss retail bank studied, the spine's first application — an email assistant for customer service — saved roughly 700 hours in an early limited rollout and cut email handling time 15% once scaled, but its early technical version underwhelmed users because it couldn't capture the tacit knowledge (tone, escalation thresholds, edge-case handling) agents held implicitly; the spine's structure of regular project reviews, employee compensation for rating AI outputs on a 1-5 scale, and logging of manual edits surfaced that tacit knowledge systematically, driving iterative improvement, and the same structure was later reused to launch an HR chatbot and an employee-handbook chatbot from insights gathered through employee surveys and shadowing sessions. At a medical coding company, the spine's first application reduced coding time from 25 minutes to 2 seconds per case and cut costs 60% — and when nontechnical spine members challenged AI developers' assumption that poor early performance was a data-volume problem, they discovered the actual cause was inconsistent human-labeled training data (arising from coders' differing medical education levels), a root-cause fix that would likely have gone undiscovered in a purely technical AI center of excellence; the company then built the internal tool into an entirely new externally-sold product verifying insurance billing accuracy. The other 21 organizations studied fell into two weaker structural patterns: 13 built standalone, technology-focused "GenAI units," which concentrate talent well for early exploration but cannot properly assess individual business units' specific needs or coordinate across use cases — illustrated by a Swiss health insurer whose email assistant stalled and became a "zombie use case" after compliance specialists, consulted only after the pilot, revealed regulated text-template requirements the technical team hadn't known to check for; and 8 embedded small, cross-functional "GenAI squads" directly within business units, which move to working pilots faster (better harnessing local business knowledge, facing fewer coordination hurdles) but struggle once their use-case portfolios mature, as illustrated by a Swiss insurer's product-information chatbot that users quickly manipulated into recommending pizza recipes, forcing repeated hardening iterations that consumed the squad's capacity for pursuing new use cases — and because squads are funded and staffed at the business-unit level, well-resourced units pull ahead while others must scale back expectations regardless of a use case's actual potential value, perpetuating exactly the organizational silos gen AI was meant to help dissolve. <span class="src">Create Generative AI Value at Scale</span>

Where MIT SMR's AI-spine research addresses organizational structure, a separate MIT SMR study of 21 large companies (interviewing senior managers across AI, data science, innovation, operations, and sales) addresses staged risk management, proposing that most companies pursuing real gen AI value are deliberately avoiding both extremes of "wait for certainty" and "transform everything at once," instead pursuing what the researchers call "small t" transformation — targeted, risk-calibrated deployment that builds toward larger transformations without betting the business on unproven applications upfront. The researchers organize adoption along a rising "risk slope" with three tiers. At the lowest-risk tier sit individual-task uses common across many roles — writing, synthesis, imagery generation, meeting documentation — where value depends heavily on individual initiative and prompting skill; companies are moving from ungoverned use of public tools (employees adopting ChatGPT or Gemini without asking permission) toward company-specific LLM instances, built-in integration with office productivity suites (creating what one manager called "a super search engine" across emails, transcripts, and documents), and custom integrations trained on proprietary company knowledge — McKinsey's internal tool Lilli, linked to more than 40 internal knowledge sources and specifically engineered to parse the PowerPoint decks the firm communicates through, reached roughly 75% active employee adoption within a year, delivering up to 30% time savings and substantially improved output quality. The middle tier covers specialized gen AI in specific roles and business processes, where tolerance for unacceptable output is lower and human-in-the-loop review remains standard: coding and data science tools (rigorous studies show major productivity gains for engineers using copilot tools), customer-facing support tools that keep a human agent as the final decision-maker (a studied call-center coaching tool improved issues resolved per hour by 14% on average, with a 34% improvement specifically among novice and low-skilled workers; Amazon Pharmacy, Morgan Stanley, and Sysco all deploy variants of this pattern), online content generation (CarMax uses gen AI to summarize customer reviews across more than 5,000 car model pages — work that would take multiple humans 11 years manually — completing the process in hours with an 80% editorial approval rate after fine-tuning), and creative and finance/regulatory processes (Dentsu uses gen AI throughout its creative pipeline from proposal to ideation; Amazon's finance function combines rules-based AI, machine learning, and LLMs across fraud detection, contract review, and tax work). The highest-risk tier is direct customer-facing products and interactions, where gen AI increasingly operates without an employee in the loop at all: John Hancock's chatbot assistants handle common insurance queries to free human agents for complex cases; Tapestry uses real-time language personalization across its e-commerce sites (claiming at least a 3% e-commerce revenue increase from the change); and major software vendors — Lucidchart, Canva, Adobe — have embedded gen AI natively into their product suites, meaning their corporate customers increasingly wait for vendor-delivered features rather than building comparable functionality themselves. The researchers frame the risk-slope logic as explicitly deliberate rather than merely cautious: companies invest first at the low-risk end of the slope both to capture early value and to build organizational capabilities (data quality, back-end system readiness, governance muscle) that partially de-risk the higher-cost, higher-return applications further up the slope. <span class="src">Generate Value From GenAI With ‘Small t’ Transformations</span>

A third framework, HBR's Generative AI Value-Creation Pyramid, reframes the same underlying challenge — how organizations move from incidental to systematic gen AI value — not around risk tolerance but around organizational maturity, proposing four cumulative levels of competence that each build on the one below. The bottom two levels represent "value capture through fast work": individual improvements (enhancing personal productivity and building foundational AI skills for quick, measurable wins — studies cited show customer-service agents resolving issues up to 34% faster, software engineers delivering 26% more code, and data scientists completing tasks 10% faster on average, though the authors caution that even a meaningful one-time individual productivity gain represents what economists Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo call a "so-so technology" when applied only at the individual level across an entire enterprise, since it displaces work without necessarily improving overall competitiveness) and collective intelligence (using gen AI to build shared understanding across a team, treating the tool almost as a specialized colleague with a defined role — illustrated by an insurer's innovation team using gen AI to surface that stalled post-merger work stemmed from unclear stakeholder requirements, not capacity, resolving the actual bottleneck within weeks). The top two levels represent "value creation through slow work": transformation and growth (reimagining core work processes themselves rather than just accelerating them — Cleveland Clinic's AI-assisted documentation frees physicians to engage more fully with patients rather than dividing attention between listening and note-taking, though the authors stress this tier requires careful ethical and safety protocols given the stakes of mission-critical work) and visionary innovation (creating genuinely new markets, products, and business models — a study at one large U.S. R&D lab found gen AI helped scientists discover 44% more materials and file 39% more patents than in a prior comparable period, though 82% of scientists in the same study reported reduced job satisfaction from decreased creative engagement and skill underutilization, a tradeoff the authors say targeted human-skills training can partially or wholly mitigate). The pyramid's authors report that organizations can move from concept to a working transformational prototype in roughly half a day using a structured three-step process: a 60-minute discovery session where cross-functional teams of four to six inventory current gen AI usage and hunt specifically for opportunities at the higher pyramid levels (not just obvious productivity wins); a 30-minute prioritization step evaluating opportunities on potential value and feasibility rather than speed; and a 90-minute build-to-learn session producing a working prototype using existing enterprise gen AI tools, with no need for new technical infrastructure. <span class="src">How to Create Value Systematically with Gen AI</span>

A fourth framework, developed by MIT Sloan Management Review's David Kiron and MIT's Michael Schrage, targets a mechanism cutting across all three of the above frameworks: the discipline that actually makes gen AI value compound over time rather than plateau. The authors' central distinction is between "consumption economics" (treating AI purely as a throughput accelerator — task in, output out, loop closed, which behaves like asset depreciation) and "compounding value" (treating AI as a capability accelerator, where every interaction generates insight that's captured and applied to the next one, behaving like asset appreciation). Two structural economic dynamics make this distinction newly urgent rather than merely old productivity advice restated: first, AI increasingly infers tacit expertise directly from the behavioral traces experts leave behind (legal reasoning embedded in briefs, financial judgment embedded in analyst reports) rather than requiring that expertise to be explicitly codified first — Anthropic's Boris Cherny described watching Claude Code explore his file system on its own to find answers, a capability he had never explicitly programmed, simply inferred from developer behavior traces; second, as tacit expertise becomes machine-readable, the cost of sophisticated capability falls sharply, which (per the century-old Jevons paradox, in which more efficient steam engines increased rather than decreased coal consumption) stimulates more demand and more attempted projects rather than less, creating a self-reinforcing loop. The authors' data shows organizations building systematic human-AI feedback loops are six times more likely to derive substantial financial benefit from AI, and those investing specifically in learning with AI are 73% more likely to see significant financial impact — yet as of 2024, 70% of companies had adopted AI but only 15% were using it for organizational learning, a large and consequential gap. Their proposed mechanism — the "verification-evaluation-learning capture" flywheel — requires three distinct operations to be present and mutually reinforcing, since missing any one collapses the loop back into mere consumption: verification asks "does this output meet the standard?" (a binary check against an existing criterion — fast and cheap, but catches errors without generating new learning on its own); evaluation asks "what does this output reveal?" (requiring genuine domain expertise, since evaluation can generate standards that didn't previously exist, rather than merely checking against ones that did — the authors' central failure mode, "verification masquerading as evaluation," is illustrated by Google engineer Jaana Dogan's experiment pointing rival tool Claude Code at a problem her team had spent months solving: rather than simply accepting or rejecting the AI's comparable solution, she treated it as a starting point to interrogate — "it's not perfect and I'm iterating on it" — a third move only available to someone who already had months of domain judgment to bring to the encounter); and learning capture asks "how do we ensure this insight persists?" (converting a one-time evaluative insight into durable organizational knowledge — a documented standard, an updated prompt template, a shared repository — since evaluation without capture evaporates after each individual interaction; Cherny's own practice at Anthropic layers a CLAUDE.md file that accumulates mistakes, corrections, and design principles directly inside the workflow, so each new coding session inherits what every prior session learned). The authors propose five concrete moves for building this capability at the organizational level: preserving evaluation expertise deliberately rather than letting domain skill atrophy as "AI can do that now" (repositioning experts as evaluators rather than only producers); building minimally viable verification mechanisms calibrated to a domain's actual verification cost (cheap for code, which either runs or doesn't; moderate for finance, which can be backtested; expensive for strategic bets that may not resolve for years — using multi-judge systems and cross-formulation consistency checks where full verification isn't feasible); instituting structured evaluation practices that specifically probe for "what was interestingly wrong" (the failure mode that reveals something about the problem the team hadn't yet named, not just whether the output was simply right or wrong); building lightweight capture systems (decision journals, prompt repositories, evaluation logs) designed for retrievability rather than comprehensiveness; and measuring the cycle itself rather than only the output — tracking how many interactions were verified, evaluated, and captured, and whether captured learning demonstrably changed subsequent practice, rather than relying on consumption metrics like tools adopted or hours saved. <span class="src">How to Reap Compound Benefits From Generative AI</span>

A fifth framework, developed by a team of economists and management researchers writing in HBR, supplies the rigorous testing methodology the other four frameworks implicitly assume organizations can execute: organizational experimentation. The starting diagnosis is the "productivity J-curve" — a well-documented historical pattern (economic historian Paul David traced a roughly 40-year lag before manufacturing plants fully adapted to and optimized around electricity) in which new general-purpose technologies produce an initial productivity dip as organizations struggle to reorganize tasks, skills, and workflows around them, before sustained gains eventually materialize once complementary investments pay off — a pattern the authors argue current gen AI data fits closely, citing a 2025 McKinsey survey in which more than 80% of firms reported no significant earnings impact from gen AI yet, despite rapid adoption. Facing genuine uncertainty about how gen AI will actually pay off, managers confront a real strategic dilemma between waiting for more clarity (risking falling behind competitors) and committing early (risking investment in applications that don't deliver) — and the authors' proposed resolution is treating gen AI adoption not as a single up-front decision but as an ongoing portfolio of organizational experiments, applying the scientific method (a specified, testable hypothesis; a real treatment group and control group; sustained measurement over weeks or months capturing both initial and eventual effects) directly inside live business operations. The authors are explicit that organizational experiments differ meaningfully from both informal pilots (handpicked teams, anecdotal feedback, scaling decisions driven by enthusiasm rather than evidence, no control group, limited generalizability) and standard A/B tests (well suited to fine-tuning discrete product features, but rarely capturing broader effects on coordination, workflow, or worker experience) — organizational experiments specifically evaluate real-world impact and reveal not just whether gen AI works, but how it works, for whom, and under what conditions. Concrete examples illustrate the range of methods available: GitHub and Google ran controlled trials randomly assigning developers to code manually or with AI assistants, finding AI-assisted developers completed tasks 21-55% faster with slightly higher completion rates and reported greater job satisfaction and reduced mental strain; when true randomization proved difficult, a Fortune 500 business-process software company instead staggered rollout of a gen AI assistant to more than 5,000 customer-support agents over time, creating natural control groups, and found productivity rose about 14% overall with a 34% increase specifically among less-experienced agents, alongside improved customer-sentiment scores and retention; and Procter & Gamble ran a "lab in the field" experiment during an innovation hackathon, randomly assigning 776 product developers to work solo or in pairs, with or without AI, finding solo AI users matched the performance of non-AI teams and that both AI-using individuals and teams were better at blending technical and commercial ideas — leading P&G to conclude gen AI could reduce siloed thinking and potentially support smaller cross-functional teams going forward. Beyond isolating causal effects (distinguishing genuine AI impact from confounds like early-adopter skill or motivation) and revealing how impact varies by worker type (one study cited found gen AI copilots deliver large benefits for less-experienced customer-service workers but almost undetectable benefit for more-experienced ones — a finding with direct implications for where to prioritize deployment investment), the authors highlight organizational experiments' role in surfacing implementation hurdles before a full-scale rollout locks in a flawed design, drawing on economist John A. List's framework in The Voltage Effect to name five specific traps experimentation helps avoid: false positives (early wins that turn out to be flukes), audience mismatch (results driven by one especially motivated group rather than a representative one), non-replicable "ingredients" (success dependent on one unique person or context rather than a repeatable process), unintended consequences at scale, and unsustainable cost growth as an initiative expands — the last being especially relevant to gen AI given its nontrivial ongoing technology, talent, and process investment requirements. Siemens' shop-floor gen AI assistant illustrates the full arc: starting with clearly defined hypotheses about productivity and worker well-being (rather than a generic rollout), the company ran its first structured test in 2024 at its experimental factory in Erlangen, Germany, finding the assistant cut the time needed to find repair information and let maintenance technicians handle more incidents independently; contrary to technicians' initial anxiety about job security, the tool actually made them feel more secure once it freed their time for the higher-value work only they could do, and even amid an unrelated team downsizing during the test period, the smaller remaining team maintained stable output — with workers reporting reduced stress even when working through incidents alone, without colleagues available to consult. Siemens has since extended the same experimental discipline to test whether and how the design of new "AI-intensive" jobs affects the quantity and quality of job applicants, and other companies — Microsoft (studying more than 7,000 employees' Copilot adoption across 66 firms with academic partners, finding users saved 1.3-3.6 hours weekly on email though meeting behavior was unchanged) and Grab (studying a gen AI assistant's impact on more than one million entrepreneurs across six countries with Harvard Business School and INSEAD researchers) — are running comparable "ecosystem experiments" that extend the same rigor beyond their own organizational walls to their broader customer and partner base. <span class="src">A Systematic Approach to Experimenting with Gen AI</span>

<span class="src">A Practical Guide to Gaining Value from LLMs</span> (MIT SMR, November 2024) supplies the upstream selection methodology the other five frameworks implicitly assume an organization has already worked through: given the whole space of possible gen AI applications, how do you screen down to the tasks actually worth pursuing? Author Rama Ramakrishnan opens by cataloging LLM failure modes an adoption decision has to weigh honestly — basic logical-reasoning errors ("what is the fifth word of this sentence?" routinely stumps even frontier models), the "reversal curse" (a model that correctly names Tom Cruise's mother fails when the same relationship is asked in reverse), confident hallucination, and guardrails that are trivially circumvented by rephrasing a harmful request through an unrelated frame. Against this backdrop, three techniques exist to adapt an off-the-shelf LLM to a specific task, in ascending order of effort and cost: prompting (simply instructing the model, sufficient when a layperson could perform the task from the same instructions — routing a customer email to the right department, for instance); retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG (grounding responses in up-to-date or proprietary data the model wasn't trained on, which empirically lowers hallucination rates and lets outputs cite their sources for easier human verification); and instruction fine-tuning (updating the model's internal weights using domain-specific examples, the most computationally expensive route, needed for dense-jargon domains like law or medicine, or for tasks too hard to fully specify in a prompt — including "knowledge distillation," where a larger model's outputs train a smaller, cheaper one). Building on this foundation, the article's three-step selection methodology is: first, disaggregate a business process into its discrete component tasks, since automatability varies enormously within a single job (a university's 25 catalogued teaching tasks split sharply between easily LLM-assisted ones like grading and ones, like moderating live discussion, that don't translate to an LLM use case at all); second, evaluate each task against the "generative AI cost equation" — the ongoing cost of using gen AI, plus the upfront cost of adapting it to the task, plus the ongoing cost of detecting and fixing its errors, all weighed against the cost of doing the task without gen AI, with accuracy requirements (ad copy tolerates error; legal or medical tasks demand a costly human-in-the-loop layer) driving how expensive the adaptation and error-correction terms turn out to be; and third, only once a task clears the equation, launch a pilot (commercial vendor application or custom build on a proprietary or open-source model) and build a rigorous, ongoing evaluation process, since LLM output is nondeterministic text rather than a single verifiable number and therefore much harder to automatically grade than a traditional predictive model's output. The article's worked example — writing simple programs — shows a task clearing the equation cheaply: LLMs need no adaptation for common languages given their training on vast public code corpora, and testing/debugging AI-suggested code is already built into normal software workflows, so the only genuinely new cost is the subscription itself, which is why GitHub Copilot reached 10,000 organizations within a year of its 2022 launch. The article closes by noting that GPT-4's own API cost fell 89 percent between March 2023 and August 2024 alone, meaning a task that fails the cost equation today is worth revisiting periodically rather than shelving permanently. <span class="src">A Practical Guide to Gaining Value from LLMs</span>

<span class="src">How to find the right business use cases for generative AI</span> (MIT SMR, June 2025) is a shorter, independently-written report on the same underlying framework — a webinar in which Ramakrishnan presented this methodology roughly seven months after the original article, with a slightly different worked example (a university professor's task list rather than a generic business process) but the identical core recommendation: disaggregate, apply the cost equation, then pilot. It adds two practical notes not emphasized in the original piece: favor narrow use cases over broad ones, since smaller, more targeted LLM deployments cost less and are easier to maintain (particularly for smaller organizations); and deliberately look for gen AI talent outside the traditional data science organization, since prompting competence doesn't require a technical background and "there's talent hiding in the enterprise." <span class="src">How to find the right business use cases for generative AI</span>

A companion cluster of four sources shifts focus from how to structure and stage gen AI adoption to how to measure and execute on the value it's supposed to produce — addressing why, even with all the above frameworks available, most organizations still fail to convert AI activity into P&L impact. BCG's "AI Talk Is Cheap. Value Creation Is Rare" built an outside-in AI-adoption score — combining AI tech, AI talent, and AI deployment sub-scores — and applied it to more than 600 US public companies specifically to cut through both corporate-communications spin and self-reported survey bias; the result found only 6% of firms qualify as genuine adoption leaders, but that group posts industry-adjusted total shareholder returns 9 percentage points above the sample median, and — pushing back on a "hype" reading of AI valuations — the outperformance decomposes almost entirely into revenue growth and margin expansion rather than P/E multiple expansion, meaning the market is pricing in real fundamentals rather than narrative. Leaders pursue value through three paths BCG calls save, scale, and innovate: "doing the same with less" (IBM's AskHR agent handling 94% of employee requests, cutting HR costs 40% over four years) is the least common path among leaders (10%) precisely because pure efficiency gains don't yield durable advantage once the underlying technology is widely available; "expanding what each employee can deliver" (Salesforce service agents freeing four hours weekly for higher-value work, projected to lift upsell revenue 15%) is the dominant path (59%), converting productivity into both margin and revenue growth simultaneously; and "building new products, services, and business models" (Meta's AI-generated ad creative, Moody's new analytics offerings built on proprietary data) is the most ambitious path (21%), requiring a temporary margin trade-off in exchange for revenue streams that don't yet exist. Tracing how leaders got there, BCG finds tech and deployment scores barely change between the "active" and "leading" tiers, while the talent score nearly triples — AI-related skills reach 13% of the workforce at leaders versus 1% at laggards, and dedicated AI positions reach 3.5% versus 0.1% — because tools have commoditized (every company can buy the same models from the same vendors) while the organizational capability to deploy them into a specific business's actual economics cannot be bought, only built. <span class="src">AI Talk Is Cheap. Value Creation Is Rare</span>

BCG's "Look Past Productivity to Get Real Value from AI" diagnoses the same gap from the pilot-design side: its 2026 AI Radar survey found 82% of CEOs are more optimistic about AI ROI than a year earlier, yet only 6% of companies see meaningful value in reduced costs or increased revenue — a gap BCG attributes to three specific pilot failure modes rather than to the technology itself. First, pilots typically automate existing tasks rather than redesigning the process around them, so underlying complexity survives intact — illustrated by a company that used AI to compress a ten-day task into one day, only to have customers still wait the full ten days because the surrounding approval committee and process steps hadn't changed, a fix that only arrived once the company started measuring customer-facing outcomes rather than task speed. Second, efficiency gains get silently reabsorbed into the existing system rather than converted into headcount reduction, throughput increases, or other P&L-visible action. Third, companies commonly measure activity (hours saved, tasks automated) rather than explicit financial value, leaving productivity gains theoretical until explicitly linked to reduced vendor spend, higher throughput, or removed organizational capacity. The companies BCG finds winning go deep rather than broad: they prioritize aggressively (one manufacturer rejected an AI use case for production waste specifically because the waste represented under 1% of cost of goods sold — technically feasible but economically immaterial) and redesign organizational elements end-to-end (spans of control, management layers, decision rights) rather than layering AI onto an unchanged operating model, with concrete results including a consumer company generating €250 million in savings by redesigning marketing workflows and a technology company targeting a 30% opex reduction on a $15 billion cost base by automating more than a million back-office activities. <span class="src">Look Past Productivity to Get Real Value from AI</span>

A more skeptical companion piece in the *Financial Times* supplies independent empirical grounding for the same productivity-versus-value gap, drawing on an MIT study (Mert Demirer and co-authors) that tracked software developers' output at multiple funnel stages before and after AI adoption. The study found an explosive 300% increase in files created or edited at the top of the funnel shrank to a 150% increase in discrete work actually submitted for review, and shrank further, fivefold, to just a roughly 30% uplift in full software releases — with no corresponding increase in mobile app downloads despite a marked rise in app releases, indicating most of the additional output failed to find an audience. The FT's John Burn-Murdoch reads this not as evidence AI's value is illusory, but as evidence that current organizational structures and workflows aren't yet built to capture gains that a technology as capability-dense as AI can generate — drawing a direct historical parallel to early electrification, where factories that simply swapped giant steam engines for giant electric motors saw only modest productivity gains, with the real boom arriving decades later once engineers redesigned individual workstations around small, distributed motors instead. The same dynamic, he argues, may explain why AI-native companies like Anthropic and OpenAI are seeing explosive usage and productivity growth while incumbents layering AI onto legacy workflows see far more modest gains — the tools are equally powerful, but the surrounding structures are not equally suited to exploit them. <span class="src">How much value is AI really creating?</span>

BCG's "CEOs Are Starting to See Value from AI. Now Comes Execution." supplies the sharpest empirical account of the specific execution gap the above three pieces describe conceptually, comparing CEOs at high-performing companies (at least 10% cost reduction or 5% revenue growth attributable to AI) against low performers in the same survey. The gap is not primarily technical: CEOs cite unclear links between AI initiatives and financial outcomes, and the difficulty of redesigning workflows, roles, and incentives, far more often than model performance or data architecture — yet despite widespread awareness of these barriers, few CEOs act on them: only 14% clearly define the P&L impact of every AI initiative, and fewer than a third fund the process and skills redesign work they say is necessary. BCG's four differentiating moves, each with a quantified performance gap, are: making the CEO the orchestrator of AI strategy while holding CXOs and P&L owners individually accountable for outcomes (high performers are twice as likely to have restructured accountability as part of their AI program); concentrating AI investment in a small number of high-value, end-to-end business domains rather than scattering pilots everywhere (high performers are roughly seven times more likely to redesign workflows end-to-end, and 64% of CEOs report pursuing pilots but only 26% embed AI into a genuine business transformation); defining an explicit, revisable value path — expected P&L impact, baseline, KPIs, and owner — before each initiative launches, with finance enforcing accountability from day one (high performers are roughly five times more likely to define a P&L line for every initiative, and 1.4 times more likely to have finance enforce accountability); and prioritizing people and change management on the understanding that just 10% of AI's value traces to algorithms, 20% to data, and the remaining 70% to changes in the operating model and new ways of working (high performers are 2.4 times more likely to assign top talent to AI workstreams and twice as likely to fully fund people, leadership, and change work). <span class="src">CEOs Are Starting to See Value from AI. Now Comes Execution.</span>

MIT SMR's "Three Approaches to Measuring and Managing AI ROI" completes this companion cluster by addressing a problem the other frameworks above largely take for granted — how to actually measure AI ROI once value-creating initiatives are underway — based on interviews with more than 30 CEOs and senior leaders. The authors find companies cluster into three maturity stages, most moving through all three sequentially rather than starting at the top. The function-focused approach concentrates deployment in one or a few business functions with tailored, function-specific metrics (shorter response times, fewer errors, reduced unit cost), producing credible, easy-to-defend proof points — illustrated by Unilever's AI-based candidate-assessment system, which reduced recruiter reliance and shortened time to hire — but these successes resist comparison or aggregation as adoption spreads, since each function defines "return" differently. The coordinated approach manages a growing portfolio of function-specific deployments alongside shared, general-purpose AI platforms touching multiple teams — illustrated by JPMorgan Chase's LLM Suite, deployed to more than 200,000 employees across legal, research, and operations, and Amazon's internally-reused "AI flywheel" of machine-learning capabilities — with the central challenge being coordination without standardization collapsing into an incomparable patchwork of ad hoc ROI methods. The enterprise portfolio approach, the highest maturity stage, treats every AI initiative like any other capital investment, governed through capital-project-style forums with formal business cases, NPV, and IRR — as at Morgan Stanley, which evaluates every use case against a structured real-world criteria framework before deployment — though the authors caution that fully enforced financial discipline can kill breakthrough bets too early if it ignores indirect benefits, delayed adoption curves, or learning-driven value creation, recommending lighter-touch tracking for early-stage experiments and heavier scrutiny only as initiatives scale. <span class="src">Three Approaches to Measuring and Managing AI ROI</span>

<span class="src">6 ways businesses can leverage generative AI</span> (MIT Sloan, January 2025) offers a practitioner-panel complement to the research frameworks above. Panelists stressed building a modern digital core before chasing use cases, grounding AI in maintenance logs and live production data (Corning), hiring for curiosity and healthy skepticism, and the point that AI models remain probabilistic and cannot be expected to model a problem perfectly. <span class="src">6 ways businesses can leverage generative AI</span>

<span class="src">The Formula for Agentic AI Value (BCG)</span> (September 2026) is the survey-scale counterpart to the case-based frameworks above. It argues the value formula is strategic clarity (a few high-value plays reshaped end to end, C-suite ownership, P&L tracking) plus applied AI built on an AI-first operating model with agent controls, a workforce reinvented around humans and agents, and an agent-ready data platform. Future-built companies run 20 AI workflows at scale against four for laggards, spend 62% of initiatives on reshaping and inventing processes rather than deploying tools, reach P&L impact in about 11 months (against 17 for laggards) and are more likely to make explicit decisions about token spending. On workforce, 55% of future-built companies do strategic workforce planning (17% of laggards), 89% of respondents expect AI to generate new work rather than mainly replace jobs, yet companies expect a 10-15% workforce reduction concentrated in middle management and frontline leadership. BCG warns of "cognitive lock-in" and recommends keeping the enterprise's proprietary knowledge, ontology and decision logic in a portable layer under its control, measuring cost per successful outcome rather than per token. Examples include an insurer that rebuilt workflows as agentic-first and a health insurer whose agentic chatbots handle about 60% of call types and cut handling time 35%. <span class="src">The Formula for Agentic AI Value (BCG)</span>

## Open Questions

- None of the five sources directly compares its framework against the others, so it's the vault's own synthesis (not the original authors') that these five are complementary rather than competing — a reader adopting the AI spine's organizational structure, the small-t risk slope's staged rollout, the value-creation pyramid's maturity tracking, the verify-evaluate-capture flywheel's learning discipline, and organizational experiments' testing rigor simultaneously has not been tested as a combined system by any of these five research teams.
- The AI-spine research's evidence base is explicitly small (2 of 23 companies achieved the outcome being studied), and the organizational-experiments research doesn't report how many of the companies it studied ultimately achieved compounding, sustained value versus a one-time experimental result — it's unclear how durable any of these frameworks' reported gains are beyond the specific study window.
- The panel claims, including the estimate of a billion Salesforce agents within a year, are executive statements without supporting data in the source.
- BCG's index relies on self-reported executive estimates, and the report itself notes that figures are expected future impact unless stated as realized and may reflect perception bias; the correlations between maturity and financial results do not establish causation.
