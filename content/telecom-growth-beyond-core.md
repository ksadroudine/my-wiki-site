## Summary

Core connectivity has become table stakes with slowing growth, so telecom operators are looking to adjacent revenue in consumer ecosystems, enterprise ICT, network APIs, private 5G, sustainability and AI infrastructure. Evidence from McKinsey and Accenture shows that business customers already grant operators a right to play beyond connectivity, with security and increasingly agentic AI driving demand, but operators have not yet converted that preference into delivery. Capturing the opportunity depends on platform-led operating models, vertical partnerships, interoperable standards and, for AI infrastructure, a choice among fiber, intelligent network services, data-center space and GPU-as-a-service plays that carry different risks.

## Concept

Telecom operators occupy a structurally advantaged position for growth beyond connectivity. Their central role in the digital economy gives them real-time visibility into network usage, device behavior, location and consumption at a scale few industries match, and together with trusted customer relationships and ubiquitous infrastructure this lets them shift from selling discrete connectivity products to delivering integrated, value-added services. The incentive to do so is strong, because the traditional engines of consumer growth have stalled. Unlimited voice and data plans have exhausted upsell opportunities, fiber is commoditizing what was once a premium gigabit product, fixed-mobile convergence has passed 50% penetration in markets such as Belgium, Portugal and Spain, and headline price increases retain less than half their value once customers migrate to cheaper front-book offers. Core connectivity has become table stakes, with growth projected below 5% annually by 2026, while beyond-core domains such as security, cloud and agentic AI grow two to three times faster. <span class="src">The critical bets on the future of telco value creation</span> <span class="src">B2C growth beyond the core</span> <span class="src">Playing to win in B2B telecom</span> <span class="src">Winning B2B customers in technology and telecommunications</span>

On the consumer side, growth takes the form of vertical ecosystems that bundle adjacent, often third-party services such as security, insurance, energy, entertainment, fintech and health around the connectivity relationship. McKinsey sizes the integrated network economy at roughly USD 125 trillion globally by 2035, with adjacent domains such as housing at about USD 7.4 trillion, health USD 7.9 trillion, digital content USD 4.8 trillion, mobility USD 3.0 trillion, education USD 5.5 trillion and marketplaces USD 12.1 trillion, and about 70% of consumers already say they would buy at least one adjacent service from their operator. The revenue is already real. NTT Docomo earns more than 20% of revenue beyond its core, Telus draws a quarter of revenue from B2B digital verticals such as healthcare, agriculture and back-office solutions, Spain's MasOrange built an app-controlled energy service later sold for EUR 90 million, an insurance business targeting more than 7.5 million policies and over EUR 1.5 billion in premium volume, and a cobranded home-security business with ADT, and Movistar's home-security venture with Prosegur gained more than 400,000 customers in its first year. Leading operators now derive 5-40% of revenue, roughly EUR 1 billion to EUR 15 billion a year, from adjacencies such as fintech, insurance, digital media, energy and security. The models need limited capital because they use existing retail, billing, care and field assets, they produce high-margin revenue, and they deepen the core relationship, since customers with several bundled services churn less, a pattern investors reward with higher multiples for integrated telcos. <span class="src">Mapping the routes to renewed success for telcos</span> <span class="src">The critical bets on the future of telco value creation</span> <span class="src">B2C growth beyond the core</span>

Sustainability is a less obvious consumer-facing adjacency. Operators' connectivity assets, proprietary consumer data, retail footprint, field technicians and in-home relationships position them to offer 5G sustainability applications, net-zero home solutions and green cloud services, tapping USD 9-12 trillion of value expected from the net-zero transition and consumers' stated willingness to pay a 15-30% premium for sustainably produced services, while also helping with talent attraction and investor relations. <span class="src">Mapping the routes to renewed success for telcos</span>

On the enterprise side the growth paths are ICT services, network APIs and private 5G. B2B ICT, extending connectivity into cloud, cybersecurity and managed IT services, can reach as much as 20% of a telco's revenue, and one European operator more than doubled its cloud-related revenue and earnings within four years by upgrading infrastructure, upskilling staff and forming key partnerships. Network APIs are a 5G-linked opportunity, exposing standardized capabilities such as speed on demand, low latency and edge-compute discovery to enterprise developers who build applications such as fraud prevention, glitch-free videoconferencing and remotely operated industrial equipment that need guaranteed rather than best-effort performance. McKinsey estimates USD 100-300 billion of connectivity and edge-computing revenue over five to seven years plus USD 10-30 billion from the APIs themselves, and TIM, Claro and Vivo already provide banks with mobile-number verification, SIM-swap detection and device-location checks through the GSMA Open Gateway framework, reaching about 150 million customers, with the SIM-swap API alone generating USD 9 million. Because an API usable on only one operator's footprint is far less valuable, the opportunity requires cooperation among competitors, as in the joint venture of Deutsche Telekom, AT&T, Orange, Singtel, Telstra and Verizon with Ericsson to sell interoperable APIs globally. Private 5G networks, customized architectures for uses such as smart hospitals, connected transport and augmented retail, are a third path that T-Mobile, O2, Vodafone and KPN target at logistics, manufacturing and public-sector customers. <span class="src">Mapping the routes to renewed success for telcos</span> <span class="src">The critical bets on the future of telco value creation</span>

Past beyond-core experimentation often produced scattered, underperforming side bets, so the sources stress what commercial excellence requires. The primary remaining barrier is organizational and operational, because structures built for scale, reliability and cost efficiency lack the speed, experimentation and customer-centricity ecosystem plays demand. Success needs a platform-led operating model with real structure, process, people and technology investment, P&L accountability linked to the core business and shared incentives, and, once early results validate a vertical, a platform-first value proposition built on orchestration, integration and bundling rather than standalone products. Operators also need partnerships with vertical leaders such as insurers, security firms and energy companies, and an internal integration platform that makes bundling and managing non-core services routine. ICT growth requires a modular platform for full-stack solutions and a specialized enterprise salesforce, network APIs need common application-layer standards and integration with hyperscaler platforms, and private 5G needs enterprise-grade spectrum access, often developed with industrial or hyperscaler partners. <span class="src">B2C growth beyond the core</span> <span class="src">The critical bets on the future of telco value creation</span>

Demand-side evidence shows that business customers already accept this ambition. McKinsey's B2B pulse surveys, published in February 2025, June 2025 and February 2026, consistently find that roughly 80% of decision-makers see operators as viable partners beyond core connectivity, with beyond-core spending growing two to three times faster than core. The first edition framed four strategic plays, namely a connectivity play that refocuses the core on near-core products such as SD-WAN, an ICT play that bundles cybersecurity and cloud, a 5G network API play, and an ecosystem-building play that creates standalone businesses in verticals such as healthcare or automotive, as with Telstra Health and Vodafone Automotive, and estimated their combined potential at USD 160 billion of additional revenue by 2028. The second edition found cybersecurity to be the most compelling B2B priority across regions and company sizes and the leading reason customers switch providers, ahead of brand, product capability and price, and recorded a shift to digital buying, with fully digital preference expected to nearly double within twelve months and end-to-end digital transactions above EUR 1 million tripling since 2022, while about one in four customers planned to switch vendors within a year, mainly to cloud providers and tech manufacturers. Omnichannel engagement moved from a nice-to-have to a baseline expectation, and stickiness toward operators is stabilizing but increasingly conditional on trust and security. <span class="src">Playing to win in B2B telecom</span> <span class="src">B2B customers have spoken. Will telcos listen?</span> <span class="src">Winning B2B customers in technology and telecommunications</span>

The latest edition adds agentic AI as the main new variable. Security and agentic AI are now the fastest-growing spending domains, each expanding above 9% a year while core connectivity growth slows below 5%, and agentic AI maturity correlates directly with willingness to transact digitally, with fully deployed enterprises reporting digital ticket sizes about twice those of early pilots and each step up the maturity curve linked to a 20-45% increase in the value customers will transact end to end online. Data privacy and sovereignty have become a gating factor rather than a differentiator as agents gain broader access to enterprise data, with more than half of respondents naming it their top concern and nearly two-thirds requiring private cloud or on-premises deployment. Although 98% of customers are open to AI-assisted service in at least one interaction, only 3-11% report full-scale deployment for any use case, even in customer care, and no provider type, whether foundation-model vendors, system integrators or operators, has established a dominant position, so the opening for operators is real but unproven. <span class="src">Winning B2B customers in technology and telecommunications</span>

Accenture's independent survey of more than 1,200 enterprise leaders corroborates the right-to-play finding while sharpening the delivery gap. It finds that 73% of enterprises rank telcos among their top three providers for integrated B2B solutions, over 70% prefer telco partnerships for local and connected AI data centers because of data proximity, security posture and existing network infrastructure, and 60% are interested in working with telcos on AI agents and industry-tailored AI-as-a-service. Yet nearly half report dissatisfaction with telcos' connectivity and digital and IT delivery, citing self-service network provisioning, standardized configuration APIs and total cost of ownership, so the binding constraint is operators' delivery rather than customer demand. Accenture's prescription is to build a connected infrastructure platform that integrates connectivity, cloud, edge and AI into one continuum, to move to an outcome-based, partner-driven model that balances direct sales with ecosystem engagement, and to redesign the operating and talent model to match the new portfolio. <span class="src">Reinventing B2B for the AI economy</span>

The AI economy also opens a supply-side role for operators, not merely connecting users to AI services but building and monetizing the fiber, space, power and GPU capacity that AI workloads need. Global data-center demand is projected to more than triple by 2030, and operators' geographic reach and experience running large networks under variable demand map onto it, while inaction risks leaving operators further behind hyperscalers and new entrants that captured most of the value from the last decade's growth in data consumption while telco revenues stayed flat. <span class="src">AI infrastructure — A new growth avenue for telco operators</span>

McKinsey describes four paths that operators can pursue singly or together depending on market structure, assets and risk appetite. Connecting new data centers with fiber is a USD 30-50 billion global market, since colocation providers and hyperscalers plan more than 2,600 new data centers by the early 2030s, and in much of Europe and Asia-Pacific a telco license is required to lay fiber, which gives incumbents first-mover choice over which markets to serve directly and which to lease to hyperscalers that often prefer leasing dark fiber from several providers for redundancy and speed. Intelligent network services, a next generation of software-defined networking, help enterprises manage the USD 70-80 billion a year in cloud data-egress fees and could let operators move from declining usage-based B2B wireline pricing toward value-based pricing, but the market is too young to have a settled feature set or business model, so it requires market research and decisions on building versus partnering before significant commitment. <span class="src">AI infrastructure — A new growth avenue for telco operators</span>

The other two paths trade risk against capital. Converting unused data-center or central-office space and power into revenue through colocation, sale-leaseback or revenue sharing is the lowest-risk entry, because new data-center construction takes five or more years and many power grids are at capacity, giving operators with underused assets a multi-year window with little new investment, provided a site offers at least about 500 kilowatts of power and can be retrofitted for the density and liquid cooling that AI racks need, ideally only after a firm tenant commitment. GPU-as-a-service, renting GPUs by the hour or on reserved terms for training, inference or sovereign-AI use, addresses a USD 35-70 billion market by 2030 excluding hyperscaler share, with projected returns on invested capital of 6-14% depending on the asset base and local power cost, but it is the most capital-intensive and risky path because of uncertain demand, hyperscaler competition, fast technology cycles and possible GPU price declines, so a modest initial investment focused on anchor tenants and partners is recommended, with sovereign-AI demand a favorable tailwind in supportive markets. <span class="src">AI infrastructure — A new growth avenue for telco operators</span>

Whichever paths operators choose, capturing the AI-infrastructure opportunity requires four operating-model shifts. Sales needs a dedicated organization for hyperscaler-facing fiber, space and power deals, which demand fast design turnaround and regular cost analysis, and an advisory-led approach with education, feasibility assessment and proof-of-concept support for enterprise GPU-as-a-service. Partnerships with chipmakers, data-center providers, platform vendors, integrators, power suppliers and retrofit contractors supply full-stack capability faster than building it alone. Financial evaluation needs nontraditional methods, such as assessing a fiber route's cumulative future demand across several data centers instead of one customer's return or negotiating with colocation providers for fewer, higher-volume connectivity partners, and GPU-ready facilities should be assessed on compute, power, cooling, networking and memory. Finally, operators should communicate their AI-value-chain strategy proactively to investors, customers and analysts before competitors define the narrative. <span class="src">AI infrastructure — A new growth avenue for telco operators</span>
