## Summary

An economic framing of AI as a technology that makes prediction cheap, which raises the value of its complements — data, judgment, action — while reducing the value of substitutes like human prediction. Requires decomposing any decision into prediction, judgment, and action to see where automation actually makes sense.

## Highlights

- Current-generation AI is best understood not as "intelligence" in the broad sense but as a dramatic drop in the cost of prediction, defined as the process of using data you have to fill in information you don't have — a definition that covers forecasting the future as well as classifying present or past states (fraud, malignancy, identity). <span class="src">Prediction Machines</span>
- Standard economics of falling input costs applies directly: as prediction gets cheaper, more of it gets used, and its "complements" — the things prediction is combined with, namely data, judgment, and action — become more valuable, while its substitute, human-generated prediction, becomes less valuable. <span class="src">Prediction Machines</span>
- A useful taxonomy for deciding the human/machine division of labor is known knowns (rich data, machine prediction works well), known unknowns (too little data, prediction machines and humans both know they can't predict well), unknown unknowns (rare events outside all past experience, where both humans and machines fail), and unknown knowns (a past correlation looks strong but is actually driven by an unobserved factor, so the machine is confidently wrong). <span class="src">Prediction Machines</span>
- A decision decomposes into prediction (what is likely to happen), judgment (the relative value assigned to different possible outcomes and errors), and action; a prediction machine only replaces the first component, so lowering the cost of prediction raises the value of human judgment rather than eliminating the need for it. <span class="src">Prediction Machines</span>
- Reward function engineering — pre-specifying judgment about payoffs so a machine can act on a prediction without a human in the loop — becomes worthwhile only when the number of plausible action-situation combinations is small enough to code up in advance; when it's too large or the situations too rare, it remains more efficient for a human to apply judgment after the machine predicts. <span class="src">Prediction Machines</span>
- Human-machine collaboration frequently takes the form of "prediction by exception": the machine handles the high-frequency, well-understood cases at scale, and hands off only the unusual cases it flags with low confidence to a human, since machines scale but struggle with rare events while humans don't scale but can reason from thin data using cognitive models. <span class="src">Prediction Machines</span>
- The AI canvas decomposes a task into seven questions — action, prediction, judgment, outcome, input data, training data, and feedback data — as a discipline for identifying where a prediction machine can profitably be inserted into an existing work flow, and companies should rank-order candidate AI tools by expected ROI across their full task list rather than adopting AI opportunistically. <span class="src">Prediction Machines</span>
- AI tools change jobs in four ways rather than one: they can augment a job (new complementary tasks appear), contract a job (some tasks are automated away), reconstitute a job (some tasks removed, others added), or shift the relative value of the skills a job requires — meaning full automation of a task does not necessarily mean automation of the surrounding job. <span class="src">Prediction Machines</span>

## Concept

Prediction Machines argues that the current wave of AI advances should be understood economically rather than mystically: what has gotten dramatically cheaper is not "intelligence" in general but prediction specifically, defined broadly as the use of available data to generate information that is currently missing — which covers forecasting future events as well as classifying uncertain present or past states such as whether a transaction is fraudulent or an image shows a malignant tumor. Applying ordinary economics to this cost drop yields the book's central prediction: as the price of prediction falls, more of it will be consumed, and it will show up in unexpected new places, following the standard logic that a cheaper input raises the value of its complements (things combined with it — for prediction, the complements are data, judgment, and action) while depressing the value of its substitutes (human-generated prediction). <span class="src">Prediction Machines</span>

To decide where machine prediction actually works well versus where it fails, the book adapts Donald Rumsfeld's known/unknown framing into four categories. Known knowns describe situations with rich historical data, where machine prediction performs well and both humans and the system know the prediction is trustworthy. Known unknowns describe situations with too little data, where both humans and machines correctly recognize that prediction will be unreliable — this is where humans retain an edge, since people can often make rough predictions from very little data using intuitive cognitive models the way machines cannot. Unknown unknowns describe genuinely novel events outside all past experience, where neither humans nor machines can predict reliably, though humans at least recognize their own uncertainty. Unknown knowns are the most dangerous category: a correlation that looked strong in historical data is actually driven by some unobserved factor that later changes, so the machine's prediction appears confident and precise while being systematically wrong, and unlike the known-unknowns case, neither the machine nor an unwary human user has any signal that the prediction should be doubted. <span class="src">Prediction Machines</span>

The book's most consequential conceptual move is decomposing any decision into three distinct components — prediction (what will happen), judgment (the relative payoff or cost assigned to each possible outcome and each possible error), and action (what is actually done) — plus the surrounding data used for training, input, and feedback. Because a prediction machine only replaces the prediction component, its widespread deployment does not eliminate the need for human judgment; it increases the value of judgment, since better and cheaper predictions make it worthwhile to apply careful judgment to a much larger number of decisions than before, including decisions previously left to defaults because judging them wasn't worth the effort. Judgment can itself sometimes be transferred to a machine in advance — a process the authors call reward function engineering, where a human specifies in code how to value different outcomes so the machine can act autonomously on its own prediction — but this only pays off when the space of plausible action-situation combinations is small enough to fully pre-specify; when combinations are too numerous or situations too rare, it remains more efficient to keep a human applying judgment after the prediction is generated rather than trying to automate that judgment away. <span class="src">Prediction Machines</span>

This logic produces a recurring collaboration pattern the book calls "prediction by exception": because machine prediction scales in a way human prediction cannot, but machines struggle precisely where data is thin (rare or unprecedented cases), the efficient division of labor typically has the machine handle the high-volume, well-understood majority of cases automatically while flagging low-confidence or unusual cases for a human to review — the redaction tool example given lets a user set the machine's aggressiveness and routes edge cases to human judgment rather than trying to force full automation everywhere. To operationalize this at the level of a specific business task, the authors propose an "AI canvas," structured around seven questions (what action are you trying to enable; what do you need to predict to make the decision; how do you value different outcomes and errors; what are your success metrics; what input, training, and feedback data does the algorithm need) as a discipline for locating exactly where a prediction machine can be profitably inserted into an existing work flow — and recommend ranking candidate AI investments across an entire organization's work flows by expected ROI rather than adopting AI tools opportunistically task by task. Because automating a task's prediction component doesn't necessarily automate the surrounding job, the book identifies four distinct effects AI can have on jobs: augmenting a job by freeing time for previously underemphasized tasks, contracting a job by directly eliminating some of its tasks, reconstituting a job by removing some tasks while adding new ones, or simply shifting which skills are most valuable within an otherwise unchanged job — meaning the effect of AI on employment is better analyzed at the level of individual tasks within a work flow than at the level of whole jobs or occupations. <span class="src">Prediction Machines</span>

This job-level anxiety about AI-driven change is a documented worker-side phenomenon in its own right, not merely a theoretical possibility: a companion source on how technology reshapes work describes the specific psychological and practical anxieties workers experience as automation and AI reach into their jobs, providing a worker's-eye-view counterpart to this concept's more employer-side framing of job augmentation, contraction, and reconstitution. <span class="src">Your Job and How Technology Will Change It</span>
