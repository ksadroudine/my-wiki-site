## Summary

Generative AI can strengthen creative ideation when people treat it as a creative partner rather than a faster search box, but its effects on human capability are mixed. Research on prompting and on persistence and flexibility shows how to extract original and appropriate ideas, and product-development cases show that the right level of trust and oversight differs by stage, while studies of creative work find that AI raises individual quality yet narrows the diversity of ideas across groups. Experiments also show that AI can flatten skill gaps for conceptual work but hits an AI wall where domain expertise is needed to evaluate and refine its output, so managers should sequence human and AI contributions deliberately, allow time for original thinking and keep the diversity that innovation depends on.

## Concept

HBR's article on training the brain to work creatively with generative AI diagnoses a failure in how most people already use it. Search tools such as Google train users to ask direct questions for a single filtered best answer, and that linear habit carries into AI prompting, so predictable, incremental output comes from a technology capable of much more. The prescription is a mindshift to treating AI as a creative collaborator, illustrated by asking for dinner recipes from available ingredients and then pushing toward unconventional constraints such as in the style of a celebrity chef from any century. It offers twelve exercises, namely daily open-ended exploratory prompting, reframing questions as what-if and how-might-we prompts, embracing ambiguity, using prompts to explore before solving, chaining prompts iteratively, thinking metaphorically or by analogy, requesting several perspectives, such as an artist's, scientist's and philosopher's, on one problem at once, using role-play prompts that invoke named experts or fictional personas, asking for outright impossibilities to reframe a problem, treating the AI as a design partner, holding a weekly future-oriented prompting session and keeping a journal of unusually valuable breakthrough prompts. <span class="src">Train Your Brain to Work Creatively with Gen AI</span>

A later HBR article supplies the research grounding. It defines ideation quality on two axes, originality, how far an idea deviates from what exists, and appropriateness, whether it is feasible, and maps two classic pathways to human creativity onto LLMs. Persistence is focused, exhaustive generation of variations within a narrow space, which LLMs achieve through tireless productivity, and cited research shows that the originality of a generated pool rises with volume up to a plateau, and flexibility is the combination of distant concepts, which LLMs achieve through the semantic breadth of their training data. Because models are trained to predict the statistically most likely continuation, their unguided default is familiar rather than novel territory, the opposite of what ideation needs, so the article catalogs counteracting techniques, persona modifiers, hybrid prompting that combines many varied smaller prompts, chain-of-thought prompting and higher temperature settings for originality, and fine-tuning on brand-specific content, few-shot prompting with curated examples and retrieval-augmented generation for appropriateness. Both articles treat AI personas that invoke a named expert, historical figure or archetype as one of the highest-leverage ways to escape the most-likely-token default. <span class="src">When Used Correctly, LLMs Can Unlock More Creative Ideas</span> <span class="src">Train Your Brain to Work Creatively with Gen AI</span>

The same article proposes ideation roles for an LLM. As lead ideator it can be a Designer, versioning content variants, running multivariate tests and surfacing hidden confounding variables, or a Writer, sharpening how an idea is communicated, and as thought partner it can be an Interviewer, using Socratic questions to surface blind spots, or an Actor, roughly simulating customer responses, although such personas risk skewing toward dominant online voices rather than representing all groups, a limitation the authors connect to an HBR piece on synthetic personas in market research. Two cautions follow. While AI assistance can make individual ideators more original, it can make groups of AI-assisted ideators converge on similar ideas, because shared prompting patterns and training data pull output toward the same statistical center, so organizations that want differentiation must counteract convergence, for example with hybrid prompting and persona variation, instead of assuming that individual gains add up to group originality, and current LLMs consistently produce more small, incremental ideas than breakthrough ones, so AI should lead when the goal is broad exploration or volume and humans should lead when it is a few bold, high-impact ideas. In one adoption survey idea generation and brainstorming was the fastest-growing enterprise use between 2024 and 2025, rising nine positions to rank among the top five. <span class="src">When Used Correctly, LLMs Can Unlock More Creative Ideas</span>

A field study of four companies shows how the same ideation techniques play out across product development. Boston design agency Loft used GPT-4 to suggest features from known customer preferences and Midjourney to iterate visual concepts from designers' sketches, deliberately tolerating hallucination because the goal was idea volume, which illustrates persistence, then paused generative AI during engineering and prototyping, where reliable output is essential, and reintroduced it to transcribe and analyze consumer focus-group feedback on physical prototypes, avoiding open-ended questions that might invite hallucination. It estimates that the combination halved its product development time and used it to generate 50 concept variations of a guitar toy that would once have taken many hours of sketching. Czech innovation firm Creative Dock fine-tunes pretrained LLMs on proprietary market-research data into agents that simulate qualitative customer interviews with specific personas, letting teams test value propositions long before a real customer is involved, and reports a 30% technical-development efficiency gain, a 40% graphic-design efficiency gain, tripled content-creation speed and 50% annual growth without adding headcount. <span class="src">When Generative AI Meets Product Development</span>

The other two companies apply fine-tuning to higher-precision domains. Swiss startup FlecheTech fine-tunes a pretrained LLM on a database of printed-circuit-board designs so that non-engineers can describe a custom circuit in plain language, cutting the six-to-eight-week timeline of a human designer by more than 80%, with the largest gains for novices. Siemens pairs its Simcenter engineering-simulation software, which normally demands long training and specialist expertise, with a natural-language front end, HiSimcenter, so that many more engineers can set up and interpret complex physics simulations, raising modeling efficiency by more than 50%, and found that a reliable ground truth was the central bottleneck, so it trained selected employees to assess training-data quality across major engineering tasks before the data feeds the model. Together the cases show that the right trust and oversight vary by stage, since pure ideation and design tolerate and even benefit from hallucination because value lies in volume and unexpected combination, customer-insight and concept-validation tools need fine-tuning on proprietary data for contextual reliability, and natural-language interfaces to precision systems demand the highest data discipline because an incorrect simulation carries physical or financial consequences rather than being a discarded idea. <span class="src">When Generative AI Meets Product Development</span>

The costs to human capability are the other side. As employees lean on ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot to write and code faster, they face a trade-off between originality and effort, since AI output mimics the fluency of human work but lacks a person's stylistic choices and original thinking, and making it reflect an individual's voice and expertise needs iterative, time-consuming prompt refinement that many users skip once a first draft looks good enough. Engineers show the stakes, since they aim for elegant, original solutions shaped by their experience with object architecture, naming conventions and testing, yet Copilot makes it easy to accept large blocks of generated code under deadline, saving time now and quietly raising future costs, because people often struggle to review and adapt AI-generated code, sometimes making a rewrite more efficient than correction, and a 2023 study found that LLM-based coding tools measurably diminished code quality and long-term maintainability. <span class="src">Does GenAI Impose a Creativity Tax</span>

A mathematical model of human-AI interaction formalizes the risk, finding that as people balance optimal, personalized output against working efficiently, diversity of thought and creativity erode, since defaulting to unmodified tool output yields more homogeneous content than people working independently, as emails written by the same assistant would start to sound alike, at a cost to the originality and diversity that drive growth. The risk compounds through a feedback loop in which AI-generated content enters the training data of future models, both proprietary organizational content and the public internet, so that some researchers warn of a homogenization death spiral in which AI content displaces human content, potentially degrading model quality or causing model collapse. The cost can seem trivial against the efficiency gains, but the research argues that this understates the risk, because generative AI, unlike earlier labor-saving shifts such as automation and offshoring, operates directly in the realm of ideas and content. Its mitigations are managerial, rethinking productivity expectations task by task and allowing more time where a task needs original human thought, making AI easier to guide, amend and correct through retrieval-augmented generation for accuracy and comprehensive prompt-engineering training, and having managers encourage employees to express their distinct perspectives so that AI enriches work instead of eroding individuality. <span class="src">Does GenAI Impose a Creativity Tax</span>

An experiment tests whether AI can close the gap between novices and experts, the premise on which many organizations deploy it. Researchers from Stanford and Harvard Business School's Digital Data Design Institute studied 78 employees at the UK fintech IG Group, divided by expertise distance from a writing task into experts, professional writers who regularly drafted website articles, adjacent outsiders, marketing specialists with general familiarity, and distant outsiders, developers and data scientists with no writing background, each of whom conceptualized and wrote articles with and without gen AI, blind-scored by IG executives from 1 to 5. Without AI, conceptualization showed the expected hierarchy, with writers at 3.82, marketers at 3.04 and technologists at 3.02, and with AI the gap nearly vanished, as writers reached 4.12, marketers 4.18, slightly above AI-assisted writers, and technologists 4.05, so all three assisted groups beat unassisted writers, which suggests that AI can flatten hierarchies in work that needs less specialized judgment, since conceptualizing is mainly judging whether a topic idea is good, which one participant likened to imagining running a marathon rather than running it. <span class="src">Gen AI Won’t Make Your Employees Experts</span>

Execution told a different story. With AI, writers scored 3.96 and marketers nearly matched them at 3.92, but technologists scored 3.42 with AI and 3.38 without, so AI gave them essentially no benefit in writing, though it had helped them most in conceptualizing. Interviews showed that the further a worker's expertise lay from what a task requires, the less AI closed the gap, since marketers understood writers' professional language and had enough domain knowledge to refine generated text while technologists lacked the intuition to judge which language to keep, cut or rework and were seen copying and pasting suggestions without editing. The researchers call this limit the AI wall, the point at which AI cannot replace domain expertise because it depends on the user's capacity to assess, refine and integrate its suggestions, which challenges the assumption that AI creates universal task fluidity. They offer two recommendations. Employees need at least enough general understanding and hands-on experience in a domain to assess and improve AI output, not deep expertise but enough judgment about what is right and wrong, since in the words of study lead Luca Vendraminelli AI is not a magic fix if it cannot fully automate tasks and expertise is irreplicable, and organizations should expect structural and cultural change in processes, decision-making and collaboration, because AI blurs roles in closely related fields, such as an SEO specialist and a content strategist, but struggles to bridge large divides between functions with different expertise, budgets and power, such as marketing, sales and product, which broader, more flexible job design can help address only through deliberate investment. <span class="src">Gen AI Won’t Make Your Employees Experts</span>

A synthesis of four studies in MIT Sloan Management Review gives empirical support to the homogenization argument, with the studies covering short stories, circular-economy solutions, a caption contest and collaborative storytelling. It defines group-level creativity as diversity, measured by the semantic similarity of outputs, and finds that AI raised individual quality, especially for lower-baseline creators, but pushed distributions toward higher similarity in all four, giving higher average quality and fewer outliers. The stage mattered, since AI in idea generation reduced diversity while AI in idea selection preserved variety, and keeping humans in charge of early ideation came closest to human-only diversity. In the circular-economy study a single human iterating with AI beat a crowd on quality and viability, but the crowd produced more novel ideas, and prompting the model to tackle a different problem each time restored novelty comparable to the crowd's. The authors also cite evidence that students with unrestricted AI access did worse once it was removed while guardrails eliminated the penalty and that consultants who accepted AI recommendations uncritically underperformed. <span class="src">The Hidden Cost of AI-Assisted Creativity</span>

Recommended practices follow from the sequencing finding, namely human-first ideation, diversified AI inputs through rotated prompts, chain-of-thought and parallel models, multi-agent workflows such as Colgate-Palmolive's, and deliberate friction, such as requiring people to write their own options before consulting AI, and Ubisoft's Ghostwriter, in which writers define character and context before selecting from AI-generated lines, is offered as an example of sequencing. Across the sources the common thread is that AI is most creative when people supply context, judgment and divergence, and that organizations should measure and protect the diversity of ideas and the expertise that make AI output evaluable rather than assuming that faster output means better capability. <span class="src">The Hidden Cost of AI-Assisted Creativity</span> <span class="src">Gen AI Won’t Make Your Employees Experts</span> <span class="src">Does GenAI Impose a Creativity Tax</span>
