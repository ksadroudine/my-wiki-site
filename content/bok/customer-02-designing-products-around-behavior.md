## Summary

A successful product has to be usable and also something people return to. This chapter explains two complementary approaches. Nir Eyal's Hook Model describes a four-step cycle of trigger, action, variable reward and investment that turns repeated use into habit. Don Norman's human-centered design makes a product usable in the first place by designing from how people actually behave and think. The chapter also explains feature overshoot, which is the buildup of unused features that makes the useful ones harder to find, and why listening to customer requests alone does not prevent it.

## Key Ideas

- A product can fail in two ways. It can be hard to use, or it can be easy to use but never become a habit.
- The Hook Model is a cycle of four steps, which are trigger, action, variable reward and investment, and each pass strengthens the habit.
- The investment step separates a habit-forming product from one that is merely engaging, because what users put in raises the cost of leaving and prepares their next visit.
- Human-centered design starts from what people actually do and how they think, and not from what customers say they want or what engineers want to show off.
- Unused features are not harmless. Each one clutters the interface and makes the useful features harder to find.

## Designing Products Around Behavior

### Two disciplines, two failure modes

Behavioral product design combines two disciplines. One is designing products that people habitually return to. The other is designing products that people can actually use. They address different failure modes. A product that is well designed and easy to use, but never forms a habit, will struggle to keep users even if each session goes well. A product built around habit formation but cluttered with unusable features will struggle to get users through the first step of the cycle cleanly enough for it ever to start.

Both disciplines treat what the user actually does, meaning behavior and mental models, as the correct design input. They set this against stated customer requests and a team's own technical ambitions, which are not sufficient guides to what should be built. See [Behavioral Product Design](#/concept/behavioral-product-design).

### The Hook Model

Nir Eyal's Hook Model describes habit formation as a cycle of four steps that repeats and strengthens with each pass.

1. **Trigger.** A cue prompts the user. At first it is external, such as a notification or an email. Once a habit is established it becomes internal, such as boredom, loneliness or another emotional state that the user has learned the product can address.
2. **Action.** The user performs the simplest behavior in anticipation of a reward, such as a single tap or swipe. The design keeps the friction as low as possible.
3. **Variable reward.** The payoff is unpredictable in content or size from one use to the next. The model's central claim from behavioral psychology is that this unpredictability sustains interest far more strongly than a fixed, fully predictable reward. It relies on the same mechanism, called intermittent reinforcement, that makes gambling and social-media feeds compelling.
4. **Investment.** The user puts something of their own into the product, such as time, data, content, a growing history or a network of followers.

The investment step is what separates a genuinely habit-forming product from one that is merely engaging. It does two things at once. It raises the psychological and practical cost of leaving for a competitor, because the investment would be lost or would have to be rebuilt. It also loads the next external trigger, which prepares the user to return and starts the cycle again.

### Human-centered design

Don Norman, the author of The Design of Everyday Things, addresses a logically earlier question, which is how to make a product's existing functions genuinely usable, whether or not it also forms habits.

His diagnosis of feature overshoot is the point of contact with the rest of product strategy. Unused features are not a harmless waste of investment. They are actively negative. Each added feature clutters the interface and degrades how easily people can find and use the smaller set of features that create most of the product's value for most users.

Norman traces overshoot to three causes that appear in product organizations in every industry.

- Customers in every market segment request extra features for their own use.
- Competitors' released features create pressure to match them one for one.
- Engineers are motivated to build and show off technically impressive new capability.

The first cause has a consequence. Resisting feature overshoot takes a deliberate discipline of design and product management, and cannot be achieved by simply listening to customers, because customer requests are one of the three drivers of the problem.
