## Summary

McKinsey's four cornerstones of corporate finance explain how a company creates value. Growth and returns on invested capital that exceed the cost of capital create it, only cash flow creates it, share prices reflect market expectations as well as performance, and the value of a business depends on who manages it. This chapter applies the cornerstones to growth strategy, portfolio management, acquisitions, capital structure and communication with investors. It also covers research showing that acquisitions raise combined value by only about 4 percent on average, and that stable companies with almost no revenue growth can still match the market.

## Key Ideas

- A company creates value when it grows and earns a return on invested capital that exceeds its cost of capital. Growth at a low return destroys value.
- Value comes from generating higher cash flows, and not from rearranging investors' claims on those cash flows.
- A good company is not always a good investment, because its great future performance may already be in the share price.
- Acquisitions raise the combined value of acquirer and target by only about 4 percent on average, and acquirers rarely capture much of it.
- The most important consideration in choosing a mix of debt and equity is the trade-off between flexibility and discipline, and not the tax benefit of debt.
- Stable companies with near-zero revenue growth can match market returns with lower volatility, through four plays that need no growth.

## Creating Corporate Value

### Four cornerstones

McKinsey's four cornerstones of corporate finance describe how a company creates value. See [Corporate Value Creation](#/concept/corporate-value-creation).

1. **Growth and return on invested capital.** Value is created when a company grows and earns a return on invested capital, called ROIC, that exceeds its cost of capital. The cost of capital is the return that investors could earn elsewhere with similar risk.
2. **Cash flow.** Value is created for shareholders by generating higher cash flows. Rearranging investors' claims on those cash flows does not create value.
3. **Expectations.** A company's share price is driven by changes in market expectations, and not only by its actual performance. This is called the expectations treadmill.
4. **The best owner.** The value of a business depends on who manages it and what strategy they pursue. This is called the best-owner principle.

### Growth and returns

The general rule for choosing between growth and returns is simple. High-return businesses should focus on further growth. Low-return businesses should first improve their returns, because growth at a low return destroys value.

Revenue growth comes in four types. They are gains in market share, increases in price, growth in the underlying market and acquisitions. Each creates a different amount of value and provokes different responses from whoever loses as a result.

Sustaining a high return is easier than sustaining high growth. Most products follow a natural S-curve to maturity, so a company must find a replacement of similar size for every product that matures, merely to keep revenue flat. This is called the portfolio treadmill.

### Why a good company can be a poor investment

The expectations treadmill explains why a good company and a good investment are not the same. A company's future great performance may already be priced into its share price. A weaker company with lower expectations built into its price can be the better investment, because its expectations are easier to beat.

### Acquisitions

Research on acquisitions finds that the combined value of acquirer and target rises by only about 4 percent on average, and acquiring shareholders rarely capture much of that gain. Acquisitions that do create value for the acquirer typically fit one of five archetypes.

- They improve the performance of the target.
- They consolidate excess capacity in an industry.
- They speed up access to a market.
- They acquire skills or technology faster than the company could build them.
- They pick early winners and help develop them.

Large-scale mergers and acquisitions fail at a rate of 70 to 75 percent.

### Capital structure and investors

Capital structure is the mix of debt and equity that finances a company. The trade-off between flexibility and discipline, and not the tax benefit of debt, is the most important consideration in setting it. Most large companies are advised to aim for at least an investment-grade credit rating, to avoid the higher costs and restrictive conditions of below-investment-grade debt.

Companies should also segment their investors. Intrinsic investors hold for the long term and carry out deep research, and they are the ultimate drivers of long-term share price. Traders buy and sell over short horizons. Mechanical investors, such as index funds, buy by rule. Investor communication should focus on the intrinsic investors, and should not try to satisfy the market as a single undifferentiated group.

### Stable companies

A 20-year study of more than 10,000 companies in North America, Europe and Japan found 172 stable firms. They had steady, near-zero revenue growth. They matched market-average shareholder returns at 12 percent lower volatility, and they were half as likely to suffer a severe collapse in value of 90 percent or more. They averaged nearly 100 years of age. Fifty-seven of the 172, or one in three, outperformed the market on total shareholder return, which is the gain in share price plus dividends.

The stable outperformers cluster around four value-creation plays, none of which needs revenue growth.

- **The asset-light play** shifts a company from products toward services and software. Siemens' move to software as a service produced average annual shareholder returns of 9 percent.
- **The gross-margin play** moves a company upmarket through quality and not through cost-cutting. Morgan Advanced Materials doubled its margins, with average returns of 9 percent.
- **The balance-sheet play** uses vertical integration to capture a larger share of the value chain. Whitbread owns its Premier Inn hotels instead of franchising them, with returns of 10 percent.
- **The dividend play** offers bond-like, low-volatility payouts. GATX has paid dividends without interruption since 1919, with returns of 12 percent.

One in four stable outperformers had an owner with a controlling interest, against fewer than 8 percent of the S&P 1500 overall. This suggests that concentrated ownership supports the disciplined, long-term outlook these strategies need. The stable outperformers also avoided large-scale mergers and acquisitions.
