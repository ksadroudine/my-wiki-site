## Summary

Jeff Sutherland's Scrum is a framework for team-based, iterative work. It replaces the detailed upfront plans of traditional project management with short cycles of delivery and learning. This chapter explains the fixed-length sprint, the three roles, the daily stand-up and the sprint retrospective that closes each cycle. It also covers how teams estimate work in relative points, why velocity is the key measure, the criteria that decide when a user story is ready and done, and why Scrum treats multitasking as a measurable source of waste.

## Key Ideas

- Scrum replaces upfront control and predictability with regular checkpoints, because every real project involves ongoing discovery.
- Work happens in fixed-length sprints, commonly two weeks, during which no one outside the team may add work.
- There are exactly three roles, which are the team, the product owner and the Scrum Master.
- Velocity, the number of story points a team reliably completes in a sprint, is the key operating measure, because it allows forecasting and shows what slows the team.
- Every sprint ends with a retrospective that chooses one specific, testable improvement for the next sprint.
- A team member who splits time across two projects loses about 20 percent of available time to switching between them, and across five projects about 75 percent.

## Working in Sprints with Scrum

### The idea behind Scrum

Scrum was built to replace the pursuit of upfront control and predictability in traditional project management, which is expressed through detailed plans, Gantt charts and status reports. It accepts that every real project involves ongoing discovery. It therefore builds in regular checkpoints at which the team assesses whether the work is heading in the right direction. See [Scrum Methodology](#/concept/scrum-methodology).

### Sprints

Work happens in sprints. A sprint is a time box of fixed length, commonly two weeks, and it is not negotiable. The team commits at the start to a set of items from the backlog, which is the ordered list of work. Once the sprint has begun, no one outside the team can add work to it.

### Three roles

Scrum has exactly three roles.

- **The team members** do the work.
- **The Scrum Master** helps the team improve how it works and removes impediments.
- **The Product Owner** owns and prioritizes the backlog and is accountable for turning the team's output into business value.

### The daily stand-up

The daily stand-up is a strict fifteen-minute meeting held standing up at the same time every day. Each team member answers only three questions. What did you do yesterday to help finish the sprint? What will you do today? What obstacles stand in the team's way?

### Estimating and velocity

Teams estimate each story, which is a small piece of work described from the user's point of view, by relative size and not in hours. They use the Fibonacci sequence of 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13 and so on, through a game called Planning Poker. Team members reveal their point estimates at the same moment. If the estimates lie within two cards of each other, they are averaged. If they differ by more than three cards, the highest and the lowest estimators discuss their reasoning and then estimate again.

Two sets of criteria decide when a story is ready and when it is finished. The INVEST criteria say whether a story is ready to be pulled into a sprint. The letters stand for independent, negotiable, valuable, estimable, small and testable. A matching definition of done states the conditions a story must meet to count as complete. Teams that apply both rigorously are reported to roughly double their delivery speed.

A team's velocity is the number of story points it reliably completes per sprint. It is treated as the key operating measure. Knowing it lets a Product Owner forecast completion dates from the work that remains in the backlog, and it lets the team see what is actually slowing it down.

### The retrospective

Each sprint closes with a retrospective. The team asks what went right, what could go better and what one specific, testable improvement it will make in the next sprint. Such an improvement is called a kaizen, from the Japanese word for continuous improvement. A companion survey, called the happiness metric, asks people to rate their role and the company and to name one thing that would improve the next sprint. It helps to decide which improvement to pursue, on the premise that a team's happiness follows autonomy, mastery and purpose.

### Multitasking as waste

Scrum treats multitasking as a measurable source of waste, and not as a virtue. Research cited by the source shows that a team member who splits time across two simultaneous projects loses about 20 percent of total available time to switching between them. Across five projects, the loss rises to 75 percent.
