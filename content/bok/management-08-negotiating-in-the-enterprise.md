## Summary

Skilled negotiators can create leverage even when they have no complete alternative to a deal. This chapter explains how partial alternatives, actions that need no agreement from the other side and a walk-away presented as a warning and not a threat can shift a negotiation. It also shows that dependence between buyer and supplier is usually more balanced than it feels. It then turns to why large companies negotiate poorly for structural reasons, and sets out the recommended fixes. They are to give negotiators no authority to commit, so that they act as problem-solvers, and to replace deal review boards with proactive deal value boards.

## Key Ideas

- Having no complete alternative to a deal does not mean a negotiator is powerless. It usually means the search for alternatives was too narrow.
- A partial alternative, which covers only part of the need, can still shift the balance.
- Dependence between the parties is usually more balanced than it feels, because a sole-source supplier typically needs the customer's revenue too.
- A walk-away presented as a warning avoids the spiral of threat and counterthreat that a threat provokes.
- Large companies negotiate poorly for two structural reasons, which are an agency problem and an alignment problem, and not because negotiators lack skill.
- Companies should decide first which deals are worth negotiating hard, and should let routine, standardized deals close at market-standard terms.

## Negotiating in the Enterprise

### Leverage without a complete alternative

The starting point is that having no clear alternative to the deal on the table does not mean a negotiator is powerless. It usually means the negotiator has not widened the search enough. The alternative is called the BATNA, short for best alternative to a negotiated agreement, a term introduced by Roger Fisher and William Ury in Getting to Yes. See [Enterprise Negotiation Tactics](#/concept/enterprise-negotiation-tactics).

**Partial alternatives.** Negotiators usually look for one complete substitute that solves every problem. A breakthrough often comes instead from an option that replaces the deal only in part. A technology company depended entirely on one supplier of critical electronic components and faced repeated price increases and worsening service. It found two smaller, qualified suppliers that together could cover only 30 percent of its volume. That partial coverage was enough to reduce its dependence credibly. It won better pricing from the incumbent in exchange for a modest volume commitment, instead of accepting an unaffordable increase.

**Balanced dependence.** Dependence between the parties is almost always more balanced than it feels from the weaker-seeming position. A company that relies heavily on a sole-source supplier is usually important to that supplier's revenue too, and the higher the customer's switching costs, the more it hurts the supplier to lose that customer. Genuine walk-away brinkmanship is therefore very rare. The authors, who have advised on hundreds of negotiations over three decades, saw a deal actually fall apart under firm but reasonable pressure only once. A global manufacturer faced a coatings supplier demanding a 10 percent price increase. Its business was only 10 percent of the supplier's revenue. But that revenue came from one of the supplier's most profitable and specialized product lines, so losing it would force costly retooling and strain the supplier's cash flow far more than the percentage suggested. Even a seemingly weak position can rest on hidden vulnerabilities of the other side that are worth researching.

**Procedural alternatives.** When the search for an alternative keeps coming up empty, a negotiator can take actions that need no formal agreement from the other side. One example is paying invoices at the lowest price that the supplier charges anywhere in the world, with a note explaining the correction, and not formally demanding a renegotiation. This relies on inertia and on tacit consent, as opposed to active consent, to shift the relationship without a confrontation.

**Warning, not threat.** A possible walk-away can be presented as a warning, such as "we cannot afford this, so we will be forced to find someone else." A threat, such as "agree or we cut you off," provokes a spiral of counterthreats. A warning signals self-protection and not an intent to harm. Appealing to fairness can also win concessions that raw power cannot. People will sometimes concede rather than be shown to be acting unfairly.

### Why large companies negotiate poorly

Enterprise negotiations underperform because of two structural problems, and not because negotiators lack skill.

- **The agency problem.** Frontline negotiators are rewarded for closing any deal, through commission and time pressure, and not for the company's real interests. Companies therefore restrict their authority. This turns them into couriers who must repeatedly ask for approval.
- **The alignment problem.** In complex deals with many stakeholders, forcing an early internal consensus on the most conservative acceptable terms leaves negotiators no room to explore creative trade-offs.

### The fixes

The counterintuitive fix for the agency problem is to give negotiators no authority to commit at all. They become problem-solvers who explore options and bring a recommended business plan back to the decision-makers, and they do not defend pre-approved minimums. This removes the incentive to make self-interested concessions just to close a deal. It also lets negotiators engage the other side creatively without triggering internal vetoes.

Companies should first decide which deals and issues are worth negotiating hard. Most contract terms can be settled at reasonable market-standard levels without a real loss of value. The question is not how large an issue is but how much of its value is genuinely at stake in negotiating it, as opposed to accepting a market norm. Companies are increasingly using generative AI agents to close standardized, low-variation deals automatically.

For the alignment problem, deal review boards, which evolved from approval processes for capital projects, should be reimagined as proactive deal value boards. A deal value board gives negotiating teams an enterprise-wide view of related deals and of current and future leverage. It also brokers internal side deals that compensate business units who would otherwise block a proposal that benefits the company overall. Its role changes over the life of a deal. Early on it sets expectations. During the negotiation it supports problem-solving. At the end a small group makes a final decision on whether to go ahead, once the options are fully understood.
