## Summary

The global digital economy is splitting into diverging national and regional paths, shaped as much by US-China rivalry, regulation and access to AI computing capacity as by markets and technology. This chapter draws on the 2026 Digital Evolution Index, which covers 125 countries. It explains the four groups into which the index sorts countries, why the United States and China dominate digital GDP while pursuing different AI strategies, and why digital regulation is diverging by region. It then shows how Chinese companies, facing trade tensions and tighter scrutiny of foreign investment, expand abroad by targeting microregions, and why Singapore is a favored base.

## Key Ideas

- After 25 years of connecting countries and markets through common standards and platforms, the digital economy is eroding into diverging national and regional paths.
- The United States and China together account for just over half of the digital GDP of 125 countries, and they pursue structurally different AI strategies.
- Global digital momentum has slowed since the pandemic, from 4.3 percent annual growth to 2.4 percent, and lower-income countries have slowed the most.
- Digital regulation is diverging, and classifying markets by their regulatory logic may soon matter as much as classifying them by growth potential.
- Chinese companies expand abroad by targeting microregions, such as provinces, cities and economic zones, and not entire countries.
- Singapore is the most attractive microregion for Chinese companies that want to preserve access to Western markets.

## A Fragmenting Digital Economy

### The erosion of a single digital economy

The 2026 Digital Evolution Index is a collaboration between the Digital Planet research center at Tufts University and Via Science. It tracks 185 indicators across 125 countries. In an article in the Harvard Business Review, Bhaskar Chakravorti and his coauthors argue that a single global digital economy is eroding. For 25 years, digitalization connected countries, supply chains and markets through common standards and platforms. That period is giving way to fragmentation. See [Geopolitical Fragmentation and Global Expansion](#/concept/geopolitical-fragmentation-and-global-expansion).

### Four groups of countries

The index sorts countries by two things. The first is the current level of digital development. The second is momentum, which is the compound annual growth rate from 2008 to 2025.

- **Stand outs** combine high development with fast momentum. They include the United States, China, Singapore and South Korea.
- **Stall outs** have high development but slowing momentum. They include Finland, Denmark, Germany, Japan and the United Kingdom.
- **Break outs** have lower development but fast momentum. They include India, Vietnam, Brazil and Thailand.
- **Watch outs** have both low development and weak momentum. They include Pakistan, Nigeria, Venezuela and Ethiopia.

### Two superpowers with different strategies

The combined digital GDP of the United States and China is just over half of the total across all 125 countries studied. Their rivalry in AI sets the pace of digital investment worldwide, and they follow structurally different strategies.

- The **United States** relies on private-sector investment, the development of foundation models and a lead in computing that is estimated at 39.7 million petaflops, roughly half of the world's total AI computing, driven by advanced chip design.
- **China** relies on investment coordinated between government and industry, on algorithmic efficiency engineered around constraints on computing, on large pools of domestic data, on rapid construction of large data centers and on leadership in open-source AI. Stanford's institute for human-centered AI reports that the gap in model performance has effectively closed, despite China's computing disadvantage.

Other stand outs succeed through strategies that do not depend on scale. Singapore combines private-sector strength with digital transformation directed by the government, and it deliberately uses its ties to both the American and Chinese ecosystems. The United Arab Emirates pursues state-led investment toward a hub for AI, although regional war has brought real economic uncertainty. Estonia pioneered e-government infrastructure, with a secure data-exchange layer called X-Road and an e-residency program. Ireland positions itself as a global exporter of technology and a hub for commercializing innovation, through its membership of the European Union and its cultural closeness to the United States.

The stall outs are concentrated in Europe. They have built exceptional digital infrastructure and strong institutions, but the speed of their innovation is dampened by their own investment in regulation, on privacy, security and transparency. This neighborhood effect lowered the cost of scaling across borders enough to help smaller economies in Central and Eastern Europe and the Baltic states become stand outs and break outs in their own right.

The break outs combine lower digital scores with the fastest momentum of any group. They often skip development based on personal computers altogether and rely on infrastructure that is mobile first, paired with digital systems for public payments. India's UPI payment system handled 22.64 billion transactions in March 2026 alone, and global transactions in mobile money passed $2 trillion in 2025, double the level of four years earlier, with most of the growth in Africa.

The watch outs lag on both development and momentum. They are concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa and in fragile states of the Middle East and of Central and South Asia. Paradoxically, they report the highest trust in AI of any group, according to UN survey data. This is a mindset of adoption based on trust that could jumpstart future momentum, but it currently operates with the fewest institutional guardrails.

### Slower momentum and diverging rules

Several patterns compound the fragmentation.

- **A winner-takes-most dynamic.** AI is reinforcing it, as economies that are already digitally advanced pull further ahead.
- **Slower momentum.** Global digital momentum has slowed markedly since the pandemic, from 4.3 percent annual growth before it to 2.4 percent after it. Lower-income countries have slowed the most.
- **Diverging regulation.** Regulation is diverging by group and not converging toward one global standard. The United States minimizes federal rules on AI. China follows a model set centrally, which is to move fast but obey the rules. The European Union, whose members are concentrated among the stall outs, has extensive rules on AI and data protection. The break outs are assembling hybrid frameworks that borrow from all three.

The New Delhi Declaration of the 2026 AI Impact Summit signals an emerging Global South bloc that wants to help write its own global norms for governing AI. For business leaders, the practical implication is that classifying markets by regulatory logic may soon matter as much as classifying them by growth potential. The logics are permissive, precautionary, state-directed and hybrid. AI strategies that assume a single harmonized global technology stack will increasingly misread the real operating environment.

### How Chinese companies expand abroad

An article in MIT Sloan Management Review by Shameen Prashantham and Lola Woetzel offers a company-level playbook for this fragmented environment. It draws on 40 interviews with Chinese business leaders from early 2023 to early 2024. China's domestic consumer market has recovered slowly since the pandemic, and Chinese companies have adopted chu hai, which means going overseas, as a rallying strategy. They face three headwinds that mirror the macro picture.

1. **Trade tensions and export controls.** The US restriction of October 2022 on exports of AI chips to China hit not only Chinese semiconductor firms but also the share prices of Nvidia and Broadcom.
2. **Tighter scrutiny of foreign investment** in North America and Western Europe. Syngenta, a seed company owned by Chinese investors, was ordered to sell 160 acres of Arkansas farmland under a foreign-land-ownership law of 2023.
3. **Weak infrastructure, inefficient bureaucracy and cultural unfamiliarity** in the emerging markets that otherwise have fewer trade barriers.

The authors' central finding is that Chinese executives respond by changing the unit of their international strategy. They do not target entire countries. They target microregions, which are subnational units such as provinces, cities or defined economic zones. A McKinsey study in 2019 identified more than 40,000 of them worldwide, each averaging 3,000 square kilometers, $3 billion of GDP and 180,000 residents. This mental model comes naturally to Chinese leaders, because China's own development has historically been decentralized and tied to places. Of the roughly 2 billion people worldwide who reached a high standard of living in the past 20 years, 1.1 billion were Chinese.

### Three place-based strategies

Three strategies recur across the companies studied.

- **Finding new routes to existing markets.** A company relocates parts of its supply chain, or its entire headquarters, to a microregion with strategic advantages, instead of dodging trade barriers with loopholes. The power-drill maker Chervon built its own factory in Vietnam to diversify its supply chain while still serving Western customers. The fast-fashion retailer Shein moved its entire headquarters to Singapore, to preserve its ability to form Western partnerships that have become harder to form from mainland China.
- **Building hubs to reach emerging markets.** Chinese investment clusters in gateway cities. Singapore serves Southeast Asia. For example, the Shanghai-based firm Leyan used a Singapore headquarters to market its AI chatbot to e-commerce platforms such as Shopee and Lazada. Dubai serves the Middle East and North Africa. Addis Ababa, Accra and Nairobi serve as beachheads in Africa, although they lag Singapore in trade infrastructure and efficiency of bureaucracy and so need more local partners to compensate.
- **Going virtual through digital talent hubs.** Distributed digital talent connects resources and opportunities without the heavier commitment of physical entry. Shenzhen has produced consumer-electronics brands, such as Anker Innovations and Govee, that sell through Western retail channels under their own names and not as generic listings on a marketplace. Hangzhou is home to Alibaba's Global Digital Talent training program, and it has generated a local pool of entrepreneurs who can build direct-to-consumer brands for Western markets.

Singapore recurs as the most attractive microregion for Chinese companies that want to preserve access to Western markets, for three reasons. It offers access to talent, trade infrastructure and geopolitical neutrality. Any company that is assessing a hub that can withstand fragmentation should weigh the same three attributes, whatever its country of origin.
