## Summary

Telecom operators are repositioning themselves as AI companies, because efficiency programs and connectivity growth have left the sector behind other industries in shareholder returns. This chapter explains the case for an AI-first reinvention of customer care and network operations, which consultancies estimate could lift earnings by double-digit percentage points, and the four plays that an operator can combine. It covers examples such as SK Telecom, Orange and Singtel and the partnerships with AI-native companies such as Perplexity. It also shows that capturing the value depends less on algorithms than on scaling discipline, which means redesigning workflows end to end and building shared platforms, unified data and early risk involvement.

## Key Ideas

- Over the five years to 2023, telecom ranked 31st of 33 sectors for total shareholder return, which is why AI is described as a strategic reset and not as a tool.
- BCG estimates that an AI-first operator could gain 15 percentage points of EBITDA, about 70 percent of it from productivity and about 30 percent from revenue growth.
- The value comes from reshaping end-to-end workflows around coordinated agents, and not from deploying isolated point solutions.

- Only 12 percent of operators report sizable impact from AI, because nearly three-quarters only lightly redesign existing processes.
- Seven elements separate operators that scale AI from those stuck in pilots.

## Becoming an AI Operator

### The investment problem

Telecom operators face a structural problem with investment, and this is why AI is described as a strategic reset and not as a tool. Over the five years to 2023, the median operator's annual total shareholder return, which is the gain in share price plus dividends, improved only from 3 percent to 6 percent. That ranked the sector 31st of 33 sectors, and well below the broader market's 13 percent. Eighty-five percent of the industry's net value creation came from dividends and not from growth in market value, and more than 40 percent of operators ended the period smaller than they began it. Operators also trade at multiples of enterprise value of about 5 to 7 times, far below the roughly 20 times of data-center and cloud providers. BCG attributes the gap to investors' doubt that operators can capture the value of AI without fundamental change. Conventional efficiency programs show diminishing returns, and growth in revenue per user has proved elusive. AI is therefore positioned as the lever that could change both the cost base and the growth story. See [Telecom AI Transformation](#/concept/telecom-ai-transformation).

### What an AI-first operator gains

The AI-first agenda reinvents two domains.

- **Customer care.** Agents, which are AI systems that act toward goals on their own, authenticate customers and detect their intent, to power a concierge that is always available on every channel. Calls that need a person are supported with real-time scripts and coaching modeled on the best performers. Root-cause analysis of customers' problems feeds insight across the company. BCG estimates productivity gains of 45 to 60 percent, far beyond what chatbots that only deflect calls achieve.
- **Network operations.** Reusable agents span the technology stack, from sensor agents that monitor telemetry to agents that schedule field engineers and advise them on site. An orchestrator agent coordinates diagnostic, root-cause and preventive agents. The estimated productivity gain is 40 to 50 percent.

Smaller gains are available in IT and corporate functions, and the overall estimate for productivity is 30 to 40 percent. Beyond productivity, personalized management of customer value, dynamic pricing and network as a service, which lets enterprises consume bandwidth as software, are expected to lift revenue by 2 to 3 percent. Billing more completely for the value-added services sold to enterprises also reduces revenue leakage.

BCG estimates that an AI-first operator could gain 15 percentage points of EBITDA, which is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization as a share of revenue. It also estimates a gain of 10 points of EBIT, which is the same measure after depreciation and amortization are deducted. About 70 percent of the gain comes from productivity and about 30 percent from growth. Running AI raises recurring costs, so after those costs the operator keeps 70 to 90 percent of the net benefit, and the projected internal rate of return over five years is 20 to 35 percent. This holds only if the transformation is a coherent redesign.

### Reshape, do not just deploy

BCG's central instruction is to reshape and not merely to deploy. Value comes from connecting AI initiatives, so that agents and assets are reused across the enterprise and a cycle of observing, planning and acting links the key processes from end to end. An orchestrating role coordinates specialized agents on tasks that cross functions. Disconnected pilots stay isolated and capture little.

The recommended starting point is a readiness check. It covers alignment between top management and the business, an investment horizon of three years and clear accountability for profit and loss for each major solution. The 10-20-70 rule expresses the same lesson in numbers. It directs 10 percent of effort to algorithms, 20 percent to technology and data and 70 percent to people and operational change. The data must be ready first, because consistent performance by agents depends on it. Some capability must also be built inside the company, because products bought off the shelf cannot always supply the business context, reusability and fit with the architecture that an operator's workflows need.

Singtel's chief AI, data and analytics officer makes a related argument. The strongest opportunities combine material business value, proprietary data or context, and a workflow that AI can change meaningfully. A great model in a weak process creates a demo. A good-enough model in the right workflow creates a business. He expects the advantage to move from access to models toward data, context, memory, tools and governance.

### Four plays

BCG's strategic framework describes four plays that an operator can combine, according to its ambition, assets and technical maturity.

1. **The AI-first core operator** is the mandatory baseline. It reshapes network operations, customer service and decisions into a self-learning, self-healing system, through predictive care, management of the next best action and automation of the network in a closed loop. The estimated gain is 5 to 15 percentage points of EBITDA within 12 to 18 months.
2. **AI monetization in consumer markets** builds hyper-personalized, AI-native experiences that deepen loyalty and raise revenue per user. SK Telecom's A. assistant has passed 10 million monthly active users, which is the cited proof that such products attract real use.
3. **AI convergence** repositions the operator as an end-to-end digital partner for enterprises and not a vendor of connectivity. Orange combined its acquisition of Orange Cyberdefense, partnerships with Google Cloud and Microsoft and the managed-services platform of Orange Business.
4. **Sovereign AI infrastructure** uses operators' relationships with regulators and their physical footprint to build data centers that are ready for AI, GPU-as-a-service platforms and connectivity that meets compliance standards, for governments and enterprises. Singtel's Nxera brand and its GPU service built with Nvidia are examples.

### SK Telecom's two-sided approach

SK Telecom shows how an operator can pursue the agenda by building internally and forming alliances at once. It plans to raise the share of its spending on AI from 12 percent to 33 percent by 2028, and it targets about a third of its revenue from AI. Its AI Pyramid Strategy has three layers.

- The **infrastructure layer** has data centers, AI chips through its subsidiary Sapeon and a multilingual language model for telecom.
- The **transformation layer** applies AI across mobile, broadband, enterprise and new areas such as healthcare and mobility, with mid-term goals of cutting service costs by 20 to 30 percent.
- The **service layer** is led by the A. chatbot assistant, launched in 2022 and improved with Microsoft Azure's OpenAI service.

Alongside this, SK Telecom has invested in Anthropic, Allganize and Imprimed, formed a 16-company domestic K-AI Alliance and joined a Global AI Alliance with Deutsche Telekom, e& and Singtel to develop telecom-specific language models and AI platforms. Deutsche Telekom and SK Telecom are also working with OpenAI, Google and Meta on a telecom-specific model to power a next-generation digital customer assistant.

### Partnering with AI-native companies

A second, more visible pattern is partnership with AI-native companies, with Perplexity as the example. In 2024 SK Telecom invested $10 million to co-develop language models and to integrate Perplexity's search into A. Deutsche Telekom and SoftBank followed within months, and offered Perplexity to a combined mobile and broadband base of more than 335 million users. By 2025 the arrangements had deepened from bundling free premium subscriptions into co-development, including SoftBank's plan for an AI-native phone priced under $1,000.

Operators give several reasons for the model. It brings better engagement with customers than traditional voice assistants offer. It adds subscription revenue as legacy margins compress. It differentiates the operator as an early mover. And it gives access to AI-native devices and to an ecosystem built around a well-funded challenger to the larger Silicon Valley providers. Supporters frame it as operators positioning themselves as AI companies and not just buying AI tools. Generative AI built for telecom also reaches sales and IT. Personalized sales emails generated from customer data free representatives for time with customers, and coding assistants produce code, documentation and automated tests.

### The scaling gap

The binding constraint on all these plays is scaling. McKinsey's surveys show a persistent gap between adoption and value. Reported impact roughly doubled in a year, from about 25 percent to about 50 percent of executives capturing impact from AI and generative AI. A later survey found that only 12 percent had captured sizable impact. Most were stuck at what the research calls the money step, where investment and enthusiasm have not yet reached the balance sheet.

The explanation lies mainly in how operators deploy the technology. Nearly three-quarters use AI to support only minimal or moderate redesigns of existing processes, and so they speed up legacy ways of working. Redesigning workflows from scratch around the steps that are necessary removes entire stages, handoffs and layers of coordination. This is what separates lasting gains in cost, cycle time and quality from AI that only moves a bottleneck.

Seven elements separate operators that scale AI from those stuck in pilots.

1. **End-to-end workflows** for each domain.
2. **A modular central platform.**
3. **Unified data**, both structured and unstructured.
4. **Built-in change management.**
5. **A joint operating model and talent strategy.**
6. **A partner ecosystem.**
7. **Deliberate guardrails against risk.**

An AI factory of reusable platforms, agent frameworks and governance controls replaces siloed builds. Tying every initiative to a budget line follows the principle that if AI is not in the budget, it is not real. AI-driven simulation of the effect on customer experience lets operators optimize their capital plans by 10 to 15 percent and repurpose up to 25 percent of planned spending. KPN and AT&T show the same disciplines of scaling, in agent-based customer care and in governed, AI-assisted software delivery.
