## Summary

Access to AI does not give a company a lasting advantage, because rivals can use the same tools. This chapter explains where advantage can still come from, how leaders can build an AI strategy step by step, and why executive understanding of AI matters more than technology choices. It also covers the AI factory as an operating model, the structures that let companies scale generative AI and the changing roles of senior leaders. Further sections look at the effect of AI on strategy work and at the reasons partnerships with technology firms so often fail. The chapter closes with how private equity firms build AI capability across the companies they own.

## Key Ideas

- Using AI is quickly becoming a requirement, not an advantage, because inputs such as models, computing power and talent are available to everyone.
- Lasting advantage comes from assets that AI cannot copy, such as proprietary processes, trusted relationships and the ability to frame the right problem.
- An AI strategy works best as a staged process that starts with executive understanding, not with a list of tools.
- Companies capture value when they build structures that bring business experts and technologists together and when they redesign processes instead of adding AI to old ones.
- The operating model, meaning the systems that deliver the work, matters more than the business plan for realizing AI value.
- Most AI partnerships fail to meet their goals, and the main causes are unclear value, misaligned incentives and disputes over data and ownership.
- Private equity firms treat AI capability as something to build across many companies at once, and AI readiness has become a central question when they buy.

## Strategy and Competitive Advantage

### Why AI alone is not an advantage

Powerful technologies rarely gave their users a lasting edge. The steam engine, the electric motor and the personal computer all spread so widely that every competitor eventually had them. Generative AI is following the same pattern. Cost savings at companies such as Cisco and Klarna can be copied by any rival that uses the same tools.

Two features make AI worse in this respect. Models learn from data that is continually updated, so a first mover's visible choices become part of what later movers can draw on. In addition, the inputs to AI, including computing power, algorithms and talent, are becoming cheaper and more widely available.

### Where advantage goes instead

Value moves to whatever AI leaves standing or strengthens. BCG finds that advantages based on knowledge, stored data and operational excellence are most at risk, because revenue can look healthy while margins drain away. Advantages that grow stronger with AI include data on live flows of activity, trusted relationships, deep integration with customers' systems, network scale and physical assets.

Bain estimates that AI puts $4.7 trillion of profit at stake between 2025 and 2035. About 75 percent of that comes from new products and shifts in market share, not from productivity. Most of the disruption will come from known competitors that use AI best.

A company's own situation matters too. HBR argues that a firm can use AI to strengthen assets that rivals cannot replicate, as Amazon does with its supplier network, or can rebuild its entire business model around AI. A further source of advantage is human judgment in choosing and framing the right problem, because generating ideas is now cheap. See [AI and Sustainable Competitive Advantage](#/concept/ai-sustainable-competitive-advantage).

### Building an AI strategy

The sources describe a staged process. A typical version has seven steps. Leaders first build their own understanding of AI. They then clarify current priorities, write a vision for AI, look for opportunities that match AI to business problems, assess the organization's maturity, rank the opportunities and produce a roadmap.

The first step matters most. Many AI projects fail because executives treat AI like a conventional IT project, with a fixed scope and timeline, when it behaves more like research. When results take longer than expected, leaders lose patience and cancel projects that needed more time. Executive AI fluency has three parts that build on each other. Leaders first need to understand how AI works and what a realistic deployment looks like. Next they need to know the use cases in their own industry, including the limits of what AI can do. Only then should they write a vision tied to the company's goals for the next three to five years.

Supporting mechanisms help carry the strategy through. A central team, called a center of excellence, can run experiments, set governance and benchmark tools. Communities of practice, such as hackathons and prompt-sharing sessions, bring hidden individual use of AI into view. See [AI Strategy](#/concept/ai-strategy).

### Capturing the value

Most companies adopt generative AI widely and capture little strategic value. A three-year study of 23 Swiss companies found that only two had scaled it into a real capability. Both had built what the researchers call an AI spine. This is a cross-functional team of AI developers, a risk and compliance officer, a technology owner and experts in the business processes. Senior management funds it, and a senior executive oversees it.

A traditional hub-and-spoke center of excellence keeps technical experts in one hub and sends them out to the business units. The spine does the opposite and brings experts in the work itself into the central team. It also receives a share of the savings or revenue that its applications produce, which forces strict measurement of return.

Value also builds in levels. HBR describes four. Individual productivity gains come first, then shared team understanding, then redesigned core work and finally new products and markets. Gains often arrive after an initial dip, which is called the productivity J-curve, and the sources recommend controlled experiments to find out what works. See [Gen AI Value-Creation Frameworks](#/concept/gen-ai-value-creation-frameworks).

### The operating model: the AI factory

Marco Iansiti and Karim Lakhani distinguish two things. A business model describes how a firm creates and captures value. An operating model is the set of systems that delivers the work, and it sets the practical limit on how much value the firm can realize. Digital operating models can grow in scale, scope and learning at almost no extra cost, because digital activities can be copied and connected.

They call the engine that makes this work the AI factory. It combines a data pipeline, the development of algorithms, a platform for running experiments and the software infrastructure that holds them together. Firms usually pass through four stages on the way, which are siloed data, pilots, shared data hubs and a mature factory. Long-established organizations struggle because their technology mirrors their departments, so separate systems for customers, orders and accounting resist the cross-functional data that the factory needs. Procter and Gamble built its own factory and later extended it to handle generative and agentic AI. See [AI Factory](#/concept/ai-factory).

### The leadership team

BCG finds that nearly every company is experimenting with AI, but 60 percent have produced no value and only about 5 percent, which it calls future-built companies, capture significant bottom-line gains. It estimates that success depends about 70 percent on people and process, 20 percent on technology and 10 percent on algorithms.

Each senior role changes in a specific way. The chief people officer becomes an architect of capabilities who decides what work suits people and what suits AI. The chief marketing officer owns data and growth platforms across the company. The chief transformation officer separates real AI results from mere activity. The chief financial officer shifts time from producing numbers to advising the business. The chief operating officer reinvents processes from first principles instead of automating existing ones. The chief information officer remains necessary, even as business teams build their own agents, because someone must decide what should be built and govern it. See [AI-First C-Suite Playbook](#/concept/ai-first-c-suite-playbook).

### Strategy work and decisions

AI also changes how strategy is made. Traditional tools such as SWOT analysis and the five forces are simple because people can only consider so many options. AI can search thousands of options, build living models of the business and challenge a plan in a structured way. In one experiment, individuals using AI matched two-person teams without it.

Because everyone has access to the same models, the sources place lasting advantage in company-specific data, internal processes and speed. A BCG survey of 175 chief strategy officers shows that analysis is becoming a commodity. It names four modes of thinking to cultivate, which are systems, counterfactual, integrative and critical thinking. See [AI in Strategy and Decision-Making](#/concept/ai-in-strategy-and-decision-making).

### Partnerships

Companies often turn to partners for AI capability, including competitors. BCG studied 209 industrial and 93 technology companies and found that nine in ten technology-industry AI partnerships failed to meet their goals in 2024. It describes four types of partnership and six pitfalls. Four of the pitfalls arise when the deal is designed. They are a hazy value proposition, misaligned goals, unclear ownership of data and intellectual property, and weak governance. The other two arise during execution. The right partner also changes as a company's maturity grows. See [AI-Driven Partner Collaboration](#/concept/ai-driven-partner-collaboration).

### Private equity and AI

Private equity firms show what a systematic approach looks like. Apollo Global Management built a dedicated portfolio operations team roughly five years before the account reviewed here. It hired an operating partner to run AI capability-building across the companies it owns, and it later added a second AI executive as the work expanded.

Apollo's process starts before the investment. An assessment specific to the industry and the target shows how AI is affecting the business and where it is heading, which filters out high-risk uses of AI before the purchase. Detailed due diligence, often with outside consultants, then identifies specific use cases and prepares an implementation plan that is ready to run once the deal closes. After the purchase, the aim is self-sufficiency. Apollo's central AI team supplies recommended tools, vendors, potential senior hires and consultants, but each company is expected to run its own AI capability eventually. With the venture firm 25madison, Apollo also runs an incubator for AI-native startups that can supply its portfolio companies and also grow into standalone software businesses.

The measured results are concrete. The educational publisher Cengage runs eight AI projects at once. They have cut costs by 40 percent in some content production, by 15 to 20 percent in automated lead generation, by 15 percent in customer care and by 10 to 15 percent in software development. At Yahoo, engineering productivity has improved by more than 20 percent. Apollo also runs a central AI system that compares purchase prices across more than 40 portfolio companies. In one case it analyzed 15,000 software purchase agreements in minutes and helped one company cut procurement costs by more than 65 percent.

From the buyer's side, AI readiness has become a key question in due diligence. Enterprise IT services companies have lost more than a third of their value since 2021, and A.D. Little frames this as a reprioritization and not a decline, because Gartner projects that global spending on AI services will reach $478 billion by 2028. That creates an opening to buy underpriced but AI-ready companies. Its diligence framework tests four dimensions of the target. The capabilities test looks for referenceable AI use cases with measurable results. The commercial test asks what share of revenue is truly AI-driven, watching for ordinary projects relabeled as AI. Operations asks how mature AI is in delivery and in internal work. The regulatory test covers data residency, sovereign AI and compliance with AI ethics rules.

Software buyouts face a harder setting. Bain reports that growth in US public software revenue has fallen from about 20 percent to roughly 10 percent by 2025. It also reports that technology buyouts from 2020 to 2022 are tracking a median purchase multiple of 2.1 times, against 2.9 times for entries from 2010 to 2019. Diligence now has to assess how far AI could affect or replace the workflows that a product supports, and it should ask for evidence of AI traction, not only strategy statements. Traditional software metrics such as annual recurring revenue and net revenue retention mislead for AI products that are priced on usage and outcomes. Bain recommends tracking traditional machine learning, AI add-ons and agent-based products as separate revenue groups, each with its own compute and talent costs.
