## Summary

Forrester's research treats marketing technology, known as martech, as a strategic asset and not a tactical purchase. This chapter explains why chief marketing officers should own it personally, what capabilities a stack needs and how an eight-stage cycle keeps investment aligned with business goals. It shows why the stack should be built as a flexible ecosystem of connected parts and not bought as one vendor's suite, and how the market for customer analytics is changing. It ends with the go-to-market strategy that the technology must serve, and a maturity assessment that aligns sales, marketing and product around the audience.

## Key Ideas

- Martech influences revenue, customer engagement and brand more directly than almost any other technology category, so it is a strategic asset and not a tactical purchase.
- Integration is the top priority for 91 percent of enterprise martech users, yet only 19 percent believe one vendor's suite can supply everything they need.
- Chief marketing officers should own the martech vision themselves instead of delegating it to IT.
- An eight-stage cycle of initiate, audit, analyze, propose, prioritize, plan, execute and govern keeps martech aligned with business goals.
- The stack should become a modular ecosystem built around interoperability, data, orchestration and generative AI, and vendor consolidation should be an outcome and not a goal.
- Go-to-market strategies built around internal structures, such as product lines and sales regions, underperform those built around market segments and buyers.

## Marketing Technology and Go-to-Market Strategy

### A strategic mission

Forrester argues that treating martech, which is short for marketing technology, as a tactical solution undersells it, because few technology categories influence revenue, customer engagement and brand so directly. It names four dimensions of a strategic mission. Martech must engage customers, by orchestrating relevant experiences across the entire journey and not only at separate touchpoints. It must help marketers, through usable interfaces, fewer silos and visible insight into what works. It must build differentiated intellectual property that competitors cannot copy, such as strong identity resolution, which links the different identifiers of one person, and which enables second-party data sharing in co-op marketing. And it must prepare marketers for continuous change without replacing the entire platform.

The stack was under strain even in 2021. Of enterprise martech users, 91 percent named integration as the most important feature of a marketing tool, yet only 19 percent of the customers of enterprise marketing suites believed that one vendor could deliver every capability. Marketers were caught between buying a suite and buying the best product in each category. Forrester recommends organizing priorities around value creation and not around adding features. That means removing the digital divide in the stack, because disconnected components do active harm. It means giving less emphasis to commodity functions such as sending email, buying display ads and building dashboards. And it means owning the strategically important systems, which are customer analytics, as the system of insight, and cross-channel campaign management, as the system of engagement. These generate the proprietary knowledge that a customer-obsessed organization must control. See [Martech Stack and Go-to-Market Strategy](#/concept/martech-stack-strategy).

### What the stack must do

Six interdependent capabilities define next-generation martech.

- **Customer data management** makes accessible, consistent data the foundation.
- **Identity resolution** assembles a unified picture of each customer across touchpoints.
- **Experience optimization** improves entire journeys, instead of improving campaigns or channels one at a time.
- **Contextual content** is an infrastructure that can create, manage, find and reuse creative material for specific moments on any device.
- **Journey analytics** moves measurement beyond clicks, toward journey-level measures such as satisfaction and lifetime value.
- **Unified orchestration** designs the journey in one coordinated workflow.

Near-term steps do not need to wait for vendors. Marketers can take an inventory of the current stack, weight buying criteria toward integration over standalone function, and document an identity graph that lists which identifiers are used for which interactions. They can also benchmark campaign and business measures to support continuous monitoring. One regional retail bank replaced three tools at once, to validate that they worked together.

### The chief marketing officer's role

A 2022 guide makes the case personal. There is no marketing without martech, which turns strategy into live, insight-driven engagement. Yet most companies still plan and deliver it in a decentralized, ad hoc way, buying tools one at a time and not building a deliberately designed system. Chief marketing officers have the largest stake in it, and they should take hands-on ownership of the vision and the management, and not hand them to IT. One mobile operator's chief marketing officer describes the commitment as about six months of self-education, at four to eight hours a week.

Beyond the commodity functions of data management, insight generation and campaign execution, a full stack must deliver five business capabilities.

- **Engagement velocity** is the speed and volume needed for real-time experience.
- **Personalization** uses tailored content, reach across channels and algorithms that choose channel, timing and offer.
- **Cohesive journeys** rely on unified profiles that both the insight and the engagement systems can see.
- **Agility** comes from an architecture that can be swapped and upgraded, using public cloud, low-code tools and headless technology, which separates the content from the way it is displayed.
- **Protection** means built-in or integrated data security, preference management, consent and privacy.

Four architectural pillars organize these capabilities. The data fabric collects, manages and assembles internal and external data into unified customer profiles. Customer understanding is the analysis layer of reporting, analytics, targeting and testing. Brand strategy covers the planning, resource, content and performance management that connect strategy to execution. Brand experience is the engagement layer that orchestrates lifecycle interactions across marketing, advertising and loyalty touchpoints.

Years of tactical, reactive decisions have left most stacks fragmented, so building a complete, cohesive stack is a very large undertaking that few companies fully achieve. Those that do gain an advantage. Forrester finds three differentiators and adds a fourth. The first is an enterprise martech strategy, which is a unified plan that also funds future innovation. The second is integration, which is both the top vendor-selection criterion and martech's largest technical shortcoming. It covers unified profiles, workflows across applications and orchestration across touchpoints. The third is data management and identity resolution that rest on a documented inventory of current and planned data needs, backed by real processes for collecting, cleaning and matching data. The fourth is staffing, in the form of a dedicated marketing technologist who owns strategy, roadmap and vendor relationships.

### Keeping the stack aligned

A 2023 framework treats alignment as an evolving, iterative process and not a one-time project. It is built to withstand four recurring sources of misalignment.

- **Siloed decisions** let choices that suit one department create duplication for others who were not consulted.
- **Mergers and acquisitions** force together infrastructures with different purchasing histories and cultures, which causes duplication, conflict over who owns processes and disrupted supplier relationships.
- **New technologies** arrive faster than governance can absorb them.
- **New strategic initiatives** require processes and technologies to change in ways that can stall the growth engine they were meant to speed up.

The framework runs through eight stages in two phases.

In the first phase, discovery and analysis, there are four stages.

1. **Initiate** secures agreement across the organization on what alignment means in terms of shared business objectives, with a cross-functional charter and a common reference model for martech.
2. **Audit** sets a baseline of the current infrastructure, by mapping tools to capabilities, processes and stakeholders and scoring their functionality and adoption. It is repeated periodically.
3. **Analyze** compares the baseline with the desired future state, and decides whether to close each gap through people, process, technology or a combination.
4. **Propose** builds a business case for each project, with estimated time, cost, benefit and risk.

In the second phase, execution and optimization, there are four more.

5. **Prioritize** evaluates proposals against guidelines and business objectives, selects those with the greatest effect across the organization and secures authorization.
6. **Plan** builds roadmaps with resources, financial projections, milestones and measures tied to the business case.
7. **Execute** carries out the plans, explains their purpose and benefit and highlights interim wins in phased rollouts.
8. **Govern** reviews the infrastructure, sets responsibility and accountability, records lessons, watches vendors' roadmaps for new capability and uses Forrester's maturity model to decide where to focus.

Govern feeds back into a renewed Initiate as business needs, vendor capabilities and market conditions change.

### From suite to ecosystem

A March 2024 report argues that legacy martech was built to set up and deliver outbound, mostly email-centered campaigns, and it must now evolve for the devices, channels and touchpoints where consumers engage. The new requirements are of four kinds. The first is a data strategy that puts the customer first, with a shift toward data that customers share directly, identity resolution and privacy-respecting profiles. The second is a lasting need for personalization. The third is a demand that martech investment visibly contribute to the business. The fourth is support for invisible experiences that anticipate customer needs. Legacy stacks fall short. Integration still tops feature priorities, at 91 percent, while only 19 percent believe a suite can cover their needs. Automation and access remain inefficient, which forces analysts to rebuild data objects and move data between systems for each campaign. Few stacks were built for today's volume of real-time decisions. One data leader at AARP described tens of millions of engagement decisions a day, based on billions of pieces of information.

Forrester's answer is to redefine martech as an ecosystem. This is an interconnected system of data, applications and services that supports consumer marketing strategy, insight and engagement. It is distinguished by integration and modularity, and not by one monolithic platform. That lets marketers adapt to unresolved questions about data, privacy and consumer preference without replatforming, build capability step by step, reduce a single vendor's bargaining power and tune capabilities to the needs of the company.

Four elements deserve investment.

- **Interoperability** covers integration, workflow and data connectivity, so that modules work as one cohesive unit.
- **Data** is the foundation, and it needs consolidated profiles, real-time streaming, enrichment connections and identity resolution. As one Google martech manager noted, whatever sits on top can be flexible, but the data systems must be smart.
- **Orchestration** is a unified facility for assembling audiences, communications and triggered interactions across touchpoints and lifecycle phases.
- **Generative AI**, already used by 26 percent of respondents with another 40 percent exploring, extends into automated insight, streamlined operations and generated journeys.

Realizing the ecosystem takes organizational change. Consolidation should be an outcome of good planning and not a goal, because no single vendor has delivered both technical efficiency and marketing effectiveness. Strong partnerships should be valued across IT, enterprise architecture and data science. Governance and planning should be dynamic. Data strategy should come before any technology choice and outlast it. And the circle of collaborators should widen to include the chief financial officer, for purchasing, business cases and performance management, and customer experience counterparts, for coordinating the technology and strategy of increasingly invisible journeys.

### The market for customer analytics

Forrester's 2024 landscape of the vendors that supply customer analytics shows several shifts. Buying has widened from the chief marketing officer to customer experience teams, digital product teams and IT. Offerings have moved from tools for scarce data scientists toward ready-made models that do the work for the user, a path that Salesforce Einstein began in 2016, alongside custom modeling. Legacy categories, namely customer relationship management, customer data platforms, product analytics and digital intelligence, are converging as vendors compete for the same buyers.

Siloed data that gives a fractured view of the customer is the main challenge. Generative AI, which lets users query data and build segments in natural language, is the main disruptor. The core uses are a single view of the customer, retention and personalization. Forrester describes an internal war for ownership of the customer, with a unified view as the prize. It expects vendors to move from insight to prescription. A vendor might, for example, recommend a campaign for a segment of high-value customers who are likely to leave, and identify that segment automatically.

### Go-to-market strategy

The technology serves a go-to-market strategy, which Forrester's March 2024 maturity report treats as the root of many underperforming business-to-business efforts. Strategies have historically been built around internal structures, such as product lines, business units and sales regions, and not around external ones, such as market segments and target audiences. That mismatch is exposed as buyer behavior changes and digital channels multiply. When executed well, a strategy drives growth by aligning sales, marketing and product around shared core decisions.

The Go-To-Market Maturity Assessment scores four areas on a scale from 1 to 5 of agreement.

- **Business context** asks whether a well-defined decision process that starts from the audience exists, with alignment among product, marketing and sales leaders.
- **Market strategy** asks whether the most viable segments are identified and prioritized with internal data and outside research, and not by intuition.
- **Buyer strategy** asks whether buying centers, buying groups and priority personas are documented well enough to guide buyer-focused engagement and to show which offerings are best placed.
- **Engagement strategy** asks whether activities are coordinated into one cohesive buyer experience, through persona profiles, buyer's-journey maps and buyer messaging.

Portfolio marketing usually coordinates the assessment, which guides beginner, intermediate and advanced organizations differently.
