## Summary

The Economist argues that America's affordability crisis is mostly a mirage, because real wages have risen steadily for a decade and are near record highs across income groups, especially for the poorest. This chapter explains why perceptions differ from the data, which is because people focus on price levels and not on rates of change, and because they credit wage gains to their own skill and blame price rises on outside forces. It then covers the real exceptions in housing, mortgage rates and electricity, and the political pressure for price caps. It ends with the Economist's warning that price controls distort markets and tend to become permanent.

## Key Ideas

- Real wages in America have risen steadily for a decade and are near record highs across the income spectrum, and they have risen most for the poorest.
- Price levels are about 25 percent higher than before the pandemic, and people focus on levels, while the data show rates of change.
- People credit wage gains to their own skill and blame price rises on outside forces.
- The strongest real grievances are in housing in superstar cities and in the cost of borrowing, and electricity is the one essential that has outpaced inflation.
- Polling shows overwhelming support for caps on prices, but the Economist argues that price controls distort markets and tend to become permanent.
- The Economist says the policies that are actually raising prices are tariffs, tax cuts financed by deficits and crackdowns on immigration.

## The US Affordability Debate

### Why affordability became a slogan

The Economist's year-end briefing for 2025 asks why affordability became the central political slogan for the 2026 midterm elections. Its answer starts from the fact that the data do not show a crisis in household purchasing power. President Trump won the 2024 election partly by promising to lower prices. This was a pledge that could not be kept, because price falls across an entire economy are unprecedented outside deep recessions. His tariffs have raised prices further. Democrats, from Zohran Mamdani's rent-freeze campaign to the utility-cost pledges of moderate governors, have adopted the theme. See [US Affordability Debate](#/concept/us-affordability-debate).

The administration's response is to deny that affordability is a problem. This is politically ineffective, although on the economics it has a point. Real wages have kept rising since the inflation that followed the pandemic, which was driven more by excess stimulus than by supply shocks. Low earners gained most in the tight labor market. The Economist judges the claim that Americans can afford less than before to be essentially false.

### Category by category

The briefing finds the strongest real grievances in housing and in borrowing costs.

- **Groceries.** Prices have largely tracked overall inflation, apart from eggs.
- **Electricity.** It is the one essential that has outpaced inflation, by more than ten points since 2019. The likely causes are the modernization of the grid and rising exports of liquefied natural gas. An Economist analysis found no link between the concentration of data centers and state energy prices. Petrol prices have fallen by more than electricity has risen.
- **Housing.** It is the strongest case. In superstar cities, scarce land and restrictive zoning make ownership punishing. The mortgage, insurance and tax on the average property in Manhattan or Brooklyn exceed 90 percent of average household income before tax. Elsewhere, most counties were affordable by the rule of thumb of 30 percent of income until mortgage rates rose in 2022. The squeeze comes from mortgage rates and not from prices, although the average rate on all outstanding mortgages is only 4.3 percent, nearly two points below the rate on new mortgages.

### Why perceptions differ from the data

Inflation is running at about 2.8 percent, a modest overshoot of the Federal Reserve's target of 2 percent. The anger that persists therefore needs other explanations.

- **Levels, not rates.** Price levels remain about 25 percent higher than before the pandemic, and people feel the level and not the rate of change.
- **Credit and blame.** People attribute wage gains to their own merit and blame prices on outside forces, according to surveys by Stefanie Stantcheva.
- **Volatility.** Prices in individual categories were volatile, so some purchase was always spiking.
- **Borrowing costs.** They matter more to sentiment than conventional measures of inflation capture, according to Harvard and IMF research.

Stanford researchers estimate that anger about inflation has a half-life of about one year, which suggests that it should have faded by now. The shock after decades of flat prices seems to be different.

### Phantom and real problems

The Economist's leader generalizes the argument. Affordability combines phantom problems, such as milk, haircuts and fondness for the prices of 2019, with real problems that arise from affluence.

- **A shift to services.** Goods fell from 60 percent to under 40 percent of household consumption. Wages rising in services with low productivity show up as higher prices.
- **Assets that outran wages.** Wealth has risen faster than wages, which makes goods that are positional, meaning they depend on rank, feel unaffordable even to high earners. The leader's example is two equally paid people, one of whom inherited $1 million that is now $4 million in an index fund, and who competes for something such as an apartment with a view.

These problems are hard for policymakers. Solutions such as easing permits for housing and wind farms, ending excessive occupational licensing and lowering tariffs are favored in principle, but local interests that are protected oppose them.

### The danger of misdiagnosis

Polling shows overwhelming support for caps on grocery prices, rents and credit-card fees. The Economist argues that price controls distort markets and tend to become permanent. Richard Nixon introduced them and then abandoned them. It identifies tariffs, tax cuts financed by deficits and crackdowns on immigration as the policies that are actually raising prices. It also criticizes the combination of a fiscal boost from tax cuts with pressure on the Federal Reserve to cut rates. And it criticizes President Trump's proposed $2,000 checks funded by tariffs. Together, these would tax imports and then hand out money to buy them.

Bharat Ramamurti, a former aide to President Biden, and Neale Mahoney describe an affordability conundrum. Voters want relief immediately, while the tools on the supply side work slowly. They argue for pairing reforms with price controls. The Economist rejects this as perilous. The briefing concludes that the only politically unsatisfying path is time, as inflation expectations reset and real wages keep rising.
