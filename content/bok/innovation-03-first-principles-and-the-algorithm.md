## Summary

Elon Musk is reported to solve large engineering and manufacturing problems with a five-step process that former Tesla president Jon McNeill calls the Algorithm. The steps are to question every requirement, delete every possible step or part, simplify what remains, speed up the cycle time and then automate. This chapter explains the first-principles thinking behind it, the habit of driving one or two urgent issues relentlessly through recurring reviews and how Musk is applying the approach to Terafab, a planned chip factory. It also records the skepticism of the source, which notes that the same playbook has produced goals that were abandoned.

## Key Ideas

- The Algorithm is a five-step sequence, and the order matters. Question requirements first, delete next, simplify, speed up and automate last.
- It rests on first-principles thinking, which means breaking a problem down to its most basic elements and not reasoning by analogy from earlier solutions.
- A less formal ingredient is injected urgency. One or two existential issues are driven relentlessly, week after week, through recurring reviews.
- Musk is applying the approach to Terafab, a planned chip factory costing $20 billion or more, to remove a single point of failure in chip supply.
- The source is skeptical, because the same playbook produced ideas that fizzled, such as Tesla's abandoned goal of 20 million vehicles a year.

## First Principles and the Algorithm

### The five steps

The Algorithm is documented by Jon McNeill, a former president of Tesla, in his book of the same name. It was first described publicly in Walter Isaacson's 2023 biography of Musk. It became a named process during the difficult ramp-up of the Model 3 and the scaling of the Model X, when the pattern was routine enough that an executive suggested a name so that it could be communicated consistently across the company. It has five steps.

1. **Question every requirement.** Even a specification that seems fixed is treated as open to challenge and not as given.
2. **Delete every possible step or part.** Anything not proven necessary is removed, and not merely optimized.
3. **Simplify and optimize what remains.** Optimization comes only after the unnecessary elements are gone.
4. **Accelerate the cycle time.** The remaining process is made faster.
5. **Automate.** Automation is deliberately placed last, because automating a flawed or unnecessarily complex process only makes the flaws move faster.

### First-principles thinking

The Algorithm is rooted in first-principles thinking. McNeill describes it as breaking a problem down to its lowest common denominator, or atomic level, instead of reasoning by analogy from the way similar problems were solved before.

### Injected urgency

A less formal ingredient of the Algorithm is urgency. Musk fixates on one or two existential issues and drives them relentlessly, week after week, through recurring engineering reviews. McNeill's theory is that the chief executives of competitors do not run their organizations at the same pace. A competitive advantage then builds up from sustained tempo alone.

### Terafab

Musk is applying the Algorithm to Terafab, a planned chip factory shared by Tesla and SpaceX. It is estimated to cost $20 billion or more. It is intended to exceed the combined output of every existing chip factory in the world. Musk's justification is that current global suppliers together can produce only about 2 percent of what his companies need. The plan is also part of a shift toward building data centers in space, where Musk argues that solar power will eventually be cheaper than on Earth. Those needs include Tesla's robotaxis and robots, SpaceX's AI data centers and xAI.

The logic is about removing a single point of failure. Three separate businesses depend on the chip supply, and suppliers are unwilling to expand capacity as fast as Musk wants. His conclusion is that nothing requires him to buy chips from anyone else. In his words, either the company builds the factory or it does not have the chips.

An earlier application of the same pattern came before the Model 3. After the success of the Model S, Musk expected that the world would not produce enough electric-vehicle batteries to meet demand. He began building a giant battery factory. He eventually convinced the supplier Panasonic to open a large facility in Nevada, which became critical to the Model 3's success.

### The skeptic's view

The source is explicitly skeptical of the Terafab plan. It draws on the same playbook that lay behind some of Musk's ideas that fizzled. One was Tesla's abandoned goal of 20 million vehicles a year, against 1.6 million vehicles actually delivered. The plan also arrives as Musk prepares to take SpaceX public, which raises the stakes of whether this application of the Algorithm succeeds.
