## Summary

Research on roughly 6,000 people in teams rated as high-performing or average found that a culture of experimentation predicts success better than a concentration of talent. This chapter describes the five habits that keep such teams improving, and why catching problems early separates the best teams most clearly. It then turns to disagreement, which stays constructive because of the words people use more than their private intentions. It explains two gaps that undermine good intentions, five tested ways of speaking that close them and how organizations can train, measure and hire for the skill.

## High-Performing Teams and Constructive Disagreement

### What high-performing teams do

The psychologist and workplace researcher Ron Friedman studied roughly 6,000 people in teams that their organizations had identified as either high-performing or average. He looked for the habits that most reliably separated the two groups. The strongest predictor of success was not a concentration of talent. It was a culture of experimentation. See [High-Performing Team Practices](#/concept/high-performing-team-practices).

The teams that keep improving share five habits that reinforce one another.

1. **They try new approaches routinely.** They run small, low-stakes experiments all the time, and do not wait for a formal initiative or a crisis to justify change. The Oklahoma City Thunder built its roster years ago around analytics and defense, before similar approaches spread across basketball. This shows a team willing to test unconventional ideas ahead of consensus.
2. **They value curiosity over ego.** Members treat being wrong as useful information and not as a threat to their status. This measurably increases how often problems and disagreements are voiced instead of suppressed.
3. **They surface problems early.** This habit separates high performers from average teams most sharply. Problems caught early are cheap to fix, and problems caught late compound. Teams with the psychological safety to raise a concern before it becomes a crisis consistently outperform teams in which concerns wait for a formal review.
4. **They stay close to the actual work.** Leaders and senior members keep direct, hands-on contact with front-line execution and do not manage only through reports and dashboards. This speeds up the detection of problems and opportunities that would otherwise appear only after a significant delay.
5. **They invest in people's growth.** Skill development is treated as a team investment, and not as an individual's private responsibility. It reinforces the other four habits, because teams that are confident their members are growing take more experimental risks, raise more problems and stay curious for longer.

Growth rests on feedback that is frequent and specific, and not saved for formal reviews. Adobe overhauled its performance-review process, and Spotify built a platform called Confidence. Both aim to replace infrequent, high-stakes evaluation with continuous, lower-stakes feedback.

### Two gaps that undermine good intentions

The words people use, more than their private intentions, decide whether a workplace disagreement stays constructive. Research identifies two gaps. See [Constructive Disagreement Language](#/concept/constructive-disagreement-language).

The first is the intention-behavior gap. People who sincerely mean to be curious and respectful often cannot keep it up under the emotional pressure of an active disagreement. In the researchers' studies, participants agreed that expressing curiosity was a good idea, and they still fell into disguised argument. An example is asking "How can you believe that?" as though it were a real question.

The second is the behavior-perception gap. Even behavior that is carried out well may not be read as intended. A question meant to show curiosity, such as "Why would you think that?", can land as a sarcastic taunt. The researchers found a consistent disconnect between how curious and receptive speakers rated their own messages and how readers rated the same messages.

### Five techniques that close the gaps

Five linguistic techniques, developed and tested across a decade of experiments, close both gaps.

1. **State curiosity directly.** Say "I'm curious how you think about this," and do not imply curiosity through rhetorical questions.
2. **Acknowledge the other side.** Restate the core of their position before responding. This shows that you were listening, even if you do not agree.
3. **Make common ground explicit.** Say "we both want," because some shared basis almost always exists, even in sharp disagreement.
4. **Hedge claims.** Say "from my view," and do not assert certainty. The average person is wrong at least half the time in factual disputes, and hedging reads as more thoughtful and not as weaker.
5. **Share a personal story behind a belief.** In a controlled experiment about allocating bonuses, vulnerability built trust more effectively than citing research or data.

A study of 1,113 people compared three groups. One group was taught the specific words and phrases for showing receptiveness to an opposing view. A second group received no guidance, and a third received the classic advice to consider the other side's perspective and be empathetic. Their disagreement partners rated those taught the specific language as more objective, intelligent and trustworthy, and were more willing to work with them again. Only concrete language produced behavior that others could observe and judge.

### Language as the lever

Language, and not nonverbal behavior, is the right lever for an organization. Most professional communication now happens in writing, in email, chat and text, where nonverbal cues do not apply. Language is also easier to control than tone or body language and less likely to be misread, although words can still be misunderstood.

Organizations can put this to work in four ways.

- **Teach explicit verbal skills** and not abstract advice about mindset. Train people to express curiosity in words, and do not just tell them to be curious.
- **Have leaders model the behaviors** in team meetings, one-to-one conversations and town halls. Receptiveness is contagious, because people who are treated respectfully during a disagreement tend to respond in kind.
- **Use technology to measure and reinforce the right language at scale.** Tools include monitors that detect toxic language, dashboards that give feedback after a conversation and AI chatbots as practice partners. Employees are often more willing to practice disagreement with an AI than with a colleague or a boss.
- **Include the skill in hiring and promotion,** in the same way as other soft skills. Harvard's approach is to ask applicants to describe how they handled a real disagreement.
