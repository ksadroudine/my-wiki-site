## Summary

Customer centricity means organizing a company around the lifetime value of its customers and not around the products it sells. This chapter explains why customers differ greatly in value and why firms should invest more in those worth the most. It describes the customer-base audit and the five questions that structure it, the measures of customer lifetime value and customer equity, and the mistakes that undermine decisions based on them. It also covers customer portfolio management, which groups customers by the strength of their relationship with the firm as acquaintances, friends and partners.

## Key Ideas

- A product-centric company builds a product and sells it to as many customers as possible. A customer-centric company aligns its products and services with the needs of its most valuable customers.
- Customer equity, which is the sum of the lifetime values of all current and future customers, is the recommended measure of whether a strategy is truly customer-centric.
- A customer-base audit arranges transaction history by customer and by time, so that behavior can be studied cohort by cohort and not as one average.
- The most common mistakes are treating all customers as equally valuable and spending too much on acquisition compared with retention.
- A customer-centric organization is built around customer segments, not around product lines.
- In one retailer, "partners" were 20 percent of customers and produced 45 percent of the value of the customer portfolio.

## Customer Centricity

### Products or customers

Peter Fader and his coauthors argue that a firm should organize itself around maximizing the lifetime value of its customers, and not around selling as much as possible of its existing products. The more common default is product-centricity. A company builds a product or product line and then tries to sell it to as many customers as possible, and it treats all customers roughly alike, however differently valuable they are over time.

The proposed replacement for product revenue or market share is customer equity. It is the sum of the projected lifetime values of a company's entire current and future customer base. Strategic questions then change. Which customers to win, how much to spend winning them and which products to build next are judged by their effect on this total, and not by next quarter's unit sales. ### The customer-base audit

The customer-base audit makes the idea operational. It organizes a company's raw transaction history as a data cube, indexed by customer and by time. An analyst can then study cohorts, which are groups of customers won in the same period, and see how each group behaves over its life. Aggregate averages can hide sharply different groups of customers.

The audit follows five questions, called the Five Lenses.

1. How many customers does the firm have, and is the number growing or shrinking?
2. How much is the customer base worth in total?
3. How much of that value has already been captured, and how much remains to be captured?
4. How does value differ across identifiable customer segments?
5. How does the firm's customer value compare with competitors and the wider market?

Taken together, the answers show where growth or decline actually comes from. Growth may come from new customers, from keeping existing customers or from higher spending by existing customers. Total revenue alone tends to hide which.

### Customer lifetime value

Customer lifetime value, or CLV, is the projected net profit that a firm expects to earn from a customer over the entire relationship. It is used to decide how much can profitably be spent to win a similar customer, how much to invest in retention and even which product features are worth building.

The Customer Centricity Playbook lists five mistakes that undermine decisions based on CLV.

- The firm treats all customers as equally valuable, when the distribution of customer value is typically heavy-tailed, with a small share of customers producing most of the value.
- It over-invests in acquisition compared with retention, although improvements in retention often compound into larger gains in lifetime value.
- It ignores how small changes in the retention rate compound over a customer's full lifetime.
- It calculates CLV only in aggregate, and not by cohort or segment, which hides which types of customer drive value.
- It uses estimates that cover too short a period, which understates the value of long-standing, loyal customers.

### Four tenets of a customer-centric firm

The three books by Fader and his coauthors agree on four tenets that separate a genuinely customer-centric organization from one that only talks about customer experience.

1. Customers differ enormously in the value they generate for the firm.
2. Customer lifetime value is the main forward-looking measure of performance, in place of revenue, market share or satisfaction scores alone.
3. Marketing, product and service resources move toward the highest-value existing customers and toward prospects who resemble them.
4. The organization itself is restructured around customer segments and not around product lines. A product-centric organization chart works against customer-centric decisions, even when individual campaigns are customer-focused.

### Customer portfolio management

Fred Selnes and Michael Johnson offer a practical alternative to the CLV method, which is based on relationship strength. Traditional needs-based segmentation is static and assumes that a customer belongs permanently to one segment. Customer portfolio management instead groups customers dynamically, by the strength of their relationship with the brand.

- **Acquaintances** provide economies of scale and a pipeline of future loyal customers.
- **Friends** are the group between acquaintances and partners.
- **Partners** generate the greatest margins and future cash flows.

The central metaphor is a choice between two buckets. One is smaller and watertight, and holds only loyal, profitable customers. The other is larger and leaky, and spans weaker and stronger relationships. The research found that the larger, leaky bucket is typically more profitable in the long run than narrowing the base to the strongest relationships.

A European building-materials retailer illustrates the idea. Its partners were 20 percent of customers and produced 45 percent of the value of the portfolio, which is more than six times the value of a low-spending acquaintance. The analysis projected that converting acquaintances and friends into partners would raise total portfolio value by 122 percent. The most useful finding was at store level. Across 29 stores with identical product ranges, layouts and marketing programs, the share of customers who were friends or partners varied from 78 percent at the best store to 13 percent at the weakest.
