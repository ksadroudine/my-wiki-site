## Summary

Core connectivity has become table stakes with slowing growth, so telecom operators are looking for new revenue in consumer ecosystems, enterprise IT services, network APIs, private 5G, sustainability and AI infrastructure. This chapter explains why the old growth engines are exhausted and what operators already earn from adjacent businesses. It shows that business customers are willing to buy beyond connectivity, with security and agentic AI driving demand, but that operators have not yet turned that preference into delivery. It also describes the four ways operators can supply AI infrastructure and the operating model that growth beyond the core requires.

## Key Ideas

- Operators hold a structural advantage for new services, because they have real-time data on usage, devices and location, trusted customer relationships and ubiquitous infrastructure.
- The old consumer growth engines are exhausted. Unlimited plans have used up the room for upselling, and price rises keep less than half of their value.
- About 80 percent of business decision-makers see operators as viable partners beyond connectivity, and security is the most durable driver of demand.
- Operators have not yet turned this preference into delivery, and nearly half of enterprises are dissatisfied with operators' connectivity and IT delivery.
- Network APIs create value only when they work across networks, which requires cooperation among competitors.
- The main barrier is organizational and not strategic, because structures built for scale and cost lack the speed that ecosystem plays need.

## Growth Beyond Connectivity

### Why the old engines have stalled

Operators hold a structural advantage for growth beyond connectivity. Their central role in the digital economy gives them real-time visibility into network usage, device behavior, location and consumption at a scale that few industries match. Together with trusted customer relationships and infrastructure that is everywhere, this lets them move from selling separate connectivity products to delivering integrated services that add value. See [Telecom Growth Beyond Core Connectivity](#/concept/telecom-growth-beyond-core).

The incentive to do so is strong, because the traditional engines of consumer growth have stalled.

- Unlimited voice and data plans have used up the room for upselling.
- Fiber is turning what was once a premium gigabit product into a commodity.
- Fixed-mobile convergence, which means selling fixed and mobile services together, has passed 50 percent of customers in markets such as Belgium, Portugal and Spain.
- Headline price increases keep less than half of their value, because customers move to cheaper offers for new customers.

Core connectivity has become table stakes, with growth projected below 5 percent a year by 2026. Beyond-core areas such as security, cloud and agentic AI are growing two to three times faster.

### Consumer ecosystems

On the consumer side, growth takes the form of vertical ecosystems. They bundle adjacent services, often supplied by third parties, around the connectivity relationship. The services include security, insurance, energy, entertainment, financial technology and health. About 70 percent of consumers say that they would buy at least one adjacent service from their operator.

The revenue is already real. NTT Docomo earns more than 20 percent of its revenue beyond its core. Telus draws a quarter of its revenue from digital businesses for specific industries, such as healthcare, agriculture and back-office services. Spain's MasOrange built an energy service controlled by an app, which it later sold for 90 million euros. It also built an insurance business that targets more than 7.5 million policies, and a home-security business with ADT. Movistar's home-security venture with Prosegur gained more than 400,000 customers in its first year. Leading operators now earn 5 to 40 percent of their revenue from adjacent businesses, roughly 1 to 15 billion euros a year. These models need limited capital, because they use existing retail, billing, care and field assets. They earn high margins, and they deepen the core relationship, because customers with several bundled services leave less often.

Sustainability is a less obvious adjacency for consumers. Operators' connectivity assets, consumer data, stores, field technicians and relationships in the home put them in a position to offer 5G sustainability applications, net-zero home solutions and green cloud services. Consumers say they would pay a premium of 15 to 30 percent for sustainably produced services.

### Enterprise services

On the enterprise side, growth comes from three sources.

- **ICT services** extend connectivity into cloud, cybersecurity and managed IT services. They can reach as much as 20 percent of an operator's revenue. One European operator more than doubled its cloud-related revenue and earnings within four years by upgrading its infrastructure, training its staff and forming key partnerships.
- **Network APIs** expose standardized capabilities, such as speed on demand, low delay and discovery of edge computing, to enterprise developers. The developers build applications that need guaranteed and not best-effort performance, such as fraud prevention, glitch-free video conferencing and remotely operated industrial equipment. McKinsey estimates $100 to $300 billion of connectivity and edge-computing revenue over five to seven years, plus $10 to $30 billion from the APIs themselves.
- **Private 5G** networks are customized for uses such as smart hospitals and factories, and operators such as T-Mobile, O2, Vodafone and KPN are launching them.

A network API that works on only one operator's footprint is far less valuable, so the opportunity needs cooperation among competitors. Deutsche Telekom, AT&T, Orange, Singtel, Telstra and Verizon formed a joint venture with Ericsson to sell interoperable APIs worldwide. In Brazil, three operators, TIM, Claro and Vivo, provide banks with mobile-number verification, SIM-swap detection and device-location checks through the GSMA Open Gateway framework. The service reaches about 150 million customers, and the SIM-swap API alone generated $9 million.

### What business customers say

McKinsey's surveys of business buyers, published in February 2025, June 2025 and February 2026, consistently find that roughly 80 percent of decision-makers see operators as viable partners beyond core connectivity. The first edition described four strategic plays, with combined potential of $160 billion of additional revenue by 2028.

1. **A connectivity play** refocuses the core on near-core products such as software-defined wide-area networking.
2. **An ICT play** bundles cybersecurity and cloud.
3. **A network API play** sells the capabilities of 5G networks.
4. **An ecosystem-building play** creates stand-alone businesses in industries such as healthcare or automotive, as Telstra Health and Vodafone Automotive do.

The second edition found cybersecurity to be the most compelling priority for business buyers across regions and company sizes. It is also the leading reason why customers switch providers, ahead of brand, product capability and price. It recorded a shift to digital buying. About one in four customers planned to switch vendors within a year, mainly to cloud providers and technology manufacturers.

The latest edition adds agentic AI as the main new variable. Security and agentic AI are now the fastest-growing areas of spending, each growing above 9 percent a year while core connectivity grows below 5 percent. The more mature a customer is in agentic AI, the more it is willing to transact digitally. Each step up the maturity curve is linked to a rise of 20 to 45 percent in the value that customers will transact end to end online. Data privacy and sovereignty have become a gating requirement and not a differentiator, as agents gain broader access to enterprise data. More than half of respondents name it their top concern, and nearly two-thirds require private cloud or deployment on their own premises. Although 98 percent of customers are open to AI-assisted service in at least one interaction, only 3 to 11 percent report full-scale deployment for any use case. No type of provider has established a dominant position, whether foundation-model vendors, system integrators or operators. The opening for operators is therefore real but unproven.

Accenture's independent survey of more than 1,200 enterprise leaders agrees and sharpens the point about delivery. Of the enterprises, 73 percent rank operators among their top three providers of integrated business solutions. More than 70 percent prefer operators as partners for local and connected AI data centers, because of the proximity of data, the security posture and the existing network. And 60 percent are interested in working with operators on AI agents and on AI as a service tailored to an industry. Yet nearly half are dissatisfied with operators' connectivity and with their digital and IT delivery. They cite self-service provisioning of the network, standardized APIs for configuration and total cost of ownership. The limit is therefore the operators' delivery and not customer demand. Accenture recommends building a connected infrastructure platform that joins connectivity, cloud, edge and AI into one continuum, moving to an outcome-based, partner-driven model that balances direct sales with ecosystem engagement, and redesigning the operating and talent model to fit the new portfolio.

### Supplying the AI economy

The AI economy gives operators a supplier's role as well. They can build and monetize the fiber, space, power and GPU capacity that AI workloads need, and not only connect users to AI services. Global demand for data centers is projected to more than triple by 2030. Operators' geographic reach and experience of running large networks under variable demand fit that need. Inaction risks leaving operators further behind the hyperscalers and new entrants, which captured most of the value from the last decade's growth in data use while operators' revenues stayed flat.

McKinsey describes four paths that operators can pursue singly or together, depending on market structure, assets and appetite for risk.

- **Fiber for new data centers** is a global market of $30 to $50 billion, because providers of shared facilities and hyperscalers plan more than 2,600 new data centers by the early 2030s. In much of Europe and Asia-Pacific a telecom license is required to lay fiber, which gives incumbents the first choice of which markets to serve directly and which to lease to hyperscalers.
- **Intelligent network services** are a next generation of software-defined networking. They help enterprises manage the $70 to $80 billion a year in cloud data-egress fees, and they could let operators move from declining usage-based business pricing toward value-based pricing. The market is too young to have a settled feature set or business model.
- **Converting unused space and power** in data centers or central offices into revenue, through colocation, sale and leaseback or revenue sharing, is the lowest-risk path. New data-center construction takes five or more years and many power grids are at capacity, so operators with underused assets have a multi-year window and need little new investment. A site should offer at least about 500 kilowatts of power and be able to be retrofitted for the density and liquid cooling that AI equipment needs.
- **GPU as a service** rents graphics processors by the hour or on reserved terms. It addresses a market of $35 to $70 billion by 2030, excluding the hyperscalers' share, with projected returns on invested capital of 6 to 14 percent. It is the most capital-intensive and risky path because of uncertain demand, competition from hyperscalers, fast technology cycles and possible falls in GPU prices. McKinsey recommends a modest first investment focused on anchor tenants and partners.

### The operating model that growth needs

Earlier attempts at growth beyond the core often produced scattered, underperforming side bets. The main remaining barrier is organizational and operational. Structures built for scale, reliability and cost efficiency lack the speed, experimentation and customer focus that ecosystem plays demand.

Success needs several things.

- A platform-led operating model with real investment in structure, process, people and technology.
- Accountability for profit and loss that is linked to the core business, and shared incentives.
- Once early results validate an industry, a platform-first offer built on orchestration, integration and bundling and not on stand-alone products.
- Partnerships with leaders in each industry, such as insurers, security firms and energy companies, and an internal integration platform that makes bundling and managing non-core services routine.

Each business also needs its own enablers. ICT growth needs a modular platform for full-stack solutions and a specialized enterprise sales force. Network APIs need common standards at the application layer and integration with hyperscaler platforms. Private 5G needs enterprise-grade access to spectrum, often developed with industrial or hyperscaler partners.
