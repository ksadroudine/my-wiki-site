## Summary

Lasting innovation capability comes from treating innovation as a permanent organizational system and not as an occasional program or a single clever bet. This chapter explains why most companies default to incremental innovation or poorly integrated acquisitions, the eight practices that build a strategic-innovation function and the three competencies it needs. It covers how to scale a proven transformational project inside a mature business and how to use outside innovation ecosystems. It also describes the three traps, which are identity, architecture and collaboration, that derail reinvention even when leaders know the threat.

## Key Ideas

- Most companies default to incremental improvement or to acquisitions and venture investments that are poorly connected to the core business, and neither secures the company's future.
- Strategic innovation should be a permanent function with its own governance. IBM's program generated more than $15 billion in revenue and was still shut down, because it was a temporary program.
- A strategic-innovation function needs three distinct competencies, which are discovery, incubation and acceleration, each with dedicated roles.
- Scaling a validated innovation inside a mature business is a separate challenge, which needs leadership attention, the right team, resources and high-stakes decisions made under uncertainty.
- Innovation ecosystems are place-based hubs for the earliest stage of innovation, and they complement a company's own capability and do not replace it.
- Organizations fall into three traps in sequence. The identity trap comes first, then the architecture trap and then the collaboration trap.

## Building Strategic Innovation Capability

### Why the defaults fail

Most companies innovate in one of two ways. They improve their existing product lines step by step, or they reach for innovation outside the core through acquisitions and venture investments that are poorly connected to the main business. Neither approach secures the company's long-term future. A strategic-innovation capability is something different. It is the organizational machinery for generating and commercializing new business models repeatedly. See [Organizational Innovation Capability](#/concept/organizational-innovation-capability).

### Eight practices

One study defines eight practices, in three groups.

The first three practices set direction and create commitment.

1. **Adopt a common language** for the innovation landscape, such as the three-horizons framework from McKinsey.
2. **Set explicit domains of innovation intent** that senior leadership commits to. Grundfos, for example, chose water-scarce cities as a domain.
3. **Make strategic innovation a permanent function,** governed by an innovation council, and not a temporary initiative.

The next three build the capabilities.

4. **Develop each domain into a portfolio of opportunities** that are managed as experiments, and not as a pipeline that faces go-or-kill decisions.
5. **Build three distinct competencies, each with dedicated roles.** Discovery generates and describes opportunities. Incubation develops business concepts with evidence. Acceleration scales a concept into a business unit that can stand on its own. Sealed Air runs six-month discovery sprints, and Corning uses two-person exploratory teams.
6. **Define career paths for innovation,** so that talent is not penalized when a venture fails.

The last two manage the system over time.

7. **Manage four types of uncertainty.** They are technological, market, resourcing and organizational. Organizational uncertainty is the most common cause of failure in mature companies.
8. **Adjust the scale of the function to the resources available in a downturn,** and do not shut it down, because stop-start cycles destroy accumulated expertise.

IBM's Emerging Business Opportunity program shows why permanence matters more than initial funding. It generated more than $15 billion in revenue and launched five businesses worth a billion dollars each within five years. It was shut down in 2009, because it had been set up as a temporary program and not as a permanent function.

### Scaling a proven innovation

Scaling an innovation that has already been validated, inside a mature core business, is a different challenge from developing it. Leaders must solve four problems in sequence. They must give the project enough of their own attention, build the right team, mobilize resources and make high-stakes decisions under uncertainty.

Procter & Gamble's chief executive delegated most operating decisions in his own area of expertise to free his time for the Oral-B iO project, which he called probably the most substantial choice he made. Teams working on transformational innovation also benefit from two appointments besides the product owner. An integration leader surfaces organizational risk and coordinates stakeholders. A commercial leader is embedded from the start, so that the product is not developed without regard for the market. Google Glass shows what happens when that step is skipped.

Financial models built for conventional products are systematically biased against high-risk transformational bets. Leaders should add forward-looking indicators, such as shifting demographics and consumer attitudes, to lagging indicators such as sales and return on investment. They should also test demand by prototyping directly with consumers before they commit capital.

### Innovation ecosystems

An innovation ecosystem is a hub tied to a place, such as Silicon Valley, Boston, London or Tel Aviv. A study of more than 60 such locations on six continents found that they share three features. They have a research-intensive university. They have a high concentration of early-stage, often precommercial, activity. And they have dense concentrations of specialized shared resources, including prototyping expertise, testing equipment and risk-tolerant investors, that no single organization could build on its own. Engaging with them complements a company's internal capability and does not replace it.

Innovation ecosystems differ from corporate ecosystems, which are supply chains, platform partnerships and other relationships anchored in commerce. Corporate ecosystems mainly benefit the incumbent at the center and support later-stage growth. Innovation ecosystems are interdependent communities of researchers, founders and specialized service providers, and they are oriented to the earliest, most precommercial stages. A company that wants discovery capability needs a different type of outside engagement from one that wants to optimize its supply chain.

### Three traps

Companies rarely fail to reinvent themselves for lack of awareness. Kodak's own executives held the first digital-photography patents, and Swiss watchmakers invented the quartz movement that nearly destroyed their industry. A study of 601 interviews, 619 focus-group participants and 231 reinvention workshops across nine sectors found that organizations fall into one or more of three traps, in sequence.

**The identity trap** strikes first, when who we are collapses into what we do. Borders stayed so tied to its identity as a physical bookseller that it outsourced e-commerce to Amazon and did not build its own. By the time it reversed course, seven years later, it was too late. Independent bookstores that redefined themselves as community gathering places grew in number by 72 percent from 2009 to 2024, despite facing the same Amazon threat. Moleskine avoided the trap because its founders had always described the company as creating tools for the creative mind and not as a notebook maker. Its employees therefore saw the move into digital note-taking as a natural extension.

**The architecture trap** follows, when leaders apply one organizational structure and one incentive system to both early-stage innovation and the mature business. Corning's Emerging Innovations Group shows the fix. A dedicated unit has autonomy, flexibility and time to incubate ideas before it hands commercially proven ones to business-unit managers. The same company moved 300 people and critical resources away from its LCD business within days, to build the durable glass touchscreen that Steve Jobs needed for the first iPhone. The result was Gorilla Glass, now used in more than 8 billion devices across more than 45 brands. Corning also holds a Day of the Dead ritual that celebrates killing unpromising projects early, and does not punish the teams that kill them.

**The collaboration trap** comes last. Teams for mature products and teams for breakthrough innovation develop rivalry and distrust and not mutual respect. This is most likely when leaders visibly favor one group's resources or attention over the other's.
