## Summary

Telecom operators are squeezed by slow revenue growth, rising costs and pricing that has become a commodity. This chapter explains two complementary responses. PwC's five Cs of convergence, customer experience, commercial innovation, cost excellence and complexity reduction target pricing power at the strategic level. AI-driven personalization uses the customer, billing and network data that operators already hold to create new revenue through targeted offers, real-time promotions and personalized billing. The chapter also describes the technology stack that personalization needs, and it notes that the sources give no documented deployment and do not address the regulatory and trust risks of using behavioral data.

## Key Ideas

- Telecom revenue is projected to grow at only 2.9 percent a year from 2023 to 2028, while energy, labor and supply-chain costs rise.
- Major US and EU mobile carriers earned $171 billion in operating cash from 2019 to 2023 and spent nearly all of it on investment, acquisitions, debt service and dividends.
- The number of operators in a market shows little link to how differentiated prices are, which challenges the assumption that regulators can set competitive intensity by setting the number of players.
- PwC's five Cs are parallel levers for rebuilding pricing power and not a staged plan.
- Operators hold behavioral, billing and network data comparable to that of consumer platforms, so the missing piece is the AI, the architecture and the operating model, and not access to data.
- The personalization proposal rests on sources with no documented deployment, and they do not address the regulatory and trust risks.

## Pricing Power and Personalization

### A two-sided squeeze

PwC projects that telecom revenue will grow at only 2.9 percent a year worldwide from 2023 to 2028. Growth is held back by slower economic growth, saturation in core connectivity and uncertainty about how fast newer streams, such as 5G services and business use of the internet of things, will arrive. Costs are rising at the same time. Energy and labor cost more, and a widening divide between East and West in technology supply chains reduces the scale savings that equipment vendors can offer. See [Telecom Pricing Power and Personalization](#/concept/telecom-pricing-and-personalization).

Research across more than 50 national mobile markets confirms the pressure from commoditization. Average revenue per user is falling in real terms in most markets. Differences in pricing between operators remain historically small, especially in Europe, despite a slight recent improvement. The number of competing operators shows surprisingly little connection to how differentiated prices are. This challenges the long-standing regulatory assumption that a particular number of mobile players decides how competitive a market is, and it bears on whether regulators should reconsider limits on mergers within a market.

The financial consequence is stark. Major US and EU mobile carriers generated $171 billion of operating cash from 2019 to 2023. Of this, $98 billion went to capital spending and acquisitions, and $72 billion went to debt service and dividends. That left almost nothing for innovation, even though years of costly 5G investment have largely failed to raise average revenue per user after inflation. PwC therefore organizes its advice for telecom chief executives around rebuilding pricing power, which means the ability to influence what the company charges. This differs from managing costs alone or waiting for conditions to improve.

### The five Cs

PwC presents five levers, which are parallel and not a staged roadmap.

1. **Convergence** bundles fixed, mobile and other services, such as television, in a single contract instead of separate contracts for each type of connectivity. It strengthens connectivity's status as a purchase that customers cannot do without, like gas, food and power, which people keep buying as prices rise. It raises the share of a customer's spending that the operator captures, deepens loyalty, extends customer lifetime value and reduces churn, because customers with several bundled services leave less often.
2. **Customer experience and service excellence** uses AI to reverse telecom's poor service reputation. It first avoids incidents and outages through better predictive maintenance, and then reduces the time and friction of recovery, as with AI-supported call-center agents. Charter Communications' chief executive describes the tools as helping agents reach a better answer faster and show more empathy. The expected gains are higher satisfaction among customers and employees, fewer costly service visits and lower churn.
3. **Commercial innovation** enriches bundles beyond basic convergence, by adding elements that third parties often supply, such as content, insurance or packages for specific segments. One example is a bundle of data, international calls and public-transit tickets for short-term visitors. Verizon's myPlan lets postpaid customers add or remove premium components, such as video on demand and gaming, for a fixed monthly fee. It moved Verizon from a rigid, one-size-fits-all bundle to a customizable subscription, and it strengthened its competitive position.
4. **Cost excellence** covers the classic levers. One is lowering average labor cost through global delivery models and centers of excellence in lower-cost regions. Another is reducing headcount through automation and AI, which now reach from back-office work into customer-facing functions and even core network and software operations. A third is managing demand and sourcing for outside spending. Vodafone's VOIS centralized back-office and technology functions in global shared-services hubs. Rakuten Mobile's fully virtualized, cloud-native, AI-automated network cut both capital and operating expense by reducing reliance on proprietary hardware. They show cost excellence as a competitive differentiator and not only a defense.
5. **Complexity reduction** targets a more structural cost, which is the variety of products, platforms and technologies accumulated over decades. It compounds cost and slows the organization even when each item is well managed. PwC recommends retiring legacy systems, which it calls challenging and unglamorous but necessary to escape a self-perpetuating spiral of cost. A blueprint for technology architecture decides which components to retire or modernize, and favors modular, reusable ones. A large continental European mobile operator brought its product and technology teams together in an agile, sprint-based program to retire legacy network assets, product platforms and IT systems. It took three years and produced savings, inside and outside the company, that more than justified the effort.

### Personalization as a second route

Personalization offers a second way to earn more from customers, by building new revenue models and not by defending price. Consumer platforms such as Instagram, TikTok and Google sustain engagement with algorithms that learn each user's moods, hobbies, intentions and emotional state. The proposal is that operators hold an equal or richer asset in the data of billing history, device data, real-time network measurements and support interactions. They could use it in the same way, not only to improve service but to create new revenue streams. The missing piece for platform-style monetization is not access to data. It is the application of AI, personalization engines and the right architecture.

Five mechanisms each connect one type of customer signal to a commercial action.

- **Add-on campaigns** respond to patterns of behavior with real-time offers, such as a roaming pack for a frequent traveler or a tailored streaming bundle for a heavy streamer.
- **Cross-sell and up-sell** extend to smart devices, gaming add-ons, home automation and converged television, mobile and broadband offers, chosen from observed habits and not from a generic catalog.
- **Event-driven promotions** fire on momentary conditions, such as a nudge to top up a low balance or a discount when a customer is near a partner retailer. They need the system to act within a short window of relevance.
- **Personalized billing** pairs usage insights with loyalty discounts to build trust while raising average revenue per user. The logic is that a fair, individually relevant billing experience is itself a way to keep customers.
- **Next-best-action recommendations** anticipate needs, such as a retention plan or an upgrade, before the customer asks. The limits of this approach are criticized in other work.

### The technology stack

Each mechanism depends on a specific technical stack.

- A **customer graph**, built with AI and machine learning, unifies billing, device, network and support history in one predictive profile per customer. Without it the data sources stay siloed, and mechanisms that combine signals, such as location with billing status for a balance-aware local promotion, cannot work.
- **Real-time charging**, through online charging systems and policy and charging rules functions, lets a promotion trigger instantly and not in a batch cycle hours later.
- **Complex event processing** correlates streams of low-level signals into meaningful micro-events, such as a change of location, a switch between apps or a spike in use. **Deep packet inspection** supplies the network-level view of what traffic consists of that this processing needs.
- **Next-best-action engines**, paired with generative AI, decide the offer and the moment and also write the campaign copy in real time, which folds creative production into the decision loop. Predictive analytics on early signs of dissatisfaction trigger retention steps before a customer cancels.
- **Omnichannel delivery** through text messages, email, app notifications and call centers makes sure that whatever the system decides can reach the customer in the channel being used.

### What the sources leave open

The two threads lead to the same structural point. Operators already hold the deep user data, real-time behavioral triggers and billing intelligence that digital platforms would covet. PwC's five Cs show how bundling, experience, innovation and simplification can turn that position into pricing power, and the personalization proposal shows how the data itself could be monetized. In both cases the gap is not access to assets. It is the operating model, the AI capability and the architecture needed to capture value from them.

The sources supply no documented deployment of the personalization stack, and they do not address the regulatory and trust risks of monetizing behavioral data. The personalization proposal should therefore be read as an argument and not as proven practice.
