## Summary

Strategic thinking is a skill that people develop mainly through informal, self-directed learning. This chapter explains Julia Sloan's finding that effective strategic thinkers move between surfing broadly across many fields and diving deeply into one, and the central insight of game theory that a decision cannot be judged apart from how rivals will respond. It then turns to prioritization, which people and companies do badly, and covers common frameworks such as the Eisenhower matrix, RICE and MoSCoW. It ends with the need to communicate priorities and to stop work deliberately, which most organizations skip.

## Key Ideas

- Strategic thinking is learned mainly through informal, self-directed learning, by exposing oneself to ideas outside one's own field and reflecting on how they connect to the problem at hand.
- Effective strategic thinkers move between surfing, which is broad exposure, and diving, which is deep study of one area when a question demands it.
- A decision cannot be judged apart from how other rational actors will respond, and a credible commitment can be a source of strength.
- People pursue goals even when doing so makes success less likely, and organizations spread attention too thin across too many priorities.
- In a 2017 study, only about half of the leaders in a typical company agreed on their company's strategic priorities.
- Deliberately stopping work is the step that most organizations skip, and it needs rules that are decided in advance.

## Strategic Thinking and Prioritization

### Learning to think strategically

Julia Sloan treats strategic thinking as a skill that can be learned. It is developed mainly through informal, self-directed learning and not through formal training programs or credentials. This conclusion rests on research into how experienced strategic thinkers describe the way they built their own capability. The pattern is ongoing exposure to ideas far outside a person's immediate field, together with active reflection on how those ideas connect to the strategic problems at hand. Capability grows less from studying strategy frameworks directly and more from the habit of continually turning unrelated input into strategic insight. See [Strategic Thinking Practice](#/concept/strategic-thinking-practice).

Sloan describes two learning modes between which effective strategic thinkers move.

- **Surfing** is a broad, low-commitment mode of staying exposed to a wide range of fields, sources and perspectives, without going deep into any of them.
- **Diving** is a focused, high-commitment mode of going deep into one field when a particular strategic question demands detailed understanding.

A person needs both modes. Surfing without occasional diving risks broad but shallow pattern-matching. Diving without surfing risks tunnel vision that misses relevant ideas from outside the immediate field.

### What game theory adds

Avinash Dixit and Barry Nalebuff's book Thinking Strategically contributes the central insight of game theory. A decision cannot be correctly judged in isolation from the way other rational actors are likely to respond. A decision that looks obviously good when it is analyzed alone can be a poor one once a competitor's rational response is taken into account, and the reverse can also hold.

One well-known application is the value of credible commitment. A deliberate and visible reduction of one's own future options, such as publicly and irreversibly committing to a course of action, can be a source of strength and not of weakness. It changes which response is rational for a competitor, and closes off responses that would otherwise have been available.

### Why prioritizing is hard

Prioritization is one of the most important and most often mishandled skills in organizational life. The difficulty is less about knowing which framework to use and more about a behavioral bias toward pursuing goals whether or not doing so is rational.

In a laboratory experiment, participants pursued two competing goals. The reward for achieving one goal equaled the reward for achieving both. They still tried to achieve both, even though splitting their effort made it less likely that either would be achieved. People appear to be motivated by pursuing an achievable goal, whether or not it serves their interests. A related bias shows that ease of achievement, and not value, often decides what people work on next. Indebted consumers paid off small debts before larger debts with higher interest rates, choosing the tangible feeling of progress over the better financial outcome. The same pattern appears at work. Being the 400th reviewer of a document adds no real value but still gives a sense of accomplishment.

Organizations show a structural version, called the peanut-butter problem. They accumulate so many priorities that attention and resources are spread too thin to make real progress on any of them. Some executives treat everything they ask for as a priority, and employees may behave as though every request carries equal weight whatever is actually said. When Niels Christiansen became chief executive of Lego, he found the company formally tracking 100 key enterprise risks at once. His question was how a company can look after 100 different risks without being risk-averse in everything it does.

### Frameworks for choosing

Several established frameworks counter both the personal and the organizational versions of the problem.

- **The Eisenhower matrix** sorts individual tasks by urgency and importance. Tasks that are urgent and important come first. Tasks that are important but not urgent need time scheduled for them deliberately. Tasks that are neither should probably be dropped.
- **The action-priority matrix** compares impact with effort.
- **RICE** scores an item on reach, impact, confidence and effort, and is common in product teams.
- **MoSCoW** sorts requirements into must have, should have, could have and will not have.
- **The 70:20:10 rule**, from Google, allocates innovation resources across the core business, adjacent activities and entirely new ones.

### Communicating and deprioritizing

Choosing a framework solves only part of the problem. A 2017 study asked senior executives at 124 organizations to recite their own company's strategic priorities. In the typical company, only about half of the leaders agreed on what the priorities were. Communicating priorities is therefore a separate challenge that is often left unmet.

The step most often skipped is deliberate deprioritization. Prioritizing something new almost always means stopping something else, and not simply adding to a list that is already overloaded. Doing this well takes explicit rules decided in advance, so that stopping does not depend on someone finding the political courage to kill a project in the moment. Phil Le-Brun and Jana Werner, Amazon executives and authors of The Octopus Organisation, recommend building explicit go-or-kill decision points into project plans. They also recommend objective kill criteria for software initiatives, such as the rate at which team members leave or thresholds for time commitment.
