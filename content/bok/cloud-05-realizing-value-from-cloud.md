## Summary

Most cloud transformations capture only a fraction of their potential value, typically plateauing at about 35 percent after five years. This chapter explains that the gap has less to do with technology than with ambition, business engagement and execution skill. It shows where cloud value comes from, why programs stall, what the warning signs and turnaround steps are and how maturity models describe what leading companies do differently, including with AI. The consistent finding is that the companies in the highest maturity states run cloud as a company-wide effort owned jointly by business and technology.

## Key Ideas

- Only 37 percent of senior executives in one survey said they had fully achieved the outcomes they expected from cloud.
- Most companies plateau at about 35 percent of cloud's potential after roughly five years, held back by talent, legacy systems and habits.
- Cloud creates value in two ways, through efficiency on the bottom line and through innovation on the top line.
- More than half of cloud transformations fail to reach their intended benefits within three years, and there are six recognizable warning signs.
- Bain groups companies into four adoption states, and the largest group, the Migrators, mostly moves applications without changing them.
- Companies that combine AI and cloud as one business strategy are about twice as likely to see measurable value.

## Realizing Value from Cloud

### The value gap

In an Accenture survey of 750 senior executives, only 37 percent said they had fully achieved the outcomes they expected from cloud. That figure was up by only two points from 2018. This was true even though the pandemic compressed into about three years a shift that had been expected to take eight to ten, from roughly 20 to 30 percent of work in the cloud toward 80 percent.

Bain finds that only about one company in 20 sets its cloud ambitions high enough to target the full potential. Most plateau at roughly 35 percent of that potential after about five years. Three obstacles block them. A shortage of cloud engineering talent affects 54 percent of companies, legacy environments affect 46 percent and entrenched cultural habits affect 43 percent. See [Cloud Value Realization and Maturity](#/concept/cloud-value-realization-and-maturity).

### Where the value comes from

Cloud creates bottom-line value through developer productivity and lower unit costs. It creates top-line value as a platform for innovation. A cloud-enabled challenger can hold a 60 to 70 percent advantage in operating cost and an advantage in time to market over an established competitor.

McKinsey estimates that more than $1 trillion of annual earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization could be achieved across the Fortune 500 by 2030. It describes three levels of ambition, which are to rejuvenate, to innovate and to pioneer. Careless lift and shift can raise costs, so the technique alone does not deliver this.

### Why programs stall

More than half of cloud transformations fail to reach their intended benefits within three years. BCG lists six warning signs.

- Goals and metrics are missing.
- Migration is not sequenced in a coordinated way.
- Cost controls are weak.
- Cloud skills are undefined.
- Technical standards are not enforced.
- Applications are stuck at the infrastructure-as-a-service level.

BCG also describes six turnaround interventions. One client that had stalled for four years used them to finish its backlog of migrations in 18 months, cut the running cost of migrated applications by more than 10 percent and raise its average application security score to about 80 percent.

### Maturity models

Bain describes four adoption states.

- **Avoiders** still run entirely on their own data centers, often citing regulation or risk.
- **Migrators** form the largest group. They have delegated cloud to IT without a strategy based on value, and about 60 percent of them lift and shift without reengineering applications.
- **Explorers** have leaders who understand the value of cloud, often a technology executive paired with a business leader. They produce isolated successes through trial and error and not a repeatable process.
- **Strategic Value Creators** treat cloud as a company-wide effort aimed at new revenue, customer-facing services and broad adoption of technology. They are the rarest group.

Only 16 percent of companies believe they have a cloud roadmap focused on strategic priorities. Cost and value optimization at the Migrator stage can improve the total cost of ownership of cloud by 20 to 30 percent, which frees funds for strategic projects.

The combination of AI and cloud is now part of the picture. PwC's 2024 survey of 1,030 executives found that 12 percent of companies were Top Performers in AI and cloud, and they were roughly twice as likely as others to have achieved measurable value. They treated AI-powered cloud as an integrated business strategy, modernized their data for generative AI, evolved IT with agent-based workflows and reset their relationships with cloud providers.

Forrester's next-generation model groups seven principles each under platforms, partners and practices. It also adds a caution. The cloud is not always the right answer, and any added complexity must return added business value.

### Moving up the curve

Across the research from Accenture, Bain, BCG, McKinsey, Forrester and PwC, the same finding recurs. Value comes from both efficiency and innovation, and the highest maturity states treat the cloud as a company-wide effort owned by business and technology together. The sources agree that the gap between leaders and laggards is explained by ambition, joint ownership by business and technology, change in the operating model and sustained investment in skills. It is not explained by the sophistication of the technology.
