## Summary

Telecom operators are restructuring both the shape of the business and the way their technology and teams are organized. Vertically integrated operators suffer a conglomerate discount from investors and carry a costly legacy of complexity. This chapter explains the three strategic options, which are separating the network from the services, merging with a rival in the same country and moving toward a utility-like model, each with documented gains and real risks. It also covers three further priorities for chief executives, and at the operating level it shows how business architecture, IT excellence and agile ways of working decide whether operators realize cost and speed benefits and can scale AI.

## Key Ideas

- A vertically integrated operator combines a capital-intensive utility business, riskier product innovation and retail-style commerce, which dilutes leadership focus and leads investors to apply a conglomerate discount.
- Delayering separates the network company from one or more service companies. The results are meaningful but mixed, and the process takes two to three years and more than $1 billion.
- Consolidation within one country can yield synergies above 15 percent of combined capital spending and 30 percent of combined operating spending, but poor integration destroys value.
- A utility-like model trades growth for efficiency and stability, and roughly three-quarters of surveyed telecom leaders say their organization would benefit from moving away from the integrated model within five to ten years.
- Top-quartile operators spend nearly 30 percent less on IT relative to revenue, but only if they improve across all dimensions together.
- Agile ways of working work only when structure, workflows, talent and culture all change together.

## Restructuring the Telecom Operator

### The cost of being integrated

A traditional, vertically integrated operator bundles three businesses with different economics. The first is network infrastructure, which needs a great deal of capital and pays back slowly, like a utility. The second is product innovation, which carries higher value and higher risk and pays back faster. The third is commerce and customer service, which need skills closer to retail. Running all three under one management team dilutes leadership focus and forces compromises between different planning horizons and capital needs. This is a large part of the reason why investors apply a conglomerate discount, which is a lower valuation for a company that combines unrelated businesses. They do so even when the operator owns valuable infrastructure assets that would command high valuation multiples on their own. See [Telecom Structure and Organizational Transformation](#/concept/telecom-structure-and-transformation).

### Delayering

Delayering separates the operator into two kinds of company.

- A **NetCo**, also called an InfraCo, operates the network infrastructure and may become a carrier-neutral wholesale provider that serves other operators.
- One or more **ServCos** focus on products, sales and customer care. They can specialize by customer type, offering, business model, investment horizon or skill set.

Each company can then pursue its own strategy, capital structure, talent model and investor base. The results are meaningful. Turkcell earned $150 million, or 8 percent of its revenue, within three years of moving digital services into stand-alone platforms. O2 and CETIN in the Czech Republic saw a 27 percent rise in market value in the year after their split. Some operators report an improvement of up to 10 percentage points in the EBITDA margin, which is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization as a share of revenue, and a tenfold rise in the valuation multiple. e& took a more elaborate route and split into five verticals. These include two geographic telecom units, a consumer digital unit, an enterprise digital unit and a capital-investments arm. This improved its ability to attract talent and its management focus.

The record is nevertheless mixed. Delayering typically takes two to three years and more than $1 billion in committed capital. No operator that has separated into a network company and service companies has yet ranked among the industry's top performers in shareholder returns.

The downsides are structural.

- A service company loses automatic preferential access to network resources. Price differentiation, cross-subsidization and control over product development that depends on the network become harder under an arm's-length commercial relationship.
- A network company that is cut off from customers loses the customer insight that informed its decisions on deployment, densification and maintenance.
- Separation can work against seamless bundling across channels, at a time when the industry is trying to become more customer-centric.
- Rules such as the European GDPR complicate the gathering and monetizing of customer data across separate entities.

Success therefore depends on conditions that must be engineered. The units must be genuinely independent and focused on pure growth. Where the law permits, they collaborate closely on data, best practice and governance. Each entity reports full performance through three to four distinct measures, with no hidden cross-subsidies. They use distinct talent and incentive models. Investor stories are sharper and attract each entity's natural investors. And the units are open to mergers, joint ventures and partnerships that the integrated structure ruled out.

### Consolidation

Consolidation is the more traditional alternative or complement. Orange Spain's 2024 combination with MasMovil targets 490 million euros of synergies after four years, while serving nearly half of Spain's fixed and mobile subscribers. The 2025 merger of Vodafone UK and Three UK, which created the largest mobile operator in the United Kingdom, targets 700 million pounds by its fifth year. In-country consolidation can unlock synergies above 15 percent of combined capital expenditure and 30 percent of combined operating expenditure. But consolidation without disciplined cost transformation and rigorous post-merger integration can destroy value. It deserves the same discipline as any other major allocation of capital, and is not an automatic win.

### A utility-like model

A third, less drastic option is to shift the operator toward a utility-like model, which deliberately trades growth ambition for efficiency, reliability and lower risk.

- It prioritizes leaner operations over volatile growth in customer-facing businesses.
- It reduces capital intensity through investment sharing, such as network sharing, joint infrastructure entities or third-party investment in wholesale capacity.
- It seeks regulatory support, such as government funding, subsidized spectrum pricing or cost-based pricing under a regulatory-asset-base framework like the one used for electricity and gas.

Close to three-quarters of surveyed telecom leaders say that their organization would benefit from moving away from the integrated model within five to ten years. The largest share favors the utility-like shift over delayering. This implicitly concedes that market-beating growth is not a realistic near-term goal, in favor of the dependable cash flows that investors in utilities reward.

### Three more priorities for chief executives

PwC's research ranks business-model reinvention among seven urgent priorities for chief executives. It names three others that structural moves do not address.

- **Workforce renewal.** In the research, 46 percent of telecom employees doubt that their employer will exist in ten years, and 32 percent plan to change jobs within twelve months. Senior network engineers are retiring, and cloud, AI and the internet of things demand new skills.
- **Resilience to cyber risk and shifting regulation.** Threats related to cloud are the top security concern, and scrutiny of deals is rising sharply. National-security reviews of foreign investment in the United States have risen by 50 percent since 2020.
- **The sustainable carrier.** Operators must address Scope 3 emissions along the supply chain, which are the emissions of suppliers and customers, while demand for computing driven by generative AI adds pressure on net-zero commitments.

### Escaping the complexity trap

Beneath these strategic choices lies the question of how technology and teams are organized. Strategy& calls it the complexity cost trap. Cost transformation, rising customer expectations, re-platforming across technologies and investment in next-generation infrastructure leave operators carrying the accumulated burden of maintaining legacy technology, rising labor costs and volatile external spending.

Business architecture is the proposed remedy. It is a blueprint that spans value-creation opportunities, business capabilities and the underlying technology, and it gives leaders a shared taxonomy for aligning investment with strategy. Strategy& recommends three steps. Build a capability map. Embed business architecture in strategic and financial planning as a distinct discipline. And create a legacy relief factory that retires outdated technology in agile increments.

### What IT excellence looks like

McKinsey benchmarked more than 20 operators in its IT Excellence Diagnostic, across six dimensions. They are business functionality, operating model, engineering excellence, IT architecture, cloud, and data and AI. Top-quartile operators spend nearly 30 percent less on IT relative to revenue, but only if they improve consistently across all six dimensions. One operator that transformed its IT in a planned, programmatic way cut IT spending by as much as 40 percent.

Most cloud migrations take a simple lift-and-shift approach that fails to capture the cost benefits. As a result, the operators with the highest rates of cloud adoption post the worst cost efficiency. The real leaders build cloud-native systems or substantially rebuild their applications. More than 60 percent of operators have adopted agile methods, but most do not realize the full benefit. Leaders industrialize a proof of concept in three to six months, against a year and a half for their peers, because of stronger architecture and engineering practices, such as automated deployment pipelines and integrated product catalogs.

### Changing four things at once

Interviews with six operators, Cogeco, Entel, Magyar Telekom, Masmovil, Safaricom and Tesco Mobile, two to five years into agile transformations, examined the operating model. All six changed four elements in parallel.

- **Structure** moved to cross-functional squads and tribes instead of functional silos.
- **Workflows** moved to a unified quarterly business review that links strategy to the objectives and key results of each team.
- **Talent** moved from models based on roles to models based on skills.
- **Culture** changed as leaders moved from directive control to coaching.

Companies that tried to limit change to one element eventually had to change all four to see an effect. Several interviewees tie the agile model to effective AI. Masmovil's cross-functional teams found and scaled use cases area by area. Only units that own a process from end to end, and not pilots confined to a single function, can apply AI and agents to the processes with the greatest potential.

### Deutsche Telekom

Deutsche Telekom shows disciplined execution of both architecture and operating model in one company. Its chief information officer, Peter Leukert, describes an IT organization that supports a fiber rollout targeting about 17.5 million homes passed in Germany by 2027. Customer experience is benchmarked against over-the-top players, and time to market is the key measure of value. The team built a custom operational support system for fiber services in nine months, instead of buying a product, and later licensed it to outside partners. It reports a 50 percent gain in the productivity of customer service from better digital tools, and expects generative AI to add another 50 percent. Leukert insists that the key is integration, and not building or training models. The company uses TM Forum's Open Digital Architecture to build shared software once and deploy it in its eleven European operating countries. This delivers the modularity and reuse that business architecture aims for.
