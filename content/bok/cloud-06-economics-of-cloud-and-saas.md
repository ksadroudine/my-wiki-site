## Summary

The cloud changed the economics of the technology industry in three ways. It shifted growth toward a few leading providers, moved financial risk from customers to suppliers and made ongoing use, not the first sale, the source of revenue. This chapter explains how established technology companies that were not built for the cloud can still capture growth, how customers differ in the speed and reasons for adopting cloud, and how a discipline called Consumption Development closes the gap between the value a product could deliver and the value customers realize. It also covers how SaaS companies are judged, including the Rule of 40 and the metrics that investors must read with care.

## Key Ideas

- Cloud captured about 60 percent of global IT industry growth in the period Bain studied, and most of that growth went to the leading providers.
- Established technology companies can respond by harvesting cash from older products to fund hybrid and multicloud offerings, by acquiring new capability and by changing how they sell.
- Customers adopt cloud in five different ways, so a vendor succeeds by choosing which type of customer to serve and not by riding overall growth.
- With subscription pricing, the supplier earns only when customers use the product, so driving use becomes essential to the business.
- A SaaS company that beats the Rule of 40, meaning growth rate plus profit margin above 40 percent, is on average three times more valuable relative to its revenue.
- Generative AI is reaching software markets faster than SaaS did, and it is likely to make customers switch vendors more often.

## Economics of Cloud and SaaS

### Who captured the growth

Bain's 2019 sizing projected that cloud would account for 60 percent of all global IT industry growth through 2021. It split that growth across three parts of the market. Public cloud infrastructure and enabling services were expected to grow 9 percent a year and reach $127 billion. Infrastructure and platform as a service were expected to grow 29 percent a year and reach $117 billion. SaaS was expected to grow 16 percent a year and reach $159 billion.

The leading providers captured most of this growth. Amazon Web Services grew more than 40 percent a quarter and delivered over half of Amazon's operating income by the third quarter of 2018. Microsoft's Azure grew 76 percent in the same quarter. See [Cloud and SaaS Business Economics](#/concept/cloud-and-saas-business-economics).

### The squeeze on established vendors

The period was difficult for established technology companies outside the leading cloud providers. From 2013 to 2017, every legacy technology category except three grew more slowly than expected. High-end enterprise servers fell 11 percent against a forecast fall of 3 percent. The shift was cannibalizing the older categories instead of adding to them.

Bain's playbook for companies not built for the cloud has three parts. They harvest the cash flow of older product lines to fund hybrid and multicloud offerings, and they do not simply defend the old business. They acquire capability in fast-growing cloud segments where they have little presence of their own. They also change their sales and channel model to match how customers now buy cloud services, and do not sell cloud-related products through a process built for on-premises deals. Bain warns that the shift is structural, not cyclical. Companies that read short-term stability as safety are setting up a later reckoning.

### Five kinds of customer

Bain describes five customer segments, first developed in 2011 and revisited with 2019 data.

- **Transformational** customers are the early adopters that set the pace. They put performance and scalability ahead of cost, though some find a ceiling, because a predictable workload at 80 percent utilization increasingly favors on-premises economics.
- **Heterogeneous** customers are mature businesses with forward-looking but risk-aware IT leaders. They deliberately split workloads across SaaS, cloud infrastructure and platforms, and on-premises systems, according to fit.
- **Safety Conscious** customers have real demand but are held back by private-cloud offerings that are too costly or complex for their need for a dedicated, non-shared environment.
- **Price Conscious** customers are the most skilled at capturing savings. They treat migration as a chance to audit and rightsize their computing and storage, which Bain's client work found can cut costs by 30 to 60 percent. Lift-and-shift migrations reliably miss this gain.
- **Slow and Steady** customers waited out the early-adopter phase and commit over three to five years. Bain projected them to become the largest segment by 2022 and to account for half of spending growth.

A rising tide does not lift all boats evenly. Success depends on choosing which of these faces of the cloud to serve.

### When the supplier carries the risk

The move to cloud changed who bears risk. Three roughly simultaneous shifts permanently changed how technology companies earn revenue. They were the 2008 to 2009 recession, cloud computing and the smartphone. Under on-premises licensing, the customer paid upfront and carried the risk that the product would go unused. Under the cloud and SaaS model, no usage means no money, so the supplier earns only as the customer consumes the product. Driving ongoing use therefore becomes essential for the vendor.

This creates the Consumption Gap. It is the growing distance between the value a product could deliver and the business value that customers realize. It arises because vendors can add features and complexity faster than customers can absorb them. Revenue builds from many small steps, such as an added user, an unlocked feature or extra capacity. Growth depends on getting existing users to adopt more advanced capability, getting new users onto an application and getting existing accounts to adopt additional applications.

Customer support and service organizations are proposed as the best-placed owner of consumption. They already have the lowest cost per interaction of any customer-facing function, a global infrastructure, deep product knowledge and ownership of the self-service channels through which guidance would flow.

On the development side, Consumption Development has three components.

- **Intelligent Listening** replaces upfront requirements documents with real-time usage data from products that are deliberately instrumented. What customers do inside a product is a more reliable signal of what to build than what they say they want.
- **Consumption Innovation** moves investment away from the race to add features, and toward simplifying the product and guiding each user along a roadmap that reveals features in steps.
- **In-Product Upsell** places recommendation and offer tools inside the product so that the next relevant feature or purchase appears at the right moment. The design guidance is a 10:1 rule, which means ten helpful offers that earn no revenue for every offer that does. Consistent help builds the trust that makes a later commercial offer more likely to be accepted.

Feature overshoot persists even though it is understood. Customers keep requesting features for their own use, competitors keep shipping new capabilities and engineers like to build impressive functions. Unused features are not harmless. They clutter the interface, add cost over the life of the product and slow the adoption of the smaller set of features that create most of the value.

### How SaaS companies are judged

Investors judge SaaS companies with the Rule of 40. A company's growth rate plus its profit margin should exceed 40 percent. Bain finds that the market rewards this decisively, because companies that beat the threshold are on average three times more valuable relative to their revenue than those that fall short.

Bain and OPEXEngine analyzed 2019 financial data for nearly 80 SaaS companies and found that the winners scored consistently higher on five operating benchmarks.

1. **Employee retention**, the most important, because customer satisfaction correlates with employee satisfaction.
2. **Efficient sales and marketing spending**, tracked through the magic number, which is the ratio of revenue growth to sales and marketing spending.
3. **A better ratio of customer lifetime value to acquisition cost.**
4. **Disciplined operating expenses**, including efficient product development and the culling of weak sales prospects.
5. **Efficient growth from the existing customer base**, which means more revenue from upgrades and cross-selling and less customer loss than peers.

Investors must also read alternative metrics with care. Annual recurring revenue smooths the up-front recognition of term contracts. The net retention rate measures revenue growth per existing customer, and a high figure signals that existing customers want more. Adjusted earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) strips out stock-based pay and one-time items. Total addressable market, the revenue a product could earn if it won the entire market, is the most marketing-oriented measure and the easiest to inflate. Bill Gurley's 1998 warning about selling dollar bills for 85 cents shows why such measures deserve skepticism.

### The generative AI effect

McKinsey reports that the global SaaS market reached roughly $3 trillion by 2022, after nearly a decade in which software market value grew at twice the pace of the broader market. Growth then slowed as enterprises cut IT spending in response to higher interest rates. Generative AI has reached about 2 percent of the enterprise software market in a single year, a share that SaaS took four years to reach.

McKinsey expects generative AI to make customers switch vendors more often as they chase new capabilities. SaaS companies therefore have to keep pace with development and defend client relationships, and they cannot assume renewal. Product-led growth, which puts the product and not a salesperson at the center of winning and expanding customers, is rarely successful alone. The more durable pattern is product-led sales, which adds it to a sales-led approach. See [AI Disruption of SaaS Business Models](#/concept/ai-disruption-of-saas-business-models).
