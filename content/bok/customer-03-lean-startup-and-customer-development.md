## Summary

Customer Development and the Lean Startup treat a startup as a temporary organization that is searching for a repeatable, scalable business model, and not as a smaller version of an established company that is carrying out a known plan. This chapter explains the four steps from customer discovery to company building, the pivot as a substantive change to the business model, and early adopters called earlyvangelists who are the right first customers. It also covers the build-measure-learn loop, in which each product version is an experiment, the metrics that suit a startup and growth hacking as a responsibility shared across the company.

## Key Ideas

- A startup is a temporary organization that searches for a repeatable and scalable business model. It is not a small version of a large company.
- Customer Development has four steps. The first two, discovery and validation, are the search for a model, and the last two, creation and company building, are the execution of a validated model.
- A pivot is a substantive change to one of the nine boxes of the business model canvas, driven by customer feedback, and it is a normal part of the search and not a failure.
- The first customers to target are earlyvangelists, who already feel a painful problem, have built a workaround and have a budget.
- A minimum viable product is an experiment designed to produce validated learning as fast as possible, and not a shrunken version of the final product.
- At this stage the financial figure that always matters is cash burn and the months of runway that remain.

## Lean Startup and Customer Development

### A startup is a search

Steve Blank and Bob Dorf's Customer Development method and Eric Ries's Lean Startup, which builds directly on it, start from the same idea. A startup is not a smaller version of an established company. It is a temporary organization whose sole purpose is to search for a business model that is repeatable, scalable and profitable. An established company, by contrast, executes a model that it already knows.

On the first day, a startup's understanding of its customer, its problem and its business model is a set of unproven hypotheses and not facts. The entire discipline exists to turn those hypotheses into evidence as quickly and cheaply as possible, before the company commits to scale. The search needs a different structure from execution. During the search, a founder-led customer development team works directly with customers. Once a model is validated, the company moves to a functional structure.

### Four steps

Customer Development runs through four steps in sequence. After each step the team stops and assesses before it goes on.

1. **Customer discovery.** The team turns the founder's vision into business-model hypotheses, which it records on the business model canvas. It then tests them face to face, first the customer's problem and then the proposed solution, with earlyvangelist customers and not with the mainstream market.
2. **Customer validation.** The team repeats the tests at greater scale and with more rigor, to confirm that the model is genuinely repeatable and scalable and not just promising in a few conversations.
3. **Customer creation.** The company spends aggressively to build demand among end users.
4. **Company building.** The organization changes from a founder-led search organization into one with a functional structure, built to execute.

The first two steps are the search for a business model. The last two are the execution of a model once it has been validated. The company moves on only after discovery and validation succeed, which the method describes as reaching escape velocity. See [Lean Startup and Customer Development](#/concept/lean-startup-customer-development).

### Pivots and the business model canvas

The business model canvas describes a business in nine boxes. They are the value proposition, customer segments, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partners and cost structure. A pivot is a substantive change to one or more of those boxes, driven directly by evidence from customer testing. It differs from an iteration, which is a minor adjustment, such as changing a price from $99 to $79. Frequent pivoting is not a sign of failure. It is the expected way for a startup to converge on a working model. The canvas is redrawn after each pivot, and the stack of past canvases records what the company has learned.

### Earlyvangelists and the minimum viable product

The right first customers are called earlyvangelists. They already recognize that they have an acute problem. They have put together an interim workaround. They are already searching for a solution. And they have a budget, or can get one easily. A minimum viable product, which is an early version with only the features needed for learning, should be aimed at them. Mainstream customers expect a finished product with every feature, and they are the wrong target at this stage.

### Build, measure, learn

The build-measure-learn loop treats each product version as an experiment. The minimum viable product is designed to generate validated learning as fast as possible. It is not a shrunken version of the final product. Each loop tests a specific hypothesis, and the team then decides, with evidence, whether to persevere with the current direction or to pivot.

### Metrics and growth

Startup metrics track how hypotheses are turning into facts. They ask, for example, whether the problem and the product features have been validated, and they do not measure performance against a fixed plan. The one financial figure that always matters at this stage is the cash-burn rate and the number of months of runway left.

A complementary account covers what happens after a startup has validated its model and begins to scale. It goes by the name growth hacking. Sustained growth comes from a shared commitment in which growth is everyone's responsibility, and not marketing's alone. Product, engineering and support all take part. The approach rests on a must-have product and on a rigorous, data-driven way of acquiring, keeping and earning from customers that is informed by a deep understanding of customer behavior. It is described as an evolution of marketing practice and not as a replacement for it or an opponent of it.
