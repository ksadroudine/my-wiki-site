## Summary

Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle is a method for structuring business writing and thinking. It starts from the observation that the mind automatically arranges information into a pyramid, so a document is easier to read when its ideas are arranged that way in advance. This chapter explains the three rules of a valid pyramid, the SCQA pattern for an introduction, the two ways ideas relate and why a document is best built from the top down. It also covers logic trees and the three modes of reasoning, which help to test thinking when solving analytical problems.

## Key Ideas

- The mind automatically groups ideas into a pyramid in order to understand them, so a document is easier to read when the writer has already done that work.
- A valid pyramid obeys three rules. Ideas at each level summarize the ideas below, ideas in a group are the same kind of idea, and ideas in a group follow a logical order.
- An introduction follows the SCQA pattern of situation, complication, question and answer, and it reminds the reader of what the reader already knows.
- Ideas relate either deductively or inductively, and the two should never be mixed in one group.
- A pyramid is best built from the top down, by deciding the reader's question first.
- Logic trees, which divide a problem into parts that do not overlap and leave nothing out, help to test thinking.

## Structuring Ideas with the Pyramid Principle

### The premise

Barbara Minto's central premise is that the human mind cannot process an unordered list of ideas. It automatically tries to group whatever it is shown into a hierarchy, in order to make sense of it. Writing that does this grouping for the reader in advance is therefore easier to read than writing that leaves the reader to rebuild the structure. See [The Pyramid Principle](#/concept/pyramid-principle).

In the pyramid, the main point sits at the top. Groups of supporting ideas sit beneath it, and each group is summarized by the idea above it.

### Three rules for a valid pyramid

A pyramid must meet three rules at once.

1. **Summarize.** Every idea at a given level must summarize the ideas grouped beneath it. A paragraph's point summarizes its sentences, and a section's point summarizes its paragraphs.
2. **Group like with like.** Every idea within one group must be the same kind of idea. One test is whether the entire set can be described with a single plural noun, such as reasons, problems or steps.
3. **Order logically.** Ideas within a group must follow one of four logical orders. Deductive order follows an argument. Chronological order follows time, as for the steps of a process. Structural order follows an existing structure, such as a list of cities in geographic sequence. Comparative order ranks ideas by importance.

### The introduction

Every well-formed pyramid begins with an introduction built from four elements, known together as SCQA.

- The **situation** is something the reader will accept as true without proof, either because the reader already knows it or because it is easy to verify.
- The **complication** is something that goes wrong, changes or creates tension in that situation.
- The **question** is what the complication naturally raises in the reader's mind.
- The **answer** becomes the single top-level point of the entire pyramid.

An introduction exists only to remind readers of what they already know or could be expected to know. It never informs the reader of new facts that would need supporting evidence. Evidence and exhibits belong in the body of the document. Once the answer is stated, it raises a new question, which the next line down the pyramid answers, and so on. This relationship of question and answer is the vertical dimension of the pyramid. The logical relationship among ideas at the same level is the horizontal dimension.

### Deduction and induction

Ideas in a single group can relate in only one of two logical forms, and a writer should never blend them within one group.

A **deductive** group is a chain of exactly three steps. The first is a statement about an existing situation. The second comments on the subject or predicate of the first. The third, the therefore, states what follows from the first two. The classic example is that men are mortal, Socrates is a man, and therefore Socrates is mortal. Moving up a level means restating the argument in its final conclusion.

An **inductive** group gathers ideas that are alike only in that one plural noun can label them all. For example, three separate reports of tanks massing at a border can all be called warlike movements. Moving up a level means drawing an inference about what the shared feature implies, such as that Poland is about to be invaded. Induction is recommended as the default for the body of a document, because it is a lighter load for the reader. Deduction is kept for the rarer cases in which the reader is expected to disagree with the conclusion and must be walked through the argument to be persuaded, or in which the recommended action cannot be understood without first explaining the reasoning.

### Building from the top down

The recommended way to build a pyramid is from the top down, because it is easier to start from what the writer is already certain about than to sort a pile of disorganized points afterward.

1. Draw a box for the subject.
2. Decide the question that the reader should have in mind by the end.
3. Write down the answer, if it is already known.
4. Identify the situation, which is the first uncontroversial statement about the subject that the reader will accept.
5. Develop the complication by asking "so what?" of the situation, until it naturally produces the question already identified. If the question and the complication do not match, revise one or both.

Working from the bottom up is only a fallback, for when the writer's thinking is not yet developed enough to start at the top. The bottom-up method means listing every point that seems relevant, working out how the points relate and then drawing a conclusion. The book also warns against a related habit, which is writing a full first draft to get everything down on the theory that structure can be added later. Once an idea is written down, it acquires a false polish that makes writers reluctant to restructure it.

### Testing ideas with logic trees

For analytical problem solving, ideas should be tested by drawing logic trees. They include financial or return-on-investment trees, task-structure trees, activity trees, choice trees and sequential-structure trees. Each traces an undesirable end result back to its causes. The causes must be mutually exclusive, which means they do not overlap, and collectively exhaustive, which means that together they leave nothing out. This discipline, known as MECE, is the same one used to divide a subject into its parts, such as an organization chart.

The book distinguishes three modes of reasoning for solving problems.

- **Deduction** combines a known rule with a known case to give a result.
- **Induction** takes an observed case and result and infers a probable rule.
- **Abduction** combines a known rule with an observed result to generate a hypothesis to test. It matches how people actually diagnose why a business problem exists.
