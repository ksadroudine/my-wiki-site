## Summary

New ideas often come from outsiders, who are free of a field's habits, yet outsiders struggle to win the backing needed to carry an idea through. This chapter explains why the most creative people tend to straddle the boundary between the familiar and the new, how organizations create that position on purpose and why a single ally can matter more than broad support. It also covers how outsiders can learn insiders' language, how moments of crisis make a system open to ideas it once rejected and why a novel business model succeeds only when it is paired with operational discipline.

## Key Ideas

- Outsiders are well placed to connect distant ideas, but the same distance makes it harder for them to win credibility and support.
- The most creative people sit neither at the center of a network nor at its far edge. They straddle both.
- A single ally is usually what turns a marginal idea into a mainstream one, and universal agreement is not needed.
- The same pitch is rated about 20 percent higher by expert panels when it uses insider language.
- Systems under acute pressure become open to outsider ideas that were dismissed before.
- A novel business model predicts good performance only when it is paired with operational discipline, efficiency and fit with strategy.

## How New Ideas Gain Traction

### The outsider's advantage and problem

Outsiders are people who are free of a field's ingrained norms and expectations. They are structurally placed to connect distant ideas and to challenge the way things are done. Sociologists call this pattern focused naivete. The same distance that fuels their originality makes it harder for them to win the backing and credibility needed to bring their ideas to fruition. See [Organizational Innovation Capability](#/concept/organizational-innovation-capability).

### Straddling the boundary

A study of the collaboration networks of roughly 12,000 Hollywood artists found that the highest creative success, measured by major awards, belonged neither to people at the center of the network nor to those at its extreme edges. It belonged to people who straddled both. They were grounded enough in the familiar to have legitimacy, and adventurous enough at the edges to bring novelty. The same held for film crews. Teams that blended core and peripheral talent consistently outperformed teams that emphasized one or the other.

Organizations can create this position deliberately.

- **Pixar** encourages its animators to take part in outside professional associations and academic conferences.
- **The National Science Foundation** fills roughly a third of its staff with rotators, who are academic scientists on loan for two to four years. Research finds that this increases both the selection of novel projects and the permanent staff's own ability to recognize novelty.

### One ally is enough

Universal agreement is not what turns a marginal idea into a mainstream one. A second ally usually is. Insiders tend to support other insiders' ideas by instinct, but even one supporting voice, or one dissenting voice, can shift a group's judgment. Venture capitalist Mike Markkula's early yes to Steve Jobs, when other investors passed, is an example.

### Speaking the insiders' language

Outsiders gain more traction by mastering the language of insiders than by staying plain-spoken and authentic. In a controlled experiment, the identical pitch was rated 20 percent higher by expert panels when it was delivered with insider jargon. Organizations that lack this fluency can use dedicated outside-in translators. Natura uses cultural translators to bridge cosmetic science and Indigenous ecological knowledge. IBM has technology advocates, and Philips has cross-pollinators.

A second experiment, with life-sciences professionals, rated identical ideas higher when they were attributed to an insider and the participants had been primed with a collective mindset. The ranking reversed when the same participants were primed toward independent thinking. A small nudge toward valuing individual judgment therefore reduces bias against outsiders' ideas. Sequoia Capital applies this by having its partners evaluate each deal independently before they meet to deliberate together.

### Inflection points

Systems under acute pressure, known as inflection points, become unexpectedly receptive to outsider ideas that they had dismissed. Katalin Karikó's research on mRNA was rejected by pharmaceutical companies and venture capitalists for years. When COVID-19 created an inflection point, her work, which later won a Nobel Prize, became indispensable. BioNTech's partnership with Pfizer shows the same pattern at the level of organizations.

### Business-model novelty needs a system

A study of roughly 300 firms, spanning the dot-com era and the digital economy of the 2010s, concluded that novelty in a business model does not by itself predict success. It identified seven essentials that separate the top performers from firms that generate buzz without a lasting advantage. The summary line of the study is to think of the system and not the sizzle.

1. **Pair novelty with operational discipline.** Spotify combined novel subscription bundling with strong discipline. Clubhouse's novel format lacked it and stalled.
2. **Treat efficiency as a strategic advantage,** and not as an afterthought about cost. Shein and Tesla did this.
3. **Fit the business model to the company's wider positioning.** Warby Parker and Southwest are examples.
4. **Recognize AI-driven business-model redesign as a distinct category.** In Duolingo and Netflix, the system and not only the product is redesigned around AI.
5. **Build sustainability into the core model.** Patagonia and Ørsted did not bolt it on.
6. **Match the scale and timing of a bet to market readiness.** OpenAI and Microsoft moved quickly, while Canva and Adobe moved more cautiously.
7. **Recognize that novelty is necessary but never sufficient.** Airbnb, Square, Stripe and Nvidia each needed a full supporting system of pricing, partnerships and lock-in to turn novelty into lasting value.
