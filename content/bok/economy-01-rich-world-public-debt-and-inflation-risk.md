## Summary

The Economist argues that the public debt of advanced economies, near 110 percent of GDP and close to an all-time high, has no politically workable path to repayment. This chapter explains why the usual routes are blocked. Spending cuts face the political power of an aging population, immigration only defers the problem, faster growth from AI would not rescue budgets and large tax rises are hard to enact. History shows that large economies have rarely repaid debt from budget surpluses. Of five possible exits from unsustainable debt, the Economist concludes that tax rises, default and inflation remain, and that inflation is the most likely, with holders of long-term bonds the likeliest losers.

## Key Ideas

- Gross public debt in advanced economies is near 110 percent of GDP, close to an all-time high, and interest costs are about one and a half times defense spending.
- No threshold defines too much debt. A stable ratio can turn explosive with a one-point rise in interest rates or a fall in growth.
- Since 1913, large economies have rarely repaid debt through surpluses. Britain's cut of 131 points of GDP between 1947 and 1956 came about half from inflation above interest rates.
- Aging, immigration and faster growth each fail to solve the problem, for reasons that the Economist sets out.
- Economies with low debt are distinguished by budget rules adopted after fiscal scares, and not by their political systems.
- Of five exits from unsustainable debt, the Economist concludes that tax rises, default and inflation remain, and that inflation is the most likely.

## Rich-World Public Debt and Inflation Risk

### The size of the problem

The Economist's special report of October 2025 frames government debt as an invention whose temptation Hume and Hamilton already feared. A state that is creditworthy can always borrow more, so debt tends to ratchet upward until the bond market pushes back. Crises of this kind were once a poor-country phenomenon. The largest advanced economies now combine record debt with persistent deficits. See [Rich-World Public Debt and Inflation Risk](#/concept/rich-world-public-debt-and-inflation-risk).

Gross public debt in advanced economies is near 110 percent of GDP, a level previously seen only after the Napoleonic wars. Average deficits are above 4 percent of GDP, and above 6 percent in the United States. Interest costs are about one and a half times defense spending. A rise in interest rates after 2022 has made debt far costlier to carry, and ten-year yields in most large rich economies are higher than when central banks began cutting short-term rates in 2024. Governments have adopted fiscal rules, such as America's pay-as-you-go rules and the European Union's Stability and Growth Pact, but they routinely suspend or evade them. There is little appetite for tightening. America has spent its windfall from tariffs and extended tax cuts financed by deficits. France has lost five prime ministers in two years over modest budget trims. Japan's incoming leadership favors expansion despite having the highest debt among major economies. Bond markets are responding, with long-term yields at multi-decade highs in Britain and Japan and French long-term yields approaching Italy's.

### A paradox of benign conditions

The conditions are, ironically, benign. There is no major recession, and interest rates in many countries are below growth. In principle most rich countries could therefore run small primary deficits, which exclude interest payments, and still stabilize their debt. Britain needs a primary surplus of only 0.3 percent of GDP. But the starting points are far from that. The required tightening exceeds 2 percent of GDP in Britain and America and 3 percent in France. Future pressure from aging, defense and the climate transition, which the IMF puts at nearly 6 percent of GDP a year by 2050 across Europe's advanced economies, is not yet priced in. Forecasts of debt also tend to be too optimistic. Britain's fiscal watchdog underestimated deficits five years ahead by 3.1 percent of GDP.

No debt ratio is inherently too high. A stable ratio can become explosive with a modest rise in interest rates or a fall in growth. A government seen as unwilling to tighten can be run on like a bank, because fear of a crisis itself raises the cost of financing. The Economist's own simulations show America and France on rising paths in every scenario, while Italy and Japan are the most sensitive to economic conditions.

### What history shows

History offers little comfort for the idea that large economies repay debts from surpluses. Before 1913, Britain ran about nine decades of primary surpluses and cut its debt from 194 percent to 28 percent of GDP between 1822 and 1913. France halved its ratio between 1896 and 1913. America nearly eliminated its debt with surpluses from 1867 to 1913. All of this happened under a gold standard that ruled out inflating debts away.

After the Second World War, by contrast, rich governments spent roughly half of the years from 1945 to 1980 gaining more from inflation than they paid in interest, with annual savings of 1 to 5 percent of GDP. Britain's reduction of 131 points of GDP between 1947 and 1956 came about half from inflation above interest rates. America's smaller reduction relied more on growth. The only post-war debt consolidation in the G7 driven mainly by budget adjustment was Canada's in 1997 to 2006. Defaults by leading economies are now taboo, with Greece and Cyprus in the 2010s the exceptions, but they happened even in the twentieth century.

### Why the usual routes are blocked

**Spending cuts.** The politics of aging make adjustment look electorally impossible. Public spending has shifted heavily toward the old, who keep power over the design of benefits. Pensions were set when retirement was short, but retirement ages have lagged life expectancy. Transfers to the elderly and health care have grown by about 5 percent of GDP across the OECD since 1980. Even minor reform provokes a backlash. More than a million French people marched against raising the retirement age from 62 to 64. An IMF analysis of party manifestos in 65 countries finds that both left and right have become more expansionist since about 1990, followed by larger deficits. The report also records better news. Employment has risen as a share of the population since 2000, healthy aging could add about 0.4 points to annual global growth from 2025 to 2050, and aging populations may lower real interest rates by more than a point by 2100. If budgets are eventually balanced, higher taxes on higher earners are the likeliest route, because the rest of the state has already been squeezed.

**Immigration.** It mechanically raises GDP and tax receipts, but it only defers the problem, because migrants age too. Housing and infrastructure constraints generate a backlash. A Bank of Canada finding links a 1 percent rise in the skilled migrant population to local house prices that are 6 to 8 percent higher. The fiscal value varies enormously with skill. A young migrant with a graduate degree brings America about $2.3 million in discounted lifetime federal fiscal benefit, against less than $15,000 for one without a high-school diploma. Closing America's forward fiscal gap of $163 trillion would take an implausible 71 million high-skilled migrants.

**Faster growth, including from AI.** Productivity growth helps less than assumed. The Congressional Budget Office estimates that half a point of extra productivity growth would cut the debt ratio in 2055 from 156 percent to 113 percent. But pensions and health spending rise with wages. And, following a standard model of growth, higher expected growth raises demand for investment and lowers saving, which pushes up real interest rates and can worsen the sustainability of debt. Global capital flows carry American growth into everyone's borrowing costs, so Europe risks importing higher rates while its own productivity stays flat. AI could also raise demand for redistribution as knowledge work is disrupted.

### Why some countries avoid the problem

A significant minority of rich economies, a third of rich-world output, have net debt under 50 percent of GDP. They are the junior Anglosphere, Scandinavia, the Rhineland economies and the Asian tigers. The explanation is institutions and not the political system. Older research linked fragmented, coalition-based politics to overspending, but it no longer predicts outcomes. Consensus-oriented Rhineland and Scandinavian states have low debts, while majoritarian Britain and America are deep in deficit.

What distinguishes the frugal countries is that they adopted budget rules after fiscal scares. The examples are these.

- Sweden adopted a rule of a surplus over the economic cycle, after its crisis in the early 1990s.
- New Zealand introduced a budget and strict rules in 1991.
- Switzerland adopted a debt brake in 2003, which 85 percent of voters backed.
- Germany capped its deficit in 2009.

Many of these countries also have pension systems that are pre-funded, with assets above 100 percent of GDP.

Rules have limits. They can be gamed in adversarial systems, and no country has ever been fined under the European pact. Tight constitutional limits cost flexibility, and Germany relaxed its debt brake in 2025 for infrastructure and defense. Polarization creates a war of attrition in which each side prefers delay to austerity. The scope of the political deal that is needed grows with each year that adjustment is postponed.

### The five exits

Gregory Mankiw's five exits from unsustainable debt are spending cuts, extraordinary growth, large tax rises, default and large-scale creation of money. The Economist narrows them by elimination, for the reasons above, to tax rises, default and inflation. It judges inflation the most likely, and a standing risk for holders of long-term bonds. The real danger is therefore not outright default but inflation, because nominal debts lose real value as prices rise, and the pressure on central banks to tolerate it grows as debt mounts.

The Economist's leader warns that inflation redistributes wealth arbitrarily, from creditors to debtors and from holders of cash to owners of assets. It could coincide with transfers caused by AI in labor markets and inheritance. The leader cites the decline of Argentina as the cautionary parable.
