## Summary

A Financial Times investigation traces Apple's manufacturing excellence back through a chain of teachers and students to Homer Sarasohn, an American engineer who taught quality management to Japanese executives in postwar Japan. This chapter follows that chain through Steve Jobs's years at NeXT and Pixar to the process he rebuilt at Apple. It then describes Apple's supply-chain playbook, which drew manufacturing knowledge from suppliers, wrote it into patents and specifications that Apple owned, and used that ownership to bring up competing suppliers. The same approach weakened Japanese manufacturers, but in China it helped build industrial rivals.

## Key Ideas

- Apple's manufacturing excellence is usually credited to Steve Jobs's vision and Jony Ive's design, but it also rests on a chain of knowledge transfer that began in postwar Japan.
- Homer Sarasohn found that Japan's quality problem was one of management and not of technology, and he taught that quality must be a total system across the entire organization.
- Jobs rejected quality process at first, and then adopted it at Pixar and rebuilt Apple's product process around continuous improvement in 1997.
- Apple's supply-chain playbook was to codify a supplier's best manufacturing process, own the resulting intellectual property and use it to bring up rival suppliers.
- The playbook turned Japanese electronics brands into subcontractors, but in China the suppliers used what they absorbed to build leading companies of their own.

## Quality, Manufacturing and the Apple Legacy

### Postwar Japan and Homer Sarasohn

The Financial Times traces Apple's manufacturing excellence, conventionally credited to Steve Jobs's product vision and Jony Ive's industrial design, back through an unbroken chain of knowledge transfer. It begins in postwar Japan. In 1945, General Douglas MacArthur, who was rebuilding occupied Japan, found its communications industry so shattered that he could barely issue orders. He sent Homer Sarasohn, a 33-year-old American physicist and radar engineer, to rebuild it. See [Apple's Manufacturing Quality Legacy](#/concept/apple-manufacturing-quality-legacy).

Sarasohn found no facilities and no experienced managers. He concluded that the poor quality of Japanese manufacturing came from management and culture and not from a technical deficiency. When he asked employees how to improve quality, they murmured among themselves, trying to guess what answer would please him, and did not answer directly. With a colleague, Charles Protzman, he spent a month writing an industrial-management textbook and designed a compulsory eight-week course for Japan's top managers. The course taught that quality must be a total system spread across the entire organization, and not confined to an inspection department.

His students included executives from Fujitsu, Hitachi, NEC, Mitsubishi Electric, Toshiba, Sanyo and Sharp. He also taught private classes to Masaru Ibuka and Akio Morita, who founded the startup that became Sony. He recommended W. Edwards Deming and Joseph Juran as his successors, and both became far more famous than he did. Sarasohn died largely forgotten, a few weeks before Jobs unveiled the iPod.

### Steve Jobs learns about process

At his first company, NeXT, Jobs at first dismissed quality process as bureaucratic and opposed to his philosophy of hiring geniuses and applying pressure. Two quality experts from Sarasohn's lineage, Noriaki Kano and Joseph Juran himself, convinced him in 1990 and 1991 that quality required treating the entire organization as a repetitive process, to be measured and improved continuously, and that talent alone was not enough. These lessons came too late to save NeXT, which abandoned manufacturing by 1993.

Jobs applied them at Pixar. The studio had two different standards of quality, because burnt-out stars made up the A team and inexperienced staff made up the B team. Adapting Japanese process discipline to film production fixed this. Co-founder Ed Catmull called the resulting ability of any employee to "pull the cord" the studio's defining moment.

### Rebuilding Apple's process

When Jobs returned to Apple in 1997, he tore up the company's New Product Process. A former manufacturing engineer described it as having hardened into incredible bureaucracy, with siloed vice presidents who did not talk to each other. With Jon Rubinstein and Glen Miranker, both Motorola alumni who knew Japanese quality methods, he rebuilt it around kaizen, the Japanese practice of continuous improvement. The rebuilt process took the iPod from concept to shipping in only nine months by 2001. Tim Cook joined in 1998 and added Toyota-inspired lean management. He treated time as a competitive advantage, saying that every minute is a million dollars lost.

Scale changed what quality meant. iPod volumes grew from hundreds of thousands of units in 2002 to 50 million five years later. According to Tony Fadell, a defect rate of 1 percent that felt sufficient at small scale would have produced a ton of waste at Apple's eventual volume. Apple grew from one or two production lines to dozens running in parallel, with quality checks built into every station to prevent defects from being passed along.

### The supply-chain playbook

Apple first preferred a single supplier for each component, to avoid lowering its engineering standards to the lowest common denominator. It dropped that approach once iPod volumes reached tens of millions. Apple now found the single best supplier for a component, often in Japan. It filmed and codified every step of that supplier's process, down to the angle and pressure of a craftsman's polishing wrist on the iPod Classic's steel back. It then automated the technique and scaled it across dozens of competing suppliers, mostly in China. The suppliers themselves compared the tactic to corporate espionage.

Legal terms made this possible. Apple's standard purchase-order terms require suppliers to assign Apple all ownership rights, title and interest in jointly developed intellectual property. That let Apple place its own engineers inside a supplier's factory. Sharp, after an Apple investment of nearly $1 billion, allowed a secretive Apple-only office of 50 people inside its LCD plant. Apple absorbed the supplier's process knowledge, and then used the same intellectual property to bring up rival suppliers and make the original innovator's product a commodity. Four Japanese academics labeled this a captive form of governance in a 2021 paper.

### The same playbook in Japan and China

The playbook worked as Apple intended against Japan. Over roughly two decades it turned once-dominant consumer brands such as Sony, Sharp and Panasonic into what the Financial Times calls invisible subcontractors inside Apple's products. In China it produced the opposite result. Suppliers such as BYD, which began supplying Apple metal casings around 2008, used what they absorbed to build entirely new world-leading companies. BYD now outsells Tesla in electric vehicles.

The investigation traces the difference to a different strategic posture on the Chinese side. China's approach dates to the late 1970s, when Deng Xiaoping visited factories in Singapore and Japan and saw how far behind China had fallen. China had an enormous, densely concentrated, low-wage workforce that was unskilled but willing to learn. The creation of Shenzhen as a special economic zone, together with deliberately low export costs, drew in foreign capital and expertise. The aim was that China's workforce would absorb tacit knowledge, which is the hands-on skill that can only be learned by doing. Chinese businesspeople have a term for the dynamic, yang-tao-sha, which means raise, entrap and slaughter. It describes using foreign partners to build national industrial capability, in the bet that capability would last longer than any single company's profits. It is the reverse of the smiling curve, which places low value on manufacturing and high value on design and branding at the two ends of the curve.
