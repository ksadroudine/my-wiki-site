## Summary

Digital transformation succeeds or fails on culture, process and continuous adaptation far more than on the choice of technology. This chapter draws on accounts from six established companies, a four-year Harvard Business School study of more than 8,300 leaders and the earlier Digital Masters research. It explains why building a digitally dexterous workforce is the real goal, why chief executive leadership and people who translate between groups matter, and why the work takes much longer than leaders expect. It also covers the five ways companies reinvent their business models and the Pfizer case, where the obstacle was never the technology itself.

## Key Ideas

- Technology is necessary but never sufficient. The hardest constraints in digital transformation are culture, process and the willingness to change how people work.
- Leaders who make the most progress define the goal as a digitally dexterous workforce, meaning people who are both willing and able to use new technologies.
- Progress is greater when the chief executive and the senior team lead the effort personally, and when people called bridgers translate between groups with different priorities.
- Building digital dexterity takes much longer than leaders expect. One executive planned three years and needed almost eight.
- Companies strong in both digital capability and leadership capability were 26 percent more profitable than their average industry competitor.
- Pfizer's breakthrough came when it stopped treating its manufacturing problem as a software installation and started treating it as a question of workforce capability.

## Leading Digital Transformation

### Technology is not the bottleneck

Six established companies, Moody's, Honeywell, IKEA, Levi's, Adobe and Intuit, described their own transformations in the Harvard Business Review. Their accounts agree on one point. Adopting new tools is necessary, but it is never enough. Transformation also requires rethinking how a company creates value, how its strategy works and how employees do their jobs, together with a real change in culture. See [Digital Transformation Leadership](#/concept/digital-transformation).

The accounts differ in what they stress.

- **Moody's** faced generative AI in late 2022 with no proven uses. Its leaders decided that doing nothing was riskier than uncertainty. They set three guidelines. Every employee would be involved, through a 14,000-person effort to win buy-in. Legal, compliance and risk staff would adopt a "yes, and" posture and engage with new ideas instead of rejecting them by reflex. And the work would stay focused on measurable business value even without a precise end goal. The chief executive called this sprinting into the fog.
- **Honeywell** began by confronting its own sprawl, which included more than 150 enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, 2,700 applications and 1,700 websites. It first simplified the infrastructure, reducing its 150 or more ERP systems to 10, its 2,700 applications to fewer than 1,000 and its 1,700 websites to fewer than 100. It then built master databases for products, employees and customers. Only after that did it decide how to use the consolidated data. The foundation let Honeywell add a cloud-based software layer on top of its hardware business.
- **IKEA** redesigned supply chains, inventory, logistics and fulfillment, and it rethought customer experience so that the line between digital and physical retail dissolved. Its chief digital officer at the time described the changes that customers see as the tip of an iceberg, resting on much larger shifts in the business model and operating model underneath.
- **Levi's** found that culture was the harder problem. Its perfectionist mindset, with six months of planning and six more of refinement before a launch, had to give way to an agile, digital-first way of working. It drew four lessons. Accept that failure is normal during a major transition. Aim to leapfrog competitors and not to catch up. Use the transformation to build data literacy across the organization. And allow real time for technical confidence to grow.
- **Adobe** argues that the risks of AI should be judged in context, for specific circumstances and uses, and not for AI as one undifferentiated technology. It applies four principles. Human involvement remains critical. Hype does not equal quality. AI's continuing evolution can be anticipated and not only reacted to. And an ethics framework is required.
- **Intuit** is digital-native, yet it still faces pressure, because customer expectations reset continuously and today's breakthrough becomes tomorrow's baseline. It treats transformation as continuous work, with regular, rigorous technical reviews that leave egos aside, AI experts placed inside teams and not centralized, and clear escalation routes that turn conflict into an advantage.

### Building a digitally dexterous workforce

A four-year Harvard Business School study followed leaders through case studies, roundtables with more than 240 leaders and annual surveys of more than 8,300 leaders in 109 countries. Leaders who made more progress did not define the goal as deploying tools. They defined it as building a digitally dexterous workforce, which is one that is both willing and able to use new technologies such as generative AI.

Leaders have named the same five organizational traits every year since 2021 as most important for digital agility. They are decisions that are informed by data, collaboration across functions, a focus on the customer, continuous learning and comfort with change. The wording matters. Employees resist when data is presented as replacing their judgment, which is data-driven, and accept it when it complements their judgment, which is data-informed.

Leadership matters too. In 35 percent of the surveyed companies, the chief executive or the senior team led the transformation personally, and those companies reported more progress. Among leaders who reported greater progress, 80 percent described their senior team as very or extremely tech-savvy, against only 36 percent of those who reported less progress. Generative AI has made data savviness one of the top three leadership qualities, after adaptability and curiosity.

Bridgers are people who build partnerships across organizational boundaries by translating between different priorities, limits and working styles. They help leaders reconcile employee anxiety about job security with real excitement about new tools. One airline hired a dedicated bridger team to connect every division with research institutions, universities, startups and venture firms.

Digital dexterity takes far longer than leaders expect. One executive planned for three years and needed almost eight, and six of those years went into building a single trusted source of data before the question of workforce capability could even be addressed. The clearest sign of success is that digital stops being a named initiative and becomes an assumed part of how the business runs.

Leaders' views of generative AI's effect on jobs shifted sharply in one year. In 2024, 80 percent called it complementary. In 2025 only 53 percent did, and 43 percent called it substitutive. The same leaders kept investing in digital dexterity programs.

### What the Digital Masters did

The earlier Digital Masters research surveyed 431 executives at 391 companies. Companies that excelled at both digital capability and leadership capability were 26 percent more profitable than their average industry competitor, and they earned 9 percent more revenue from their existing physical capacity. This is a correlation, and it does not prove cause, but the practical advice for managers is the same either way.

Digital Masters change customer experience through four related steps.

- They study customer behavior and design the experience from the outside in.
- They invest in new digital channels to raise reach and engagement.
- They put customer data at the center of the entire experience.
- They blend physical and digital experience, and do not replace one with the other.

Leadership alignment sets them apart. Of the Digital Masters, 82 percent agreed that their senior leaders shared a common vision of digital transformation, and 71 percent said the vision was shared between senior and middle managers. Among other companies the figures were under 30 percent and 17 percent. Two-thirds of the Digital Masters described their vision as genuinely radical, against 31 percent of all respondents.

Five patterns of business-model reinvention recur across the companies studied.

1. **Reinventing industries.** Multisided platforms such as Hailo and Airbnb gather fragmented markets and build network-effect barriers to entry.
2. **Substituting products or services.** A company cannibalizes its own revenue on purpose, before a digital substitute displaces it against its will.
3. **Creating new digital businesses.** Nike layered new digital revenue streams onto its existing products.
4. **Reconfiguring value delivery.** Volvo moved to a business-to-business-to-consumer model that serves end customers directly without cutting out its dealers.
5. **Rethinking value propositions.** A company recombines existing products, services and data for existing or new customers.

Despite broad enthusiasm, only 7 percent of surveyed companies said their digital initiatives were launching new businesses.

Two further ideas help to choose where to aim. The strategic assets worth building a digital vision around should be tested against the VRIN criteria. They must be valuable, rare, inimitable and nonsubstitutable. And digital ambitions fall into three tiers of rising difficulty and payoff. Substitution replaces an existing function with a new technology. Extension widens what the function can do. Transformation redefines a process or product fundamentally, and does not merely speed it up.

### Pfizer: when the obstacle is organizational

Pfizer spent nearly two decades failing to digitize the paper batch records in its drug manufacturing, and it broke through only in 2025. The obstacle was never the electronic batch record technology. It was organizational. Governance was unclear between the central digital teams and the manufacturing sites. The digital team lacked credibility with manufacturing. And a one-size-fits-all deployment model treated plants as recipients of a solution, not as co-designers.

The biggest turning point was a change of framing, from implementing a manufacturing execution system, which is the software that runs production, to enabling its capabilities. Pfizer also chose sites strategically. It selected sites that together covered 80 percent of revenue at 50 percent of the cost of transforming every site. It also spent up to three months diagnosing each site before any technical work began.

The results followed. Go-live schedule accuracy rose from 60 percent to over 80 percent. Yearly deployments of electronic batch records rose by more than 50 percent. The cost of implementing the system at a single site fell by 80 percent. The foundation of trust, governance and a repeatable process later made possible advanced optimization of production batches and AI-driven process control. Those could not have been added to the earlier paper-based state, with its disconnected islands of digital tools, at any cost.
