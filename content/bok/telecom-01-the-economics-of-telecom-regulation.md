## Summary

Three economic ideas shaped about a century of US telecommunications regulation, which are network effects, economies of scale and density, and the risk that a monopolist will extend its power into adjacent markets. This chapter, based on the book Digital Crossroads by Jonathan Nuechterlein and Philip Weiser, explains how each idea justified government intervention. It traces the history from rate-of-return regulation before 1996 through the Telecommunications Act of 1996, which gave new entrants the right to connect to incumbents and to lease parts of their networks. It then explains how the internet's layered design shaped the later debates over open access and net neutrality.

## Key Ideas

- Network effects, economies of scale and density, and monopoly leveraging explain most government intervention in telecom markets.
- A platform monopolist usually has little reason to monopolize an adjacent market as well, unless its own price is regulated or the adjacent market has advantages of its own.
- Rate-of-return regulation gave incumbents a reason to over-invest, and implicit cross-subsidies invited new entrants to take the most profitable customers.
- The Telecommunications Act of 1996 answered all three concerns at once, through interconnection, leasing of network parts at cost-based rates and conditions on long-distance entry.
- The internet's layered design let developers innovate without owning the network, and it made control of the physical layer the central policy question once broadband replaced dial-up.

## The Economics of Telecom Regulation

### Three economic ideas

The book Digital Crossroads explains that three related economic phenomena provided the case for roughly a century of US regulation of telecommunications. See [Telecom Regulatory Economics](#/concept/telecom-regulatory-economics).

1. **Network effects.** A network is worth more to each user as more people join it. In the early days of telephony, no rule required networks to interconnect, so markets tended to tip toward a single dominant network.
2. **Economies of scale and density.** A larger carrier can spread its fixed costs over more customers, so it can undercut smaller rivals on the cost of serving each customer. This added to the tendency to tip.
3. **Monopoly leveraging.** A dominant provider of a platform may have an incentive to extend its power from the platform, such as local telephone lines, into an adjacent market, such as long-distance service or equipment.

### The one-monopoly-profit rule

The book qualifies the third idea with the one-monopoly-profit rule. An unregulated platform monopolist usually has little independent reason to monopolize a complementary market as well, because it can already capture nearly all of the available value by pricing the platform itself. Monopoly leveraging therefore becomes a real concern mainly in two cases. One is when the platform's own price is regulated, which removes that pricing lever. The other is when the adjacent market has its own network-effect or scale advantages.

### Rate-of-return regulation and cross-subsidies

For most of the twentieth century, US regulators managed local telephone monopolies through rate-of-return regulation. A carrier could set retail rates high enough to cover its costs plus a reasonable return on its invested capital. This created a well-known perverse incentive, which was to over-invest in physical assets, called gold plating, because a larger regulated asset base meant a larger allowable profit. Price-cap regulation later replaced it in many places, to remove that incentive. A price cap ties allowed rate changes to inflation and a factor for efficiency improvement, and not to the incumbent's actual invested capital.

Regulators also used the system to fund universal residential service through implicit cross-subsidies. Rates averaged across regions, and above-cost rates for business customers, second lines and long-distance access paid for below-cost residential service on a customer's first line. These were politically convenient, but they created a weakness. The most profitable first move for any new entrant was to cherry-pick, which means to serve only the customers that paid the largest implicit tax. This shaped where competition first appeared, in long distance and then in special access for large businesses, well before the 1996 Act opened local markets.

### The Telecommunications Act of 1996

The Act was a systematic attempt to address all three concerns at once within the telephone network.

- **Sections 251 and 252** addressed network effects and the scale gap between incumbents and new entrants. Entrants gained a statutory right to interconnect their networks with an incumbent's. They also gained a right to lease, or unbundle, capacity on the incumbent's network facilities at regulated, cost-based rates. These rates followed a method called TELRIC, which stands for total element long-run incremental cost. It priced access not on the incumbent's actual historical network design but on what a hypothetical, maximally efficient carrier would need to spend to build an equivalent network today.
- **Section 271** addressed monopoly leveraging. It conditioned the regional Bell companies' entry into long-distance service on first meeting a checklist of local-competition obligations. The theory was that letting them into long distance before real local competition existed would let them use their dominant local position against long-distance rivals.

### The internet's layers

The later chapters of the book apply the same logic to the internet. They rest on its layered architecture, which has four layers. The physical layer is the wires or spectrum. The logical layer is the transmission protocols. The applications layer is browsers and servers. The content layer is the words, images and media that are exchanged.

This structure enabled the internet's explosive growth. The protocols at the logical layer were kept open and standardized, and they were governed cooperatively through bodies such as the IETF and the W3C and not by any single company. Independent developers could therefore innovate freely at the applications and content layers without having to become transmission providers. Competition and innovation could happen at every layer at once.

The same structure made control of the physical layer the central policy question once broadband began to replace dial-up access. Dial-up relied on independent internet service providers, which stood as intermediaries between applications and content providers and the telephone-network monopoly. Broadband access comes from a much smaller number of cable and telephone incumbents, and it threatened to push those independent providers out entirely.

Three regulatory responses emerged, each aimed at a different layer.

- **Open access** requires that several internet service providers can use the network, which aims to keep independent providers as competitive intermediaries at the logical layer.
- **Net neutrality** rules constrain what content or applications a broadband provider may block or degrade, at the applications and content layers.
- **Continued unbundling** of the physical layer aims to stimulate direct competition between network owners, in order to reduce reliance on rules of behavior altogether.
