## Summary

Marketing teams have moved from excitement about generative AI to the harder work of putting it into production. This chapter explains why AI is not new to marketing technology, how to map any AI capability by its level of autonomy and its technique, and which four prerequisites to meet before adopting it. It describes four groups of marketing executives by maturity and a three-step framework for moving up. It also covers a framework for taking generative AI from experiment to production, which looks at people, process, implementation and measurement.

## Key Ideas

- Generative AI is not martech's first encounter with AI. Predictive AI had been standard for more than a decade.
- Any AI capability can be placed on a map with two dimensions, which are autonomy and technique.
- Four prerequisites come before adoption, which are sufficient data and training, continuous monitoring, measurement that justifies investment, and privacy and security.
- Marketing executives fall into four groups, which are Integrators at 26 percent, Explorers at 40 percent, Considerers at 29 percent and Rejectors at 4 percent.
- Only 18 percent of business-to-business marketing decision-makers had taken generative AI from experiment to production.
- Generative AI should be judged both on efficiency and on its effect on customer experience and revenue.

## Generative AI in Marketing

### AI was already in martech

A December 2023 Forrester report cautions against overhype. Predictive AI, which identifies likely future outcomes from historical data, has been standard in marketing technology for more than a decade. Of the US marketing executives in consumer businesses who were surveyed, 54 percent already said that AI contributed to marketing execution before generative AI's public debut. Only 15 percent had rated improving AI capability a top strategic priority as recently as mid-2022. See [Martech Stack and Go-to-Market Strategy](#/concept/martech-stack-strategy).

The report offers a map for any AI capability, with two dimensions.

- **Autonomy** runs from collaborative AI, in which the marketer keeps the final say, to independent AI, which acts largely unsupervised. Lights-out automation in ad bidding and send-time optimization already works this way.
- **Technique** distinguishes predictive AI from generative AI, which creates original content from prompts in natural language and is much newer.

Beyond creative content, the report points to emerging uses. Dynamic insights build measurement into every interaction, so that campaigns can be optimized in real time, and they can even generate synthetic data to simulate the effect of a journey while it is running. Collaborative workflows extend analytical, creative and orchestration capability that was once reserved for specialists. High-velocity engagement speeds up the entire marketing-operations cycle. Anticipatory journeys combine predictive and generative AI to generate journeys from prompts.

### Four prerequisites

Technical planning is often thin. Only half of the AI decision-makers surveyed reported a documented technical framework or architecture in their AI strategy. The report therefore sets four prerequisites before generative AI is adopted.

1. **Sufficient data and training.** Marketing works with IT, enterprise architecture and data science to gather and prepare company-specific data that tunes a public language model toward on-brand output. Data quality was already among the biggest execution challenges for 31 percent of marketing decision-makers in consumer businesses.
2. **Continuous monitoring.** Testing runs across the AI lifecycle, with independent reviews for bias, invented answers and unintended consequences, and an enterprise-wide governance framework. In the survey, 64 percent of AI decision-makers worried that misuse could lead to errors in the organization.
3. **Measurement that justifies investment.** Teams track both the lift in marketing measures and the efficiency gains, so that the time saved visibly pays for more resources, training and skills. Of the AI decision-makers surveyed, 57 percent had documented outcomes, targets and return-on-investment goals.
4. **Comprehensive privacy and security.** The risk of putting sensitive business and customer data into public language models calls for privacy by design. Regulatory action, such as a US presidential executive order and EU restrictions on some open-source generative AI, and the third-party risk of reaching open-source models through martech vendors, add to the need.

### Four kinds of marketing executive

A February 2024 report starts from the observation that the honeymoon is over, because more than a quarter of AI decision-makers cited integrating generative AI with existing infrastructure as the biggest barrier to adoption. It profiles four groups of US marketing executives in consumer businesses.

- **Integrators**, 26 percent, use generative AI across the team in go-to-market work, often through centralized agency platforms. They increasingly focus on data quality as capabilities become multimodal.
- **Explorers**, 40 percent, pilot generative AI in specific tasks. They advance by adding uses such as media operations or content localization, while addressing employees' fears of losing their jobs.
- **Considerers**, 29 percent, are interested but not yet experimenting. They need to prioritize realistic uses and assess their readiness in technology, data and skills.
- **Rejectors**, 4 percent, do not intend to use generative AI in execution, typically because of heavy regulation or unconvinced leaders. They can still benefit by outsourcing to external generative AI marketing partners.

Advancing matters, because generative AI is projected to enhance 4.5 times more marketing jobs than it eliminates. Adoption is therefore a challenge of augmentation and skills.

A three-step framework supports the move from one group to the next.

1. **Make adoption an explicit objective.** Identify outcomes tied to business goals, secure a sponsor at chief marketing officer level to legitimize pilots and unlock funding, and build the investment case for spending on AI providers.
2. **Invest in a task force that builds expertise.** Find passionate volunteers to act as evangelists, adopt a repeatable test-and-learn method with checkpoints at 30, 60 and 90 days, and reward adaptability as pilots show what works.
3. **Create a playbook for responsible execution.** Make continuous training routine and not limited to isolated pockets, build alliances with AI partners for early access, and promote policies on tools and approved uses that match the organization's tolerance for risk.

### From experiment to production

A November 2024 report closes the gap between urgency and deployment. Only 18 percent of business-to-business marketing decision-makers had advanced generative AI from experimentation to production. Most were still experimenting on historical data (54 percent), within live production workflows (53 percent) or in isolated sandboxes (46 percent). They typically layered uses on existing processes and did not build new capabilities.

Common uses are content generation, such as verticalized copy for account-based campaigns, natural-language interfaces and application assistants. ActionIQ's audience tool, for example, creates a segment from a plain-language request and returns both an explanation and the underlying query so that the user can check it. Adobe's assistant answers questions such as how to create a campaign brief.

The report proposes four dimensions, to be worked through for each use case and not applied as one rollout for everyone.

**People.** Generative AI adoption is a cross-functional project. Marketers are accountable for the investment, the outcomes and the suitability of what customers see. IT extends its management of technology and data to fast-changing language models. Data scientists manage complexity, explainability and observability and check performance. Stewards set governance and manage the life cycle of data and models across engineering, data science, security and legal.

**Process.** A five-step cycle repeats for each use case.

1. **Ideate.** Decide which uses support customer goals and marketing strategy, by aligning goals set at the top with pain points raised at the bottom.
2. **Forecast.** Assess readiness in the ecosystem, the team and the culture, together with technical, ethical and legal risk.
3. **Prototype.** Put a working model in employees' hands. GoDaddy's internal platform for content as a service, for example, lets every team experiment with predictive and generative models through a shared data layer.
4. **Prioritize.** Weigh feasibility, effort and return on investment. Dell's chief AI officer used three criteria, which were customer experience, business growth and business productivity, to narrow 800 use cases to 36.
5. **Activate.** Decide whether to continue, pivot, pause or stop, and communicate the decision with realistic timelines for scaling. An Adobe customer in Asia and the Pacific began with repurposing images, which saved $300 per image, and used that proof to win support from IT, legal and security.

**Implementation.** There are three ways to get access, each with trade-offs. Generative AI embedded in a third party's technology is easiest and fastest but less customizable, may add cost and brings the privacy risk that the vendor introduces. Public language models, open or closed, trade flexibility, control and data privacy against easier integration and scale. Open models need technical expertise and a budget. Closed models cost more to access and allow less customization. Building a proprietary model offers the most control but needs substantial investment.

**Measurement.** Few companies or vendors were discussing measurement. Generative AI should be judged on efficiency, meaning productivity gains and usage, and on effectiveness, meaning the incremental lift on existing measures. Those measures span customer experience, such as satisfaction, and business results, such as opens, clicks, conversions, revenue, acquisition cost and lifetime value, benchmarked through A/B testing against output that AI did not help to create. Persado, for example, cut customer requests for content revisions by replacing manual brand-guideline checks with a review and approval process run by a language model trained on the brand. As one chief product officer put it, generating a report or brief is good for productivity, but chief executives should care about customer experience and revenue lift.
