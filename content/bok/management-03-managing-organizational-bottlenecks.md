## Summary

An organizational bottleneck is a point where work stalls. This chapter explains the two kinds, which are task bottlenecks, where one task waits on another, and resource bottlenecks, where the needed resource is not available. It describes the four factors behind them, namely centralization, complexity, fungibility and slack, and a study of 28 large US airlines showing that performance is best when each pair of factors is aligned and not when any one factor is minimized. Southwest Airlines' storm meltdown in 2022 and JetBlue's failure in 2007 illustrate what goes wrong. The chapter ends with a five-step process that managers can follow.

## Key Ideas

- A task bottleneck arises when a task waits on the output of another task. A resource bottleneck arises when the resource that a task needs is not available.
- Adding resources can solve a resource bottleneck, but it does not necessarily solve a task bottleneck.
- Task bottlenecks depend on how centralized and how complex the work system is, and resource bottlenecks depend on fungibility and slack.
- Performance is best when each pair of factors is aligned, and not when any one factor is minimized.
- Task bottlenecks and resource bottlenecks affect each other, so fixing one kind can leave the other in place.
- Too little slack makes disruptions systemic, but too much slack is costly because idle resources cost money even when they are never needed.

## Managing Organizational Bottlenecks

### Two kinds of bottleneck

Bottlenecks show up as tasks that are stalled for one of two reasons. A task bottleneck arises when a task depends on the output of another task that has not yet been completed. A resource bottleneck arises when the resources needed to complete the task are not available. Resource bottlenecks can be solved by investing in more resources. Task bottlenecks cannot necessarily be solved by simply adding resources. See [Organizational Bottleneck Management](#/concept/organizational-bottleneck-management).

### What drives task bottlenecks

Two features of the work system drive task bottlenecks.

- **Centralization** is the degree to which many activities flow into or out of a few connecting tasks, which creates dependencies. A hospital with one shared blood laboratory serving every specialty is centralized. One with separate laboratories for each specialty is decentralized.
- **Complexity** is the number of tasks in a sequence before it repeats. Diagnosing a rare infectious disease is more complex than treating common appendicitis.

### What drives resource bottlenecks

Two features of the resource portfolio drive resource bottlenecks.

- **Fungibility** is the degree to which a resource can be applied to several uses. An infusion pump is more fungible than a dialysis machine, which is essentially single-purpose.
- **Slack** is how much of a resource is available at any given time, which provides a buffer against surges in demand.

The two work together. A company benefits from more slack only when its resources are also fungible. It benefits from fungibility only when the resources are also available. A resource that is present but inapplicable, or applicable but absent, does not help.

### Alignment, not minimization

A study of 28 of the largest US airlines, covering 1,201 quarterly observations from 2002 to 2020, found that performance is best when the factors are aligned and not when either of them is minimized on its own.

- Work of low complexity performs best when it is centralized. Cycles are short and need less coordination, so centralizing is simpler and cheaper than running redundant parallel tasks.
- Work of high complexity performs best when it is decentralized. Long cycles carry more risk of failure, so running similar tasks in parallel reduces the chance that one blockage stalls the entire system.

### Two airline failures

Southwest Airlines' response to a severe winter storm in December 2022 shows misaligned task and resource characteristics compounding each other. Rivals operate hub-and-spoke networks. Southwest flies point to point, which left planes and crews scattered across airports, with none in position at some of them. Its cycles were long, so its work was highly complex, and it had no slack resources to reposition quickly. Recovery stretched to nearly 3,000 cancellations on a single day, followed by three more days with more than a thousand each.

JetBlue's operational meltdown on Valentine's Day in 2007 affected more than 130,000 customers and led to the airline's customer bill of rights. It shows the cost of too little slack. There were too few people to handle both thousands of rebookings at once and the reassignment of flight crews. A weather disruption became a systemic failure. The same article cautions that excess slack is also costly, because idle resources cost money even when they are never needed.

### How the two kinds interact

Task bottlenecks and resource bottlenecks do not operate independently. Even a well-aligned task system will produce task bottlenecks if resource bottlenecks exist elsewhere, because the resources needed to unblock tasks cannot be allocated. Even well-aligned resources, which are fungible and available, will produce resource bottlenecks if task misalignment keeps unfinished tasks holding on to their assigned resources, so that those resources cannot be reallocated.

### A five-step process

Managers can follow a practical process.

1. **Locate and identify** the specific bottlenecks.
2. **Classify** each one as driven by tasks or by resources.
3. **For a task bottleneck, check for a hidden benefit of the delay** before concluding that it is pure waste. A delay can sometimes allow information to be gathered for a better decision.
4. **For a task bottleneck, redesign the degree of centralization to match the complexity of the sequence.** Short, simple sequences stay centralized, and long, complex ones are decentralized or shortened.
5. **For a resource bottleneck, find out why the resource is missing.** If it is unavailable, add slack. If it cannot be applied, add fungibility. Both excess slack and excess specificity carry real costs.
