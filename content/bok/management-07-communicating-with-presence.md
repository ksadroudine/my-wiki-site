## Summary

Now that AI can write scripts, polish slides and coach delivery, what distinguishes a business communicator is presence, meaning a verifiable human appearing in a widening range of formats. This chapter explains why trust in AI-generated content erodes and why audiences want conversational formats. It describes communication as a system in which one message moves through several formats, the five formats that each need their own mastery and why casual-looking formats are not easier to produce. It ends with what AI cannot do, which is to read a room, and with advice on shaping one's own career narrative.

## Key Ideas

- AI can write scripts, polish slides and give feedback on tone, but audiences still want a real person to appear when the moment matters.
- Trust in AI-generated content erodes because AI cannot explain how or why it reached a conclusion.
- Communication is now a system. One message moves through several formats, and it must be redesigned for each one and not merely repackaged.
- Five formats each need their own mastery, which are written, virtual live, virtual asynchronous, facilitated and staged.
- Casual-looking formats are not faster to produce. A panel takes as much planning as a staged talk.
- What AI cannot do is read a room, and audiences value the sense that the presenter cared enough to make their time worthwhile.

## Communicating with Presence

### Why presence matters

AI can now generate presentation scripts, polish slides and give real-time feedback on vocal tone during rehearsal. Audiences still want a real person to appear when the moment matters, because human-to-human connection is what lets them trust both the message and the messenger. Presence, and not content quality alone, has become the most strategic communication skill in business. See [Presence-Based Communication](#/concept/presence-based-communication).

Trust erodes in particular because AI cannot explain how or why it reached a given conclusion, especially when the content carries real consequences. According to the 2025 Freeman Trust Report, 95 percent of respondents said they trust brands more after attending an in-person event. Even TED, which was built around formal talks by one speaker, is shifting toward conversational formats such as demonstrations, interviews and question-and-answer sessions.

### Communication as a system

Communication has become a system and not a single moment. A message may travel through several formats before it lands. It might start as a written document, become a panel discussion, then short video clips cut from that discussion, and then social posts. The same content has to be deliberately redesigned for each format's own constraints, and not simply repackaged.

Presentation stages are moving from monologues by one speaker toward moderated conversations among several presenters. This mirrors the rise of podcasts and reaction videos. The shift seems casual, but it requires as much planning as a traditional formal presentation, and not less. Aligning the message arc, preparing several speakers, planning the transitions and anticipating questions all need the same strategic work.

### Five formats

Five formats each require independent mastery.

- **Written formats** include memos and Slidedocs. A Slidedoc is a document built from slides that is meant to be read and not presented. These formats build buy-in before a meeting and last as leave-behinds.
- **Virtual live formats** are real-time video meetings, which are now routine for 86 percent of workers. They are seen as trustworthy because the delivery is face to face, even through a screen.
- **Virtual asynchronous formats** are recorded video or audio updates and pitches. They are harder to deliver with real presence, because the speaker addresses a camera and not a person.
- **Facilitated formats** are panels and live question-and-answer sessions. They are designed to feel spontaneous but need detailed advance planning of tone, timing and roles.
- **Staged formats** are keynotes for high-stakes moments. They are now designed from the start to be cut into shareable clips and quoted across other channels, and not treated as a single, self-contained event.

### A myth about casual formats

It is a myth that casual-looking formats are faster to produce than formal ones. A panel takes as much planning as a staged talk. A podcast episode can take more editing than a slide deck. A town hall without slides can demand more preparation than one with them, because every word, pause and transition carries more of the communicative weight.

### What AI cannot do

What AI cannot do is read a room. Audiences are less interested in being impressed or dazzled by content. They are more interested in feeling that the presenter cared enough to make their time worthwhile. Organizations that treat communication as a core operating discipline, and not as an afterthought laid on top of real work, gain an advantage that AI does not easily erode.

### Telling your own story

The executive coach Andrea Wojnicki advises leaders to manage their career narrative through a self-introduction in three parts, which are present, past and future. She recommends turning perceived liabilities into strengths, so that an accent becomes global experience. She also advises telling the path to leadership through lessons and not as a chronological list. A short piece in the Globe and Mail makes a related point. Hybrid work, social media and scrutiny of leaders' credibility make it more important to shape one's own story deliberately than to leave others to do it.
