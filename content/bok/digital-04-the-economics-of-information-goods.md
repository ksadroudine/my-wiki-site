## Summary

Information goods such as software, films and research reports are expensive to create the first time but almost free to copy. This chapter, based on the book Information Rules by Carl Shapiro and Hal Varian, explains why cost-based pricing fails for such goods, why sellers price by the value to each customer and why buyers cannot judge information before they consume it. It also covers how network effects make growth a strategic necessity, why only two market structures last in the long run, how lock-in and standards battles work and how an incumbent can defend itself against copycats.

## Key Ideas

- Information goods have high fixed costs for the first copy and almost no cost for each further copy, so cost-based pricing does not work.
- Sellers price by the value a customer places on the information, which leads to charging different prices to different groups.
- Information is an experience good every time it is consumed, because buyers cannot judge it before they have it.
- Network effects make growth a strategic necessity, because more users raise the value of the product for everyone.
- In the long run only two market structures last. In one, a single firm holds a durable scale advantage. In the other, many firms each offer a distinct variety.
- A firm should encourage competition among suppliers of its partners' components while preventing a strong rival from emerging in its own component.

## The Economics of Information Goods

### Pricing a good that costs almost nothing to copy

Information Rules by Carl Shapiro and Hal Varian starts from one observation about cost. An information good has a high fixed cost to create the first copy, such as writing the software, making the film or researching the report. After that, each extra copy costs almost nothing to reproduce and distribute. See [Information Goods Economics](#/concept/information-goods-economics).

This has a direct effect on pricing. Cost-based pricing means adding a fixed percentage markup to the unit cost of production, which is the usual approach for physical goods. It does not work for information goods, because unit cost is close to zero whatever value a copy delivers to its buyer. Information must instead be priced by the value a particular customer places on it.

Customers value the same information very differently. A stock quote is worth far more to an active trader than to a casual investor. Value-based pricing therefore leads naturally to differential pricing, which means charging different amounts to different customer groups for essentially the same good. Physical goods markets limit this approach, because the cost of making each unit sets a floor under the price.

### Information as an experience good

Information is an experience good every time it is consumed, and not only on the first purchase from a brand. A buyer usually cannot judge whether a piece of information is valuable without experiencing it, and experiencing it is exactly what reveals the value. This creates a tension. The seller must show what is on offer, and also needs to be paid for it.

Information industries have used several ways to resolve the tension. Newsstands allow browsing, radio plays previews of songs and cinemas show trailers. More durably, branding and reputation let a buyer form expectations about new information from the producer's track record, without experiencing each new item first. Free distribution of samples online, and even of full content, follows the same logic and is not unique to the internet.

### Network effects and growth

A product has network effects, also called network externalities, when its value to a user depends on how many other people use it. These effects create a demand-side form of economies of scale, on top of the usual savings from making more units. That is why growth itself becomes a strategic necessity in network markets. Each new user does more than spread the fixed costs. Each one also raises the value of the product for every existing user.

### Only two lasting market structures

Given the cost structure and the network effects, the book argues that an information market can sustain only two structures in the long run.

- **A dominant-firm market**, in which one firm achieves a durable cost advantage through scale. The example is Microsoft's long dominance of desktop operating systems.
- **A differentiated-product market**, in which many firms coexist because each offers a distinct variety of essentially the same type of information. Examples are publishing, film, television and much of the software industry.

The firm's core strategic choice is therefore close to binary. It can pursue cost leadership through scale in a market of the first kind, or genuine differentiation in a fragmented market of the second kind. Strategies that fall between the two tend to be unstable.

### Lock-in, complements and standards

Information systems are usually assembled from complementary components supplied by different companies, and no single firm makes the entire system. Switching costs and lock-in are therefore common. A sophisticated firm watches its collaborators as closely as its direct competitors. The book recommends that a firm encourage several competing suppliers for the component that its partners provide, which keeps that side of the ecosystem competitive and cheap. At the same time, it should work to prevent a strong rival from emerging for its own component, which protects its position in the system.

The same logic applies to competition over standards. A go-it-alone strategy tries to make a firm's own technology the market standard. It is a high-stakes bet in which the winner takes all, and the book cautions against it unless the firm can be genuinely aggressive on pricing, timing and its relationships with makers of complementary products. The alternative is to join a formal, cooperative standard-setting process or to build an alliance around a shared technology. The firm then competes within an already agreed standard. This is a much lower-risk position, because the firm gives up the chance to own the standard and gains less exposure to loss.

### Defending against copycats

An incumbent information provider that faces commoditization by copycat entrants has two pricing responses.

- **Limit pricing** means deliberately giving up some short-term margin by pricing low enough that entry becomes unattractive to a would-be competitor.
- **A reputation for retaliation** means building a credible name for pricing aggressively against any new entrant. This is costly if it triggers an actual price war, but it pays for itself over time by discouraging future entry.
