## Summary

An operating model describes how an IT organization is structured and run to deliver its services. This chapter explains why the older shared-services model, built on hierarchy and rationing, no longer fits the cloud, and walks through Forrester's six layers for designing a replacement, which are customers, value, capabilities, structure, governance and leadership. It also covers the growing role of outside partners, the redesign of application architecture around composability and the shift from a controlling center of excellence to a center of enablement.

## Key Ideas

- The older shared-services model rationed scarce resources through hierarchy, because data centers could not meet every demand, and the cloud removes that constraint.
- Many early cloud operating models were assembled informally over a decade, and scale and AI workloads now call for deliberate redesign.
- Forrester's six layers are customers, value, capabilities, structure, governance and leadership, and each layer raises questions that an organization must answer for itself.
- Digitally advanced enterprises rely more on outside services partners, treating them as part of the innovation system and not as interchangeable suppliers.
- Application architecture should be modular, so that business processes can be reconfigured quickly, and it should let business staff build their own solutions.
- Oversight of sourcing works better as a center of enablement that helps the business than as a center that controls every move.

## The Cloud Operating Model

### Why the old model no longer fits

The traditional shared-services model of IT relied on hierarchy, triage and rationing. On-premises data centers could not scale to meet competing demand from inside and outside the company, so someone had to decide whose request came first. The cloud removes that structural limit. This is why early cloud operating models need rework. Many were pieced together informally over a decade of use, and they now face greater scale, a growing range of managed cloud services and the surge of AI workloads.

Forrester offers a six-layer framework for the redesign. Each layer poses questions that the organization must answer for itself. See [Cloud Operating Model Design](#/concept/cloud-operating-model-design).

### Layer one, customers

The first task is to define who the cloud serves. Internal stakeholders include developers, business users, security, governance, risk and compliance teams (GRC), procurement and senior executives, and each has a distinct interest in the cloud. External stakeholders are customers and partners, for whom the cloud may mean faster new features, lower cost, higher resilience or easier collaboration outside the firewall.

### Layer two, value

The second layer defines the value delivered. It includes the journeys that stakeholders go through, the specific cloud offering and its boundaries, including service level agreements and training resources, and a value proposition that goes beyond acting as a secure and compliant broker. The value proposition should gather and inspire stakeholders around their needs. The layer also covers the experience delivered, since shared-services models were known for long lead times, even for basic requests.

### Layer three, capabilities

Capabilities have five parts.

- **Activities** are mapped to value streams, which are the sequences of work that deliver value to a customer. Mapping avoids a sprawl of services.
- **Metrics** need rethinking. The shared-responsibility model, in which the provider and the customer each secure part of the environment, complicates the older uptime metrics. Teams move toward observability, meaning detailed insight into what systems are doing, and toward AI-assisted tools that find the root cause of a failure.
- **Skills** remain a constraint. The roles in demand include cloud administrator, cloud architect and solution architect, and increasingly cloud security engineer and FinOps practitioner, who manages cloud spending.
- **Competencies** are the abilities that the organization must own and those it can source from partners or the cloud provider.
- **Technologies** include governance tools, developer platforms and incident-management systems.

### Layer four, structure

Structure has five elements.

- **Reporting lines** for cross-functional teams may need a two-in-a-box arrangement, in which one leader holds business accountability and another manages technical careers.
- **The definition of work** clarifies scope and ownership, in place of the narrow, siloed definitions of traditional IT.
- **Operating units**, such as a cloud center of excellence and a cloud business office, coordinate across units beyond the formal reporting lines.
- **Location** matters less, because the cloud lets operating units be placed virtually.
- **Interactions** describe how units affect one another's ability to deliver value. They can be independent, coordinated, contracted or collaborative.

### Layer five, governance

Governance has five parts.

- **Security** uses Zero Trust, a model in which no user or device is trusted by default, as its foundation, and it maps the shared-responsibility model carefully.
- **Compliance** means building the GRC functions directly into the operating model, since a provider's own regulatory documents will not automatically match an organization's control framework.
- **Decisions** need a formal framework and catalog, to counter the cloud's constant pressure toward decentralized and inconsistent choices.
- **Transparency** means real-time dashboards, which replace the narrow, hierarchical reporting of the private data center era.
- **Accountability** uses RACI charts, which show who is responsible, accountable, consulted and informed. These charts now cover more participants and move at a faster pace than they did in the data center era.

### Layer six, leadership

Leadership covers six matters.

- **Vision** was rarely needed in early cloud adoption, but cloud-native technology and AI workloads now require an expansive vision tied to organizational goals.
- **Strategy** is the day-to-day reference point that puts the vision into practice. One example is adopting multicloud to address vendor concentration and sovereign-cloud rules.
- **Culture** matters because cloud technology is designed to support empowerment and collaboration, but an organization has to embrace that deliberately and not fall back on old habits.
- **Performance** is measured against one of four styles of IT, which are enabling, amplifying, cocreating and transforming, and not by automatically adopting the newest cloud service.
- **Values** matter because a cloud provider becomes a long-term strategic partner, and its policies on privacy and responsible AI become the customer's concern too.
- **Motivation** means freeing IT staff from the rigidity of shared services so that they can form empowered platform teams.

### Partners, architecture and sourcing

A companion Forrester report finds that digitally advanced enterprises rely far more on outside services for digital transformation. The share is 90 percent for advanced enterprises, 79 percent for intermediate ones and 69 percent for beginners. These enterprises treat services partners as a core part of the innovation ecosystem, to be aligned strategically, and not as interchangeable suppliers.

Modernizing application architecture means giving priority to three goals. Composability lets business processes be reconfigured quickly during market turbulence. Democratized work styles upskill citizen developers, who are business staff building their own solutions. ConfigOps centralizes configuration management as application portfolios become decentralized and hard to manage.

Sourcing governance should move away from an ivory-tower center of excellence that controls every move, toward a center of enablement that works closely with the business. It should use several tiers of oversight, calibrated to scale, risk and the need for speed, instead of one rigid approach for everything.
