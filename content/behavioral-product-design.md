## Summary

Successful products have to be both usable and habit-forming, and two frameworks address these needs. Nir Eyal's Hook Model describes a four-step cycle of trigger, action, variable reward and investment that turns repeated use into habit, and the investment step, such as a completed profile or accumulated history, is what separates a habit-forming product from one that is merely engaging. Don Norman's human-centered design principles make products usable in the first place by designing from how people actually behave and think instead of from what customers say they want or what engineers want to show off. Norman also treats unused features as a cost, since each one clutters the interface and makes the useful features harder to find.

## Highlights

- The Hook Model describes a four-step cycle — Trigger (external or internal cue), Action (the simplest behavior done in anticipation of reward), Variable Reward (an unpredictable payoff that sustains interest better than a predictable one), and Investment (the user puts something in — time, data, effort, content — that increases the odds they'll return and improves the product for their next use) — that repeats and strengthens into habit with each cycle. <span class="src">Hooked</span>
- The Investment phase is what distinguishes a genuinely habit-forming product from a merely engaging one, because what the user invests (a completed profile, an accumulated history, a follower graph) both raises the psychological cost of switching to a competitor and loads the next external trigger, priming the user to return. <span class="src">Hooked</span>
- Human-centered design, per Don Norman's account, treats unused product features as actively negative rather than neutral, since every added feature clutters the interface and degrades the discoverability of the features that actually matter — a diagnosis echoed directly in Consumption Economics' account of feature overshoot. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>
- Products should be designed from an understanding of actual user behavior and mental models, not from what customers explicitly request or from an engineering team's desire to demonstrate technical capability — the same three causes of feature overshoot Norman identifies recur across multiple sources in this vault's product-design literature. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>

## Concept

Behavioral product design covers two complementary disciplines captured in this vault: designing products that people habitually return to, and designing products that people can actually use in the first place. Nir Eyal's Hook Model addresses the first discipline directly, describing habit formation as a repeating four-step cycle. It begins with a Trigger — either an external cue (a notification, an email) or, once a habit is established, an internal cue (boredom, loneliness, a specific emotional state that the user has learned the product can address) — which prompts an Action, defined as the simplest behavior a user can perform in anticipation of a reward (a single tap or swipe, deliberately minimized in friction). The Action is followed by a Variable Reward, a payoff whose exact content or magnitude is unpredictable from one instance to the next; the model's central behavioral-psychology claim is that this unpredictability sustains user interest and engagement far more powerfully than a fixed, fully predictable reward would, drawing on the same intermittent-reinforcement mechanism that makes gambling and social-media feeds compelling. The cycle closes with Investment, in which the user puts something of their own into the product — time spent, data provided, content created, a growing history or social graph — that serves two functions simultaneously: it raises the psychological and practical cost of abandoning the product for a competitor (since that investment would be lost or need to be rebuilt), and it loads the next external trigger, priming the user's eventual return and starting the cycle again. <span class="src">Hooked</span>

Human-centered design, associated in this vault's sources with Don Norman (author of The Design of Everyday Things), addresses a different and logically prior discipline: making a product's existing functionality genuinely usable, independent of whether it also forms habits. The most direct point of contact between Norman's design philosophy and the rest of this vault's product-strategy literature is his diagnosis of feature overshoot, cited at length in Consumption Economics: unused features are not simply a wasted but harmless investment, they are actively negative, because every added feature clutters an interface and measurably degrades the discoverability and usability of the smaller set of features that actually drive most of a product's value for most users. Norman traces this overshoot to three recurring causes that show up across product organizations regardless of industry — customers in every served market segment request incremental features specific to their own use case, competitors' shipped features create pressure to match them feature-for-feature, and engineers are intrinsically motivated to build and publicly demonstrate technically impressive new capability — implying that resisting feature overshoot requires a deliberate design and product-management discipline rather than simply "listening to customers," since customer requests themselves are one of the three drivers of the problem. <span class="src">Consumption Economics</span>

Read together, these two disciplines describe complementary failure modes a product team must avoid: a product that is well-designed and usable by Norman's standard but never forms a habit will struggle to retain users even if each individual session goes well, while a product engineered around the Hook Model's trigger-action-reward-investment cycle but cluttered with unusable, overshooting features will struggle to get users through the Action step cleanly enough for the cycle to ever engage. Both disciplines converge on treating "what the user actually does" — behavior and mental models — as the correct design input, in explicit contrast to treating stated customer requests or a team's own technical ambitions as sufficient guides to what should be built. <span class="src">Hooked</span>
