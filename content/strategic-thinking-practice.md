## Summary

Strategic thinking is developed mainly through informal, self-directed learning, not through formal training programs. Julia Sloan describes effective strategic thinkers as moving between two learning modes, surfing broadly across many domains to stay exposed to new ideas and diving deeply into a specific area when something demands it. The concept also covers the personal attributes of effective strategic thinkers. It sets these beside the central insight of game theory, which is that a decision cannot be judged in isolation from how other rational actors will respond, and that a credible commitment, a deliberate and visible reduction of one's own future options, can change how rivals behave.

## Concept

Learning to Think Strategically (Julia Sloan) treats strategic thinking as a learnable skill developed primarily through informal, self-directed learning rather than through formal training programs or credentialed education — a claim grounded in research on how experienced strategic thinkers describe having actually developed their own capability over time. The recurring pattern Sloan identifies is one of deliberate, ongoing exposure to ideas well outside a person's immediate professional domain, paired with active reflection on how those outside ideas connect back to the specific strategic problems the person is currently facing; strategic thinking capability, on this account, grows less from studying strategy frameworks directly and more from the habit of continuously translating unrelated input into strategic insight. Sloan formalizes this into two complementary learning modes that effective strategic thinkers move between rather than relying on exclusively: "surfing," a broad, low-commitment mode of staying exposed to a wide range of domains, sources, and perspectives without going deep into any one of them, and "diving," a focused, high-commitment mode of going deep into a specific domain once a particular strategic question demands genuinely detailed understanding of it. The implication is that a strategic thinker's informal learning "portfolio" needs both modes represented — pure surfing without occasional diving risks producing broad but shallow pattern-matching, while pure diving without surfing risks tunnel vision that misses relevant ideas from outside the immediate domain. <span class="src">Learning to Think Strategically</span>

Thinking Strategically (Avinash Dixit and Barry Nalebuff) contributes game theory's foundational insight to this vault's account of strategic thinking, even though only a single highlight from the book was captured in this ingest: the central claim that a decision cannot be correctly evaluated in isolation from how other rational actors are likely to respond to it — a decision that looks obviously good when analyzed alone can be a poor decision once a competitor's rational counter-response is factored in, and vice versa. One of the discipline's best-known specific applications of this principle is the value of credible commitment: deliberately and visibly reducing one's own future options (for instance, publicly and irreversibly committing to a course of action) can function as a source of strategic strength rather than weakness, precisely because it changes what response is rational for a competitor to make in return, closing off certain competitive responses that would otherwise have been available to them. <span class="src">Thinking Strategically</span>
