## Summary

The core vocabulary and history connecting artificial intelligence, machine learning, and deep learning, and what each term actually means. Covers the three types of machine learning (supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement), the three common neural network architectures, why deep learning specifically requires massive data and compute, Google's landmark 2016 case study of that shift in production, and a 2017 HBR framing of ML as a general-purpose technology with concrete early business impact and risks.

## Highlights

- AI, machine learning, and deep learning nest inside one another: AI is the broad goal of making machines perform tasks that normally require human intelligence; machine learning is the specific subfield that lets machines learn from data rather than being explicitly programmed for each task; deep learning is a further specialization of machine learning using many-layered neural networks, particularly well-suited to unstructured data like images and text. <span class="src">What Is AI (Artificial Intelligence)</span>
- Machine learning was first defined by Arthur Samuel in the 1950s as "the field of study that gives computers the ability to learn without explicitly being programmed" — a definition traditional programming can't satisfy for tasks like recognizing faces, where writing an explicit rule-based program is either impractical or impossible even though humans do it effortlessly. <span class="src">Machine learning, explained</span>
- Machine learning systems can serve three distinct functions: descriptive (using data to explain what happened), predictive (using data to predict what will happen), or prescriptive (using data to recommend what action to take) — and fall into three training subcategories: supervised learning (trained on labeled data, the most common type in production use), unsupervised learning (finding patterns in unlabeled data, useful for discovering unanticipated structure), and reinforcement learning (trained through trial-and-error against a reward signal). <span class="src">Machine learning, explained</span>
- Deep learning uses neural networks with many layers, each recognizing increasingly complex features of the data (an early layer might detect an edge, a later layer might recognize that edges combine into a stop sign), and generally needs less human intervention and produces more accurate results on unstructured data than traditional machine learning — at the cost of requiring much more computing power. <span class="src">What Is Deep Learning</span>
- Three developments made modern deep learning viable: Geoffrey Hinton's layered self-training method (each layer trains on the previous layer's output, allowing the network to build up from simple to complex feature recognition); the sheer volume of digitized data now available to train on, since deep learning is fundamentally a brute-force pattern-detection process requiring vastly more examples than a human needs to learn the same concept; and the repurposing of GPUs (originally built for video-game graphics) for parallel neural-network computation, which sped up training by orders of magnitude. <span class="src">Deep Learning Will Radically Change the Ways We Interact With Technology</span>
- Three types of neural network architecture serve different purposes: feed-forward networks (information flows one direction only, well suited to tasks like flagging a single transaction as fraudulent based on its own features); convolutional neural networks or CNNs (modeled on the brain's visual cortex, well suited to perceptual/image tasks like medical-scan diagnosis or logo detection); and recurrent neural networks or RNNs (connections loop backward as well as forward, well suited to sequences like detecting fraud across an entire transaction history rather than one transaction in isolation). <span class="src">What Is Machine Learning</span>
- A foundation model is a deep learning model trained on transformer-network architecture over vast quantities of unstructured, unlabeled data, usable out of the box for a wide range of tasks or adapted to a narrower task through fine-tuning (a much shorter follow-up training pass on a smaller, labeled data set); large language models are the subclass of foundation models specialized for processing and generating natural language text. <span class="src">What Is Deep Learning</span>
- A basic glossary of terms recurring across the AI/ML literature: algorithm (rules telling a machine what to do with input data), hallucination (confidently-presented fabricated or inaccurate output), natural language processing/NLP (machines analyzing and generating human language), neural network (interconnected processing nodes loosely modeled on brain neurons), and token (the sub-word unit of text an LLM processes and generates). <span class="src">Artificial Intelligence, Explained</span>
- Google's 2016 overhaul of Google Translate is the canonical real-world case study of the "three enabling developments" converging in production: engineers replaced the old phrase-based statistical translation system with a neural-network-based one (Google Neural Machine Translation) trained on Google's vast trove of translated text and run on custom-built Tensor Processing Units, producing a jump in translation quality so large that some observers described it as nearly closing the gap to human translators overnight. <span class="src">The Great A.I. Awakening</span>
- By 2016, the same deep-learning capability once confined to elite research labs was becoming accessible to nonspecialists through user-friendly frameworks and pretrained models, mirroring how earlier eras of software development moved from specialist-only to broadly accessible — a shift Google's own Jeff Dean framed as urgent, telling a conference audience "if you're not using deep learning already, you should be." <span class="src">Machine Learning Is No Longer Just for Experts</span>
- A 2017 HBR framing situates ML among history's general-purpose technologies (steam engine, electricity, internal combustion engine) — each catalyzing waves of complementary innovation — and argues machine learning overcomes "Polanyi's Paradox" (the 1964 observation that humans "know more than we can tell," historically blocking automation of tacit skills like recognizing a face) by letting machines learn from labeled examples rather than requiring an explicit hand-coded rule. <span class="src">The Business of Artificial Intelligence</span>
- Concrete 2017-era business impact: JPMorgan Chase's ML system for reviewing commercial loan contracts cut work that used to take loan officers 360,000 hours down to a few seconds; Infinite Analytics' ad-placement ML system tripled advertising ROI for a global consumer packaged goods company and drove a $125 million annual revenue increase for a Brazilian online retailer's search and discovery process. <span class="src">The Business of Artificial Intelligence</span>
- Machine learning introduces three specific new risks that traditional explicit-rule software did not carry to the same degree: hidden bias inherited from historical training data (e.g. a hiring model perpetuating past recruiters' demographic biases without any explicit biased rule being written), low "interpretability" since neural networks deal in statistical rather than literal truths, making it hard to prove a system will work in all cases, and difficulty diagnosing errors when they occur, since the underlying structure that produced a wrong answer can be too complex to unpack. <span class="src">The Business of Artificial Intelligence</span>
- Machine learning tends to redesign work at three nested levels rather than eliminating entire jobs outright: tasks and occupations (e.g. machine vision freeing radiologists to focus on critical cases and patient communication), business processes (Amazon redesigning fulfillment-center workflow and layout around robots and optimization algorithms), and business models (shifting from à la carte song sales to a personalized subscription station) — with the most effective division of labor typically automating one or two steps of a ten-step process rather than handing the whole process to a machine. <span class="src">The Business of Artificial Intelligence</span>

## Concept

Artificial intelligence, machine learning, and deep learning are frequently used as if interchangeable, but they nest inside one another as progressively narrower specializations. AI is the broad, decades-old goal of building machines that perform tasks normally requiring human cognition — perceiving, reasoning, learning, problem-solving. Machine learning is the specific technique, first defined by Arthur Samuel in the 1950s as "the field of study that gives computers the ability to learn without explicitly being programmed," that has become the dominant way most current AI is actually built: rather than a programmer writing an explicit rule for every case (the traditional approach, sometimes called "software 1.0"), the system is given data and learns its own internal rules from patterns in that data. This distinction matters because there are tasks — recognizing a face, for instance — that humans perform effortlessly but that resist being reduced to a hand-written explicit algorithm; machine learning solves exactly this class of problem by letting the machine essentially program itself through exposure to examples. Deep learning is a further specialization within machine learning, using neural networks with many layers to process especially complex or unstructured data (text, images, audio) with less human intervention and often greater accuracy than traditional machine learning techniques — at the cost of requiring significantly more training data and computational power. <span class="src">What Is AI (Artificial Intelligence)</span> <span class="src">Machine learning, explained</span>

Machine learning systems can be categorized by what they're designed to do — descriptive (explaining what already happened in the data), predictive (forecasting what will happen), or prescriptive (recommending what action to take) — and, separately, by how they're trained. Supervised learning trains a model on data that has already been labeled by humans (e.g. thousands of images individually tagged "dog" or "not dog"), letting the model learn to replicate that labeling on new, unlabeled examples; it's the most common approach in production systems today. Unsupervised learning gives the model unlabeled data and lets it surface patterns or groupings that weren't specified in advance, useful for discovering structure (like unexpected customer segments) that a human analyst wasn't explicitly looking for. Reinforcement learning trains a model through trial and error against an explicit reward signal, telling the system when it made a good decision so it can learn, over repeated attempts, which actions lead to better outcomes — the approach behind training systems to play games or drive autonomously. <span class="src">Machine learning, explained</span>

Within deep learning specifically, three neural network architectures serve different purposes depending on the shape of the problem. Feed-forward networks move information in one direction only, from input layer to output layer, well suited to tasks that evaluate a single instance on its own merits — flagging one financial transaction as potentially fraudulent based on its own features, for example. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are structured to mirror the organization of the brain's visual cortex, making them particularly well suited to perceptual tasks like identifying a disease from a medical scan or detecting a company's logo in a social media post. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) include backward-looping connections, letting them weigh a sequence of inputs against each other rather than judging one input in isolation — useful for detecting fraud not just from a single transaction but from how that transaction compares against an individual's entire prior spending history, or for predicting how a sequence of text or speech is likely to continue. <span class="src">What Is Machine Learning</span>

Three specific developments converged to make today's deep learning boom possible, none of which existed together before roughly the last decade. First, Geoffrey Hinton and colleagues at the University of Toronto developed a layered self-training method: an early network layer learns to recognize simple features (an edge, a contour) from massive exposure to raw data, and once trained, feeds its output to the next layer, which learns to recognize more complex features built from the first layer's output (a nose, an ear), and so on — hence the "deep" in deep learning, referring to the number of stacked layers rather than any depth of understanding. Second, digitization produced the sheer volume of data deep learning needs, since — unlike a child who can learn a new concept from a handful of examples — a deep learning system is fundamentally a brute-force pattern-recognition process that typically needs to be shown millions of examples to reliably learn a pattern (the article's example: showing a network 19 million cat pictures until statistically significant "cat" features emerge). Third, researchers realized that GPUs — originally built to handle the parallel visual-rendering demands of video games — could be repurposed to run the millions of simultaneous connections in a neural network, compressing what had previously taken weeks of training into a single day. <span class="src">Deep Learning Will Radically Change the Ways We Interact With Technology</span>

Google's 2016 overhaul of Google Translate is the field's own canonical illustration of these three developments converging in a real production system, documented in detail in <span class="src">The Great A.I. Awakening</span>. The Google Brain team replaced Translate's decade-old phrase-based statistical system — which translated text piece by piece with limited awareness of surrounding context — with Google Neural Machine Translation, a deep neural network trained end-to-end on Google's enormous accumulated corpus of previously translated text and run on custom Tensor Processing Units built specifically to accelerate neural-network computation. The resulting jump in translation quality was large enough that some bilingual reviewers described the system's output as approaching human-level competence almost overnight, a discontinuity the article frames as evidence that deep learning was shifting from a research curiosity to infrastructure capable of reinventing existing consumer products at global scale. The same historical moment saw deep learning begin moving beyond specialist research teams like Google Brain: <span class="src">Machine Learning Is No Longer Just for Experts</span> documents how increasingly accessible frameworks and pretrained models let a broader range of engineers experiment with techniques that had previously required deep specialized expertise, a democratization the article compares to earlier shifts in software development history, and one Google's own Jeff Dean urged practitioners not to ignore, declaring at a conference keynote, "If you're not using deep learning already, you should be."

Foundation models sit at the top of this technical stack: they are deep learning models built on transformer-network architecture (see [transformer architecture](#/concept/transformer-architecture)) and trained on vast quantities of unstructured, unlabeled data, which makes them usable out of the box across a wide range of tasks, or adaptable to a narrower specialized task through fine-tuning — a much shorter, cheaper follow-up training pass on a smaller labeled data set specific to the target task. Large language models are the specific subclass of foundation models trained and optimized for processing and generating natural-language text, and are the technology underlying the current generation of AI chatbots and writing assistants; their mechanics, capabilities, and limitations are covered in more depth in [llm mechanics and limitations](#/concept/llm-mechanics-and-limitations). <span class="src">What Is Deep Learning</span>

<span class="src">The Business of Artificial Intelligence</span> (HBR, Erik Brynjolfsson and Andrew McAfee, July 2017) is an early, still-influential framing of machine learning's business significance, situating it among history's "general-purpose technologies" — the steam engine, electricity, the internal combustion engine — each of which catalyzed waves of complementary innovation across unrelated industries (the internal combustion engine, for instance, eventually gave rise not just to cars but to big-box retailers, cross-docking warehouses, and suburbs). The article's central conceptual claim is that machine learning succeeds specifically because it overcomes "Polanyi's Paradox," philosopher Michael Polanyi's 1964 observation that humans "know more than we can tell" — we can recognize a face or ride a bike without being able to write down the explicit rules that let us do so, which historically blocked automating any task resting on that kind of tacit knowledge. Rather than a programmer writing an explicit rule for each case, supervised learning systems learn a mapping from labeled examples (pictures of animals to species labels, sound waveforms to words), letting the machine essentially program itself through exposure to data — the same mechanism this concept's other sources describe, but framed here through the lens of what kind of human knowledge automation had previously been unable to reach. The article documents concrete, still-cited 2017-era business impact: JPMorgan Chase's ML system for reviewing commercial loan contracts reduced work that used to take loan officers 360,000 hours down to a few seconds; Infinite Analytics' ad-placement ML system tripled advertising ROI for a global consumer packaged goods company and separately drove a $125 million annual revenue increase for a Brazilian online retailer's search and discovery process; and Udacity's chat-room sales-support system, built from ordinary chat logs treated as labeled training data, raised salesperson effectiveness by 54 percent after 1,000 training cycles. Alongside this promise, the article names three specific new risks machine learning introduces relative to traditional explicit-rule software: hidden bias inherited from historical training data (a hiring model can perpetuate past recruiters' demographic biases without any single biased rule ever being written); low interpretability, since neural networks deal in statistical rather than literal truths, making it difficult to prove with certainty that a system will behave correctly on cases outside its training data; and difficulty diagnosing errors once they occur, since the underlying structure producing a wrong answer can be too complex to unpack cleanly. The article's practical framework for where ML actually changes work operates at three nested levels — tasks and occupations (machine vision freeing radiologists to focus on critical cases and patient communication rather than routine image screening), business processes (Amazon redesigning fulfillment-center workflow and physical layout around robots and optimization algorithms), and business models (shifting from selling individual songs to a personalized subscription station) — with the most effective division of labor typically automating one or two steps of a longer process rather than handing an entire job to a machine, since designing new combinations of technology, human skill, and capital to meet customer needs is itself a task machines remain poor at. <span class="src">The Business of Artificial Intelligence</span>
