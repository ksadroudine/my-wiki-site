## Summary

Why a growing number of telecom operators are delayering into independent NetCo (infrastructure) and ServCo (services) entities, or pursuing strategic M&A consolidation, to escape the "conglomerate discount" the market applies to vertically integrated telcos. Covers the mechanics, real-world results, risks, and enabling conditions for each path.

## Concept

A traditional, vertically integrated telecom operator bundles together several businesses with genuinely different underlying economics: capital-intensive network infrastructure with long-term, utility-style payback profiles; higher-value, higher-risk product and service innovation with faster payback; and commerce and customer-service channels that require skills closer to a retail business than a network operator. Running all three under one roof, and one management team, dilutes leadership focus and forces compromises across fundamentally different planning horizons and capital needs — which is a large part of why investors apply what's described as a "conglomerate discount" to vertically integrated telcos' valuations, even when those telcos own genuinely valuable, high-multiple infrastructure assets. <span class="src">Seven urgent innovation priorities for telecom CEOs</span>

Delayering addresses this directly by separating the integrated telco into independent entities: a "NetCo" or "InfraCo" focused on operating network infrastructure (which, once separated, can become a carrier-neutral wholesale access provider serving other operators, not just its former parent), and one or more "ServCo" entities focused on products, sales, and customer care, which can further specialize by customer type, offering, business model, investment horizon, or skill set. Real operators have realized meaningful gains from this move: Turkcell generated $150 million (8 percent of total revenue) within three years of spinning off its digital-services portfolio into stand-alone platforms; Czech carrier O2/CETIN saw a 27 percent total market-cap increase in the year following its split, one of the earliest such moves in the industry; and some operators have realized as much as a 10-percentage-point EBITDA margin uplift and a tenfold increase in enterprise value multiple. Middle East operator e& (formerly Etisalat) took a more elaborate approach, splitting into five verticals including two geographic telco units, a consumer digital unit, an enterprise digital unit, and a dedicated capital-investments arm — a structure credited with improved talent attraction (each entity offers a distinct culture and career track) and sharper management focus. Despite these documented successes, the record is genuinely mixed rather than uniformly positive: delayering typically takes two to three years and upward of $1 billion in committed capital, and — notably — no telco that has separated into NetCo/ServCo structure has yet ranked among the industry's top total-shareholder-return performers, suggesting the transition costs and risks are real even when the destination proves worthwhile. <span class="src">Mapping the routes to renewed success for telcos</span> <span class="src">Bright Spots for Telcos in a Downturn</span> <span class="src">The critical bets on the future of telco value creation</span>

The downside risks of separation are specific and structural, not merely execution risk. A ServCo separated from its NetCo loses automatic preferential access to network resources — price differentiation, cross-subsidization, and control over product development tied to complex network integration all become harder to sustain across an arm's-length commercial relationship rather than internal coordination. The disintermediated NetCo, in turn, loses direct visibility into customer relationships that previously informed decisions about deployment priorities, densification, and maintenance scheduling. At a moment when the industry is broadly trying to become more customer-centric, delayering can also directly work against seamless omnichannel bundling and one-stop-shop service, since coordinating a unified customer experience across legally and operationally separate entities is harder than coordinating it within one organization — a tension compounded by stricter data-protection regimes like the EU's GDPR, which complicate aggregating and monetizing customer data across separated entities in ways that used to be straightforward internally. Because of these risks, successful delayering requires several enabling conditions to be deliberately engineered rather than assumed: genuinely independent units with a single-minded focus on pure-play growth rather than half-measures; close collaboration between the separated entities wherever legally permitted (shared data, shared best practices and governance) to preserve as much ecosystem benefit as possible; full performance transparency built around three to four targeted, differentiated KPIs per entity with no hidden cross-subsidies masking the true economics of either side; distinct talent and incentive models tailored to each entity's different needs; sharper, more targeted investor narratives that let each entity attract the investor base suited to its risk/return profile; and openness to new M&A, joint-venture, and partnership opportunities that the previously interconnected structure would have foreclosed. <span class="src">Mapping the routes to renewed success for telcos</span>

Strategic M&A and consolidation represent a more traditional, and in some ways less structurally disruptive, alternative or complement to delayering. Recent large-scale telecom mergers illustrate the scale of available synergies: the 2024 combination of Orange Spain and MasMovil targets €490 million in synergies after four years while the combined entity serves nearly half of Spain's fixed and mobile subscribers and close to a third of national industry revenue, and the 2025 merger of Vodafone UK and Three UK — creating the UK's largest mobile operator by subscriber and revenue share — targets £700 million in synergies by its fifth year. In-country consolidation specifically can unlock potential synergies exceeding 15 percent of combined capital expenditure and 30 percent of combined operating expenditure through economies of scale and differentiated positioning. But the caveat is significant: consolidation carried out without disciplined cost transformation and genuinely rigorous post-merger execution can destroy rather than create value, turning what looked like a straightforward scale play into a costly distraction — meaning the decision to pursue M&A should be evaluated with the same discipline applied to any other major capital allocation choice, not treated as an automatic win because scale itself sounds attractive. <span class="src">The critical bets on the future of telco value creation</span>

A third, less drastic strategic option sits alongside delayering and M&A: deliberately shifting the operator's own archetype toward a "utility-like" model, explicitly trading growth ambition for efficiency, reliability, and lower risk. This means prioritizing streamlined, leaner operations over volatile, highly competitive customer-facing growth; reducing capex intensity specifically through investment sharing (network sharing between operators, joint infrastructure entities, or third-party investment in wholesale network capacity); and actively pursuing regulatory support, including government funding, subsidized spectrum pricing, or cost-based pricing set through a regulatory-asset-base framework similar to how some countries regulate electricity or gas utilities. Notably, this path enjoys broad executive support in principle: close to three-quarters of surveyed telco leaders say their organization would benefit from shifting away from the traditional integrated telco archetype within the next five to ten years, and of those, the largest share specifically favors a utility-like shift over delayering — even though choosing this path implicitly concedes that market-beating revenue growth is not a realistic near-term goal, in favor of the more dependable, income-investor-friendly cash flows that utilities and infrastructure companies have historically commanded relative to growth-starved integrated telcos. <span class="src">Mapping the routes to renewed success for telcos</span>

- Beyond business-model reinvention, the same PwC research names three further CEO priorities telcos cannot defer: workforce renewal (46% of telecom employees don't believe their employer will still exist in ten years, and 32% plan to change jobs within a year, against a backdrop of retiring senior engineers and new skill demands from cloud, AI, and IoT); building resilience to cyber risk and regulatory change (cloud-related threats are the top-cited security worry, and regulatory deal scrutiny has risen sharply, e.g. a 50% rise in US CFIUS reviews since 2020); and building the sustainable carrier (addressing Scope 3 emissions along the supply chain as GenAI-driven compute demand adds further pressure on net-zero commitments). <span class="src">Seven urgent innovation priorities for telecom CEOs</span>
