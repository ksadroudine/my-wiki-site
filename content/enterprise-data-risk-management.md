## Summary

Why data risk needs to be managed as a company-wide business problem rather than a siloed IT or compliance issue, since every C-suite function (CDO, CFO, CRO, CISO, CCO) sees only a fragment of it. Covers PwC's five data-risk categories and a five-step framework for building enterprise-wide visibility and accountability.

## Concept

The article opens with a deliberate image: for many business leaders, viewing their organization's data is like trying to see the entire night sky through a straw — technically possible, piece by piece, but never as a coherent whole. This fragmentation is structural, not accidental: a chief data officer typically focuses on data governance and quality, a chief financial officer on the reliability of data feeding planning and decision-making, a chief risk officer on data integrity for accurate risk reporting, a chief information security officer on classifying, encrypting, and preventing loss of sensitive data, and a chief compliance officer on privacy and on coordinating the multiple departments touched by data-related compliance requirements — each a legitimate and necessary lens, but none of them, alone or in simple aggregate, providing genuine enterprise-wide visibility. The consequence is that data ends up functionally "at a dead end": disconnected from a single ecosystem, it can quietly undermine large-scale legacy-system migrations, slow AI adoption, constrain product-portfolio expansion, and degrade reporting and innovation, while eroding regulator and consumer trust if it isn't protected and collected safely. There are signs leadership is starting to recognize the stakes: in PwC's 2025 Global Digital Trust Insights survey, 48 percent of business executives said they're prioritizing data protection and data trust investments over the next year, ahead of technology modernization and enhancement — yet many organizations still default to treating data risk as a technology problem handled by IT, rather than the enterprise-wide business problem the article argues it actually is. <span class="src">Data risk is everywhere 5 steps to manage it</span>

Several forces are simultaneously raising the cost of this gap. Regulation keeps tightening — a steady stream of new global and state-level privacy laws (the Consumer Data Privacy Act, the EU Data Act, California's DELETE Act, the Protecting Americans' Data Act among them) makes noncompliance a growing legal exposure, not just a reputational one. Cloud transformation keeps accelerating, with 72 percent of "top performer" organizations in PwC's 2024 Cloud and AI Business Survey prioritizing data modernization and cloud migration. And AI adoption keeps compounding both trends, since 69 percent of the same survey's respondents said they've implemented cloud data modernization specifically to power AI and unlock insight across data sources — meaning data now needs to be simultaneously more accessible (to fuel AI) and more tightly controlled (to satisfy tightening regulation), a tension that traditional, IT-centric data governance alone is no longer equipped to resolve. Against this backdrop, the article defines data risk at its core as exposure to financial or reputational harm caused by loss, quality limitations, or related problems affecting an organization's ability to acquire, store, transform, move, protect, and use its own data assets — and breaks it into five concrete categories with distinct business consequences: data quality risk (entry errors, missing or misclassified records, poor system integration) leads to compliance problems, uninformed decisions, and direct financial loss; data protection risk (mishandled customer data, third-party vendor breaches, weak encryption, unsecured APIs) opens the door to privacy and security vulnerabilities that damage customer trust; data loss risk (cyberattacks, inadequate backup and disaster-recovery planning) produces outages, downtime, and response costs; data compliance risk (failing to properly identify, classify, or retain data for disclosure and audit purposes) invites direct regulatory scrutiny, fines, and disciplinary action; and data exposure risk (trade-secret or IP theft, insider threats, shadow IT and unsanctioned data usage) erodes access controls and, with them, competitive advantage. <span class="src">Data risk is everywhere 5 steps to manage it</span>

To close the resulting gap, the article proposes a five-step approach that moves organizations from fragmented, IT-owned data risk toward a genuinely enterprise-wide discipline. The first step is simply knowing what data risk actually is in the terms defined above, since organizations can't manage what they haven't clearly named. The second is pursuing overall — not partial — visibility, framed as a "digital paper trail" connecting the full data life cycle; visibility historically falls apart because IT departments manage technical aspects, compliance teams manage regulatory aspects, and each functional area keeps its own objectives, tools, and controls, with data logs often interpreted differently across environments, producing redundancy, inefficiency, and heightened exposure that business leaders need to get directly involved in resolving rather than deferring entirely to IT and compliance. The third step is rallying cross-functional teams — privacy, data, security, risk, and technology — to identify, document, and measure risk together as one unit rather than in isolation, paired with deliberate investment in upskilling: role-specific training for both technical staff (data engineers, analysts) and non-technical stakeholders (compliance officers, executives), certifications in data governance, security, and privacy, and engaging external experts to independently assess and validate systems. The fourth step is treating the right questions as an ongoing discipline rather than a one-time exercise, with the article proposing specific prompts tailored to six C-suite roles: whether departments keep data siloed and whether the organization is prepared to manage privacy and exposure risk as data becomes more accessible (CEO); whether the data underlying financial decisions is reliable and current, and how its quality is actually measured (CFO); whether data location and sensitivity are known across on-premises and cloud environments, with controls established across the full modernization cycle (CISO); whether data can be tracked, organized, and managed from collection through disposal, including legacy data (COO); what the major data risks and vulnerabilities are, and whether regulatory-change management is in place (CRO); and whether data policies are well defined and data is complete and accurate enough to support genuine business insight (CDO). The fifth and final step is to stop treating data risk as an afterthought relative to data-driven innovation — the article notes that while AI adoption has sharply raised C-suite awareness of data's value and spurred greater investment, many organizations are only beginning to grapple with the corresponding risk side of that equation, even though foundational capabilities like data discovery, cataloging, and lineage are prerequisites for handling both traditional data-deletion obligations and newer unstructured-data challenges. Changing how the C-suite views data risk requires not just new thinking but genuine cross-department accountability, extended to third parties and strategic partners as well, and the article's closing argument is that this shift — from a back-office IT concern to a front-and-center business priority — is what ultimately lets an organization capture data's full value rather than merely being exposed by it. <span class="src">Data risk is everywhere 5 steps to manage it</span>
