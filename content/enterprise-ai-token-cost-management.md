## Summary

Companies are shifting part of their operating expense from headcount to AI tokens, the units of computing used by AI models, but the total cost of AI agents stays hidden until it is measured directly. Token charges are only the visible part, because an agent also carries costs for licenses, platforms, governance, change management and failures, and a task that cost $0.04 two years ago can cost $1.20 now as agents add planning and tool use. Companies are already rationing AI use as bills rise, and one study found that only 18 percent of spending on AI coding tokens turns into shipped products. The sources describe Agent FinOps practices such as choosing smaller models for simpler work, measuring the cost of each task and tracking the return on AI spending, and they advise targeting controls at the few workflows where cost risk concentrates, not setting per-user caps.

## Concept

Enterprise AI spending is undergoing a structural shift that most organizations are not yet equipped to manage: from a predictable, fixed cost of software licenses and human headcount to a variable, consumption-based cost of compute, measured in tokens. EY frames the danger of managing this transition by only watching the most visible number — the token line on a model vendor's invoice — as equivalent to "managing a factory by watching the electricity bill." Its Agent FinOps framework breaks total agent cost into seven categories, only the first three of which most companies currently budget for: tokens and API calls (highly variable, visible only after the month closes, owned by CIO/CTO); subscriptions and licenses (fairly predictable, owned by CPO/CIO); platform infrastructure (step-fixed by tier, owned by CTO/CIO); governance burden (compounding as agent surface area grows, owned by CRO/CAE); organizational change (front-loaded per workflow, owned by COO/CHRO); expected failure and recovery (invisible until an incident occurs, owned by CFO); and potential future AI-specific regulatory taxes (currently only a signal, owned by CRO/GCO/CFO). The remaining four categories tend to emerge only later in an agent's deployment lifecycle, by which point they are much harder to plan for. EY's illustrative example makes the compounding vivid: a customer-service chat that cost $0.04 to answer two years ago, using a simple model call, can now cost $1.20 once tool retrieval, planning steps, and subagent orchestration are layered on top — the same task, transformed from a fixed unit cost into a variable and rising one. EY's three recommended near-term moves are to appoint a single Head of Agent Economics with visibility across all seven cost lines; to install "agentic circuit breakers" — spend ceilings, call-volume caps, and automatic shutoffs — before scaling any agent, since without them the bill only becomes visible after the damage is done; and to embed full total cost of ownership, not just model pricing, into every agentic business case from the outset, with a clear value metric (output, revenue, speed, quality, or risk reduction per dollar) attached to every agent from day one. Gartner's prediction that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027 — due to escalating costs, unclear business value, or inadequate risk controls — is, in EY's framing, not an inevitable outcome of the technology but a symptom of scaling agents before this kind of financial discipline is in place. <span class="src">Agentic AI Enterprise Token Cost</span>

Bain's three-part series on the same transition adds both an organizational lens and a granular breakdown of why the economics stay difficult even as headline prices fall. In part one, Bain describes many tech-forward CXOs converging on a mental picture of a future opex mix around 70-80% headcount and 20-30% tokens, but stresses there is no smooth glide path to that destination: at today's typical 1-2% token share of opex, an unbudgeted, multimillion-dollar token bill for a scaled rollout can still make a general manager "choke midquarter" simply because token spending doesn't map to any existing budget line or approval chain, and scaling a stellar pilot to 10,000 seats means routing through procurement, security, legal, data, and IT cycles built for an annual cadence rather than the pace of model releases. The friction, in Bain's account, is now organizational rather than technical — legacy coordination structures (sprint planning, multi-person deal reviews, escalation chains) increasingly read as friction from a previous era to the minority of employees who have already had a personal "this is it" moment with frontier AI, even as most of the organization is still stuck at "I tried Copilot a few months ago; it didn't work well." Bain frames this not as a training gap but as a widening "experiential chasm." Part two supplies the number underneath the provocation: the "cost-per-task paradox," in which model prices fall roughly tenfold per generation while the effective cost of actually completing a task stays stubbornly flat. Three forces work against the headline price decline simultaneously — users migrate to whichever model is newest and most capable rather than staying on the now-cheaper previous generation and pocketing the savings; tokens consumed per query climb as agents take on more complex, multistep work involving tool calls, error correction, and larger context windows; and total usage expands as teams discover new workflows to automate once they've seen agents work on the first one. Bain's own data shows the average cost per token fell by half between December 2024 and December 2025, while tokens consumed over the same period grew 4.5 times — a net effect Bain summarizes as "the models get cheaper, the usage gets heavier, the bill stays stubbornly high." Against that backdrop, AT&T's experience stands out as the clearest proof that architecture, not raw model choice, is the biggest lever available: by reorchestrating so that large "super agents" route individual tasks to smaller, domain-specific worker models rather than routing everything through a frontier model, the company reported a 90% cost reduction and 3x throughput improvement while processing 8 billion tokens a day — a single architectural decision that moved the underlying economics by an order of magnitude. Bain's five concrete recommended moves are to create a dedicated, protected AI compute budget rather than funding tokens out of existing line items; to actively "portfolio" model spending, reserving frontier models for genuinely high-stakes work and routing volume work to last-generation or open-weight models (Bain observes 3-5x cost differences between companies that do this and those that don't); to instrument token economics at the level of cost-per-task and cost-per-outcome, since almost no company currently knows this number and it is "painful to do early, impossible to retrofit"; to plan explicitly for a dual-cost transition period in which both legacy headcount and a scaling token bill are being paid simultaneously; and to test on-premise inference for the highest-volume workflows, since the true capex/opex tradeoff against API pricing remains the least-evaluated lever in most organizations' entire cost equation. Bain's concluding third installment shifts from economics to organizational mobilization, opening with five non-negotiable conditions without which any transition stalls entirely: the top team must feel the opportunity personally rather than merely fund it; line leaders, not the center, must own productivity targets and demonstrate new ways of working to their own teams; adoption must be measured loudly and specifically even when the metric is imperfect, since visible peer role models move a team of 50 faster than any mandate; governance, security review, and vendor-approval councils built for an annual cadence must be rewired for weekly speed to match the technology's pace; and organizations must identify and explicitly protect the small number of genuinely willing pioneers who can connect target-setting to workflow redesign to technology, shielding them from legacy approval chains and organizational "antibodies." Against that foundation, Bain lays out four sequential mobilization stages, each unlocking a step-change in value: Stage 1, "the light switch," describes the moment roughly 5-10% of an organization has a personal "I've met my match" experience with frontier AI on real work, unlocking up to 5% blended productivity as that group pulls up the organizational average — the practical move is creating that moment deliberately (concrete challenges, dedicated experimentation time) rather than waiting for it to happen organically. Stage 2, "raise the floor," proves value through structured pilots (4-12 weeks, real teams, real metrics) before scaling the same team structures to work faster without altering team sizes or reporting lines, unlocking productivity gains as high as 25% — for a 10,000-person organization at $150,000 fully loaded cost per employee, this represents roughly $300 million in freed annual capacity, some of which funds the token spend itself. Stage 3, "raise the ceiling," is where Bain's cost-management and mobilization arguments converge most directly with McKinsey's and EY's operating-model arguments above: one senior leader with a meaningful team (several hundred people) receives a six-month mandate, direct CEO/COO access to remove blockers, and genuine authority to restructure comp, roles, and team composition as if running a startup inside the company, redesigning workflows into pods of one to three people instead of teams of fifteen to a hundred — unlocking two-to-threefold productivity gains where it lands, with the broader organizational goal being not to reach Stage 3 once but to build the durable "muscle" to re-reach it every time the technology frontier moves again. Bain's FinOps-for-AI framework extends the FinOps Foundation's established four cloud-cost domains — visibility, benefits, right-sizing, and operating model — specifically to AI spending, starting from the finding that only 13% of tech executives believe they currently have adequate transparency to make sound optimization decisions. Bain's research on cost-reduction programs generally is sobering: most fail to hit their targets, roughly half miss by 50% or more, and captured savings typically re-erode within a year or two, for five recurring structural reasons — poor cost visibility (especially into shadow IT spend sitting outside the CIO's formal purview), wrong trade-offs (blanket cuts that damage the AI, data, and product-engineering capabilities that actually drive growth), governance mismatched to AI's pace (AI capability evolves in weeks while enterprise budgeting runs on annual cycles), nonlinear AI economics (falling unit token prices offset by rising usage, so cost per task stays flat), and the absence of ongoing discipline to prevent the familiar cost-creep cycle from simply repeating. Bain projects that in a typical $10 billion consumer products company, technology run spend will grow from roughly $250 million today to $550 million by 2035 without active management, and to about $450 million even under well-managed discipline — a roughly 75% increase either way — making the point that the goal of AI-era FinOps isn't holding spending flat but directing an inevitably larger budget toward the initiatives creating the most value. Bain's five CEO-level actions are treating technology cost as a capital-allocation decision overseen with the same board-level rigor as any major investment; establishing enterprise-wide cost transparency (including shadow IT) before setting any reduction target; protecting investment where genuine differentiation exists while mandating efficiency where only commodity delivery is needed; designing a self-funding AI investment engine that systematically reinvests captured savings into higher-value AI opportunities rather than simply banking them; and building durable capability (real-time visibility tools, agile funding models, ongoing accountability) rather than a one-off cost-reduction program that inevitably rebounds. <span class="src">FinOps for AI From Managing Costs to Maximizing Value</span>

The Financial Times documents the rationing pattern the vault's existing sources describe spreading to still more named companies. Uber capped individual employees at $1,500 in monthly token spending after exhausting its entire 2026 AI budget by April, with its president and COO acknowledging "it's very hard to draw a line between one of those stats and 'OK now we're actually producing like 25 per cent more useful consumer features.'" Walmart capped tokens on its internal "Code Puppy" AI coding platform after usage "really skyrocketed," per its global CTO, while Amazon warned staff against using "AI just for the sake of using AI" after engineers began deploying agents purely to climb internal usage leaderboards, and Meta took comparable steps in April. The clearest illustration of the underlying shock comes from software company Workato, whose 1,300 employees adopted AI agents so quickly that when Anthropic switched the company from flat-fee to token-based pricing in May, "our spend went up 7x the first day," in the words of its CIO — "\[large language model\] companies have been subsidizing all of our usage and now no longer... we created a monster." Deloitte's global generative AI leader frames the moment as one where consumers and businesses "have been taught that AI is cheap or free and that is definitely not the case," while both AI labs (steering users toward cheaper models when frontier capability isn't needed) and enterprise customers (routing tools, usage caps, open-source/local-model adoption) are converging on the same set of countermeasures documented across this concept's other sources. <span class="src">‘We created a monster’ companies rein in AI usage as costs strain budgets</span>

<span class="src">Managing Token Spending without Choking Off Opportunity</span> (Bain, September 2026) is a governance counterpoint to the rationing described in the news sources. It recommends business owners manage most workflows, a small central team govern only the risky tail with controls built into platforms, high soft limits with visibility into unusual consumption in place of low caps, and reserving expensive reasoning for the steps that need it. <span class="src">Managing Token Spending without Choking Off Opportunity</span>

<span class="src">How AI Is Reshaping the Economics of Technology</span> (BCG, October 2026) models effects across the entire budget across nine AI value areas (seven inside technology functions, two in the business). Using an industrial goods company (2.6% IT cost-to-income ratio) and a media company (7.7%), the model shows the same logic producing opposite results: technology-function productivity can fund enterprise AI demand, and in the media case lowers the ratio to as little as 6.1%, while in industrial goods balanced high adoption only holds the ratio at 2.6% and business-led adoption without technology-function gains lifts it to 2.8%. BCG tells CIOs to create one view of technology and AI spend including shadow IT, apply AI in technology first to help fund enterprise adoption, and own the economics of a "synthetic" P&L of people and agents, optimizing cost per successful outcome since a more capable model that finishes on the first attempt can cost less than a cheaper one that retries. <span class="src">How AI Is Reshaping the Economics of Technology</span>

## Open Questions

- None of the six sources quantifies how governance burden or organizational-change costs (EY's categories 4-5) compare in magnitude to token costs directly — EY names them as significant but the vault has no source with hard figures for them yet.
- It's unclear from these sources whether on-premise/open-weight inference (Bain's "wildcard" lever) is already being adopted at scale anywhere, or remains purely theoretical for most enterprises as of these articles' publication dates (May-June 2026).
- The company examples in the Bain piece are anonymous, and the claim that underuse outweighs overspend conflicts with the rationing reported in the WSJ and FT sources cited in this concept.
- BCG reports average token cost rising from under $5 to $20 per million, while Bain's data in this concept show per-token cost falling sharply; the sources probably measure different model mixes, and neither reconciles the discrepancy.
- BCG's budget scenarios rest on two illustrative companies and assumed cost and savings profiles, not observed outcomes.
