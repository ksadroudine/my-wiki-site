## Summary

Peter Fader's customer-centricity framework argues that firms should organize around customer lifetime value rather than around products. It uses a customer-by-time data cube, the Five Lenses audit and lifetime value methods to identify and invest disproportionately in the most valuable customers, and a relationship-strength segmentation of acquaintances, friends and partners offers a practical alternative route to the same prioritization.

## Concept

Customer centricity, as developed across three related books by Peter Fader and coauthors, is the strategic position that a firm should organize itself around maximizing the lifetime value of its customer base rather than around maximizing sales of its existing products. This is explicitly framed as a contrast with product-centricity, the more common default in which a company builds a product or product line and then tries to sell it to as many customers as possible, treating all customers roughly the same regardless of how differently valuable they actually are to the firm over time. The proposed replacement north-star metric is customer equity: the sum of the projected lifetime values of a company's entire current and future customer base, which reframes strategic questions (which customers to acquire, how much to spend acquiring them, which products to build next) around their effect on that aggregate number rather than around next quarter's unit sales. <span class="src">Customer Centricity</span>

The Customer-Base Audit supplies the analytical machinery to make customer centricity operational. It organizes a company's raw transaction history as a data cube indexed by customer and by time, which lets an analyst examine cohort-level patterns — how a group of customers acquired in a given period behaves over its subsequent lifetime — rather than only aggregate averages that can mask sharply different subpopulations. This structure underlies the Five Lenses framework, a five-question audit sequence: how many customers does the firm actually have and is that number growing or shrinking; how much is the customer base worth in aggregate; how much of that value has already been captured versus how much remains to be captured in the future; how does value differ across identifiable customer segments; and how does the firm's customer value compare against competitors and the broader market. Run together, the Five Lenses are intended to surface exactly where a firm's growth or decline is actually coming from — new customer acquisition, existing customer retention, or increased spend per existing customer — a distinction that aggregate revenue figures alone tend to obscure. <span class="src">The Customer-Base Audit</span>

Customer lifetime value (CLV) is the specific metric that operationalizes customer centricity at the level of an individual customer or segment: the projected net profit a firm expects to earn from a customer across the entire span of that customer's relationship with the firm, used to decide how much can profitably be spent to acquire a similar customer, how much retention investment is justified, and even which product features are worth building. The Customer Centricity Playbook catalogs five recurring mistakes that undermine CLV-based decision-making in practice: treating all customers as if they carried equal value (rather than recognizing the typically heavy-tailed distribution of customer value); systematically over-investing in acquisition relative to retention, despite retention improvements often compounding into larger CLV gains; ignoring how small changes in retention rate compound nonlinearly over a customer's full lifetime; computing CLV only in aggregate rather than by cohort or segment, which hides which specific customer types are actually driving value; and using CLV estimates over too short a time horizon, which understates the true value of long-tenured, high-loyalty customers. <span class="src">The Customer Centricity Playbook</span>

Taken together, the three books converge on four tenets that distinguish a genuinely customer-centric organization from one that merely talks about customer experience: explicitly recognizing that customers differ enormously in the value they generate for the firm; adopting CLV (rather than revenue, market share, or satisfaction scores alone) as the primary forward-looking performance metric; deliberately reallocating marketing, product, and service resources toward the highest-value existing customers and toward prospects who resemble them; and restructuring the organization itself — not just its marketing messaging — around customer segments rather than around product lines, since a product-centric org chart structurally works against customer-centric decision-making even when individual campaigns are customer-focused. <span class="src">Customer Centricity</span>

<span class="src">What Leaders Still Get Wrong About Customer Portfolio Management</span>, drawn from Fred Selnes and Michael D. Johnson's book Customer Portfolio Management, supplies a practical, relationship-strength-based alternative to Fader's CLV cohort methodology for identifying which customers deserve disproportionate investment. Rather than the traditional needs-based market segmentation common in marketing (which is static and assumes a customer belongs permanently to one segment), CPM segments customers dynamically by the strength of their relationship with a brand: "acquaintances," who provide scale economies and a pipeline of future loyal customers; "friends"; and "partners," who generate the greatest margins and future cash flows. The framework's central metaphor is a choice between two portfolios — a smaller, watertight bucket of only loyal, profitable customers, or a larger, "leaky" bucket spanning weaker and stronger relationships — with the research finding it is typically more profitable in the long run to pursue the larger, leaky bucket rather than narrow the customer base to only the strongest relationships. A CPM analysis for a European building-materials retailer illustrates the same underlying insight as Fader's heavy-tailed CLV distribution from a different angle: partners (20% of customers) generated 45% of total portfolio cash-flow value, more than six times the value of a low-share-of-wallet acquaintance, and the analysis projected that converting existing acquaintances and friends into partners would increase total portfolio value by 122%. The retailer's most actionable finding, however, was store-level: despite identical product assortment, layout, and marketing programs across 29 stores, relationship strength varied from 78% friends/partners at the best-performing store to just 13% at the weakest, pointing management toward local service-culture gaps and store-manager best-practice transfer as the real growth lever, rather than centralized marketing spend alone. <span class="src">What Leaders Still Get Wrong About Customer Portfolio Management</span>

## Open Questions

- None of the three original sources in this vault provide the specific mathematical formula Fader uses to compute CLV (e.g. discount rate assumptions, churn modeling approach); only the conceptual framework is captured here.
- The CPM source doesn't reconcile its relationship-strength segmentation (acquaintances/friends/partners) with Fader's CLV methodology into a single unified measurement approach — it's left open whether a company should run both frameworks in parallel or pick one, and how their outputs would be expected to correlate in practice.
