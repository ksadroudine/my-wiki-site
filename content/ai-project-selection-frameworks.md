## Summary

Three complementary frameworks for choosing which AI projects to pursue and how broadly to deploy them — a risk/demand 2x2 matrix for picking generative AI use cases, five traits of a strong first AI pilot, and the "deep and narrow" case against running dozens of shallow AI pilots at once.

## Concept

Three frameworks, spanning different points in an organization's AI journey, address a shared underlying problem: how to decide which AI initiatives actually deserve investment, rather than chasing every plausible use case with equal enthusiasm. The earliest-stage framework addresses the initial triage question for generative AI specifically — where should a company even start looking? — with a deliberately simple 2x2 matrix that plots risk (how likely and how damaging would fabricated or inaccurate output actually be) against demand (is there a real, sustained need for this kind of output beyond the current hype cycle). The framework's core insight is that these two dimensions should be considered jointly rather than separately, since risk and demand both vary meaningfully across industries and functions: the high-demand, low-risk quadrant — marketing copy, internal learning content, text review and editing, brainstorming — is where investment should concentrate first, since the consequences of an error are limited (a human typically reviews the output before it reaches a customer, or the content isn't fact-heavy to begin with) while genuine demand already exists. Marketing illustrates this concretely: it requires constant idea generation, audience-tailored messaging, and text-rich content, is rarely fact-dense in ways that create real risk, and already has abundant example content to prime a model with — which is exactly why several gen-AI marketing-copy startups reached unicorn status early in the technology's commercial life. The framework's caution is important, though: even in a nominally low-risk quadrant like corporate learning, generative AI remains vulnerable to bias and factual error just as humans are, so any output should be treated as a first draft to refine through better prompting and genuine human review — not distributed at face value simply because the use case scored as low-risk on the matrix. <span class="src">A Framework for Picking the Right Generative AI Project</span>

Once an organization has identified a plausible starting point, Andrew Ng's framework addresses a narrower and more tactical question: what makes a strong first AI pilot specifically, given that a pilot's purpose is only partly to create value — its more important job is convincing skeptical internal stakeholders to invest further in AI capability at all. Five traits distinguish a well-chosen pilot. It should deliver a quick win, ideally achievable within six to twelve months, and Ng recommends running two or three parallel pilots rather than betting everything on one, since this materially raises the odds that at least one produces a clear success story. It should be sized correctly — neither so trivial that success wouldn't convince anyone the technology matters, nor so large and unwieldy that it risks stalling before it can prove anything. It should be specific to the organization's own industry rather than a generic capability, since a horizontal use case (Ng's example: a medical-device company building an AI resume-screening tool) is likely to be outbuilt and undercut by a larger platform player serving many industries at once, and won't convince internal stakeholders that AI is worth investing in for their specific business either. It should be accelerated with credible external partners if the organization's own AI team is still being built, since waiting to build full in-house capability before executing risks falling too far behind the technology's own pace of advancement. And it must demonstrably create value through one of three recognizable channels: reducing costs (automation opportunities exist in nearly every industry), increasing revenue (through recommendation and prediction systems), or enabling an entirely new line of business that wasn't previously possible. Ng's own experience leading Google Brain illustrates the logic directly: rather than tackling web search or advertising (Google's most strategically central but politically fraught domains), his team's first internal customer was speech recognition, where a clear win built enough credibility to bring in Google Maps as a second project — each success compounding the next until Google Brain became central to Google's broader AI transformation. Practically, Ng recommends treating AI as automation of discrete tasks rather than entire jobs (a radiologist's job includes reading X-rays, operating equipment, consulting colleagues, and surgical planning — automating just the reading task is far more tractable than automating "radiologist" wholesale), appointing a cross-functional leader who bridges AI expertise and domain knowledge, keeping the team small (5 to 15 people is typical for a genuine pilot), conducting both business-value and technical diligence before committing resources, and deliberately publicizing milestones internally (talks, awards, even external PR) so the pilot's success becomes visible enough to build company-wide momentum, with credit shared generously with the business team specifically to encourage other business units to follow suit. <span class="src">How to Choose Your First AI Project</span>

The third framework addresses a later-stage failure mode: what happens once an organization has moved past initial pilots and is deciding how broadly to deploy gen AI across the business. The consumer packaged goods company Reckitt illustrates the choice concretely — facing a long list of plausible, individually ROI-positive use cases spanning presentations, customer support, and procurement, its executives recognized that pursuing all of them would deliver only marginal, disconnected efficiency gains rather than genuine transformation, so they instead concentrated entirely on one domain, marketing, where gen AI could be applied across interconnected tasks (insight generation, content creation, product development) that reinforce each other — real-time consumer insight feeding better product innovation, which in turn strengthens loyalty and advocacy. This "deep and narrow" approach produced measurable results within under two years: product-concept generation 60 percent faster, and brand/marketing communication processes 30 percent or more efficient. The framework names the alternative — "shallow and broad," running many disconnected, individually-justified use cases across departments — as not wrong exactly (it does generate some genuine time and cost savings) but structurally limited: when only 5 to 10 percent of tasks in any given function use gen AI, executives have little motivation to fundamentally rethink how that function operates, initiative fatigue sets in from too many disconnected pilots, and use cases that provide little real competitive advantage are easily copied by competitors, breeding employee skepticism over time. A 2024 BCG survey of 1,000 CXOs found only 4 percent of companies actually pursue the harder deep-and-narrow path, but those that do achieve roughly double the ROI of shallow-and-broad deployers over time — a gap the authors expect to widen further as AI capability continues advancing, since deep and narrow compounds structural, hard-to-copy advantage while shallow and broad mostly just captures easily-replicated efficiency. The reason deep and narrow is harder, not merely different, is that it demands wholesale change management rather than incremental technology adoption: at Reckitt, change management represented roughly 70 percent of the actual challenge, versus 20 percent for data readiness and just 10 percent for the AI technology itself. The framework offers a four-step process for deciding where to go deep. First, place candidate applications on a spectrum from office productivity (routine efficiency gains — summarizing meetings, drafting presentations — unlikely on their own to create competitive advantage) through domain reinvention (end-to-end reimagination of a function or process, the area of opportunity for almost any firm) to entirely new business models (exciting, but harder for companies whose core product doesn't lend itself easily to AI infusion, like toothpaste). Second, target domains where deep AI deployment either protects or extends an existing competitive strength, or creates a new one that's genuinely hard to copy: L'Oréal's Beauty Genius chatbot, which conducts an AI-driven skin diagnosis and personalized product recommendation, works specifically because it draws on the company's decade of augmented-reality investment and proprietary skin-biology research — advantages a copycat chatbot simply can't replicate regardless of how good its underlying model is — while IKEA used gen AI to amplify (not replace) its existing design-service strength, letting professional designers complete projects in roughly 10 minutes that previously required paid consultants working over several days, leveraging IKEA's uniquely large community of interior designers and correspondingly large volume of high-quality training data. Third, prioritize efficiency gains before growth gains within the chosen domain, since cost reduction is typically achievable faster than top-line growth and helps build the executive confidence needed to greenlight the harder, slower-to-materialize growth bets that follow — exactly the sequence Reckitt followed, demonstrating quick marketing-efficiency wins (media campaign analysis compressed from days to hours) before its leadership authorized the longer-horizon growth-oriented gen AI work. Fourth, systematically map the target domain's actual work rather than picking use cases by impression: Reckitt catalogued all 300 distinct tasks its 2,000-plus global marketers performed, through both surveys and one-on-one interviews, then plotted each task by degree of possible automation and opportunity size to identify roughly 100 tasks genuinely suited to gen AI — a rigorous inventory process that also surfaced the reality that marketers' entire role, not just isolated tasks, would need reconsidering as gen AI absorbed routine work and freed people for more demanding responsibilities previously held by their managers. The organizing question throughout, whichever domain a company chooses, is whether a top competitor could replicate the resulting advantage using gen AI too — since the competitor doesn't need to match a company's underlying strength exactly, only to offer a "good enough," cheaper, simpler alternative to cause real damage, which is precisely the competitive logic deep-and-narrow deployment is designed to defend against. <span class="src">Stop Running So Many AI Pilots</span>

## Open Questions

- None of the three sources addresses how an organization should reconcile Andrew Ng's "run two or three parallel pilots" advice with the deep-and-narrow finding that concentrated focus produces double the ROI — whether parallel pilots are appropriate only at the earliest exploratory stage before deep-and-narrow commitment, or represent a genuine tension between the frameworks.
