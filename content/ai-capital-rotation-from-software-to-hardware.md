## Summary

AI has reversed a two-decade pattern in which software outgrew hardware, with semiconductor and hardware market value rising far faster and venture capital moving toward capital-intensive deep tech. Bain and the Financial Times link the shift to AI compute demand and to doubts about durable software moats.

## Highlights

- Bain reports hardware and semiconductor stocks compounding at 24% a year from 2020 to 2026 against 6% for software, with hardware market capitalization of about $33.7 trillion versus $12.8 trillion for hyperscalers, $5 trillion for software, $2.9 trillion for AI labs and $1 trillion for tech services. <span class="src">Hardware Strikes Back in the AI Era</span>
- High-bandwidth memory revenue rose from $4 billion in 2023 to an estimated $77 billion in 2026 and a forecast $106 billion in 2027, with SK Hynix and Micron posting gross margins of 75-85% as codevelopment with logic dies ends easy supplier swapping. <span class="src">Hardware Strikes Back in the AI Era</span>
- Custom silicon is the fastest-growing data center compute segment (ASIC shipments up 96% a year and revenue up 84% from 2021 to 2025) because homogeneous training, inference and agentic workloads now run at volumes that amortize custom designs and merchant GPU margins reach 75%. <span class="src">Hardware Strikes Back in the AI Era</span>
- Global deep-tech investment excluding AI exceeded $150 billion from the start of 2024 to September 2026, more than the $133 billion of the entire decade to 2019, though it has not yet passed 2021's peak, which was driven by battery and electric vehicle deals that largely soured. <span class="src">Moonshot capitalism AI rewrites the venture capital playbook</span>
- Investors describe a shift away from software because AI model companies could swallow much of the application market, with General Catalyst's Hemant Taneja arguing that the terminal value once counted on "no longer holds" and that founders must build far bigger companies. <span class="src">Moonshot capitalism AI rewrites the venture capital playbook</span>

## Concept

Bain describes an infrastructure flywheel in which hyperscaler capital spending, venture-backed neo-cloud buildout and frontier-model development create step-change compute demand, producing new pockets of value in bottleneck technologies. Memory has become a strategic dependency with unusually stable margins, concentrating new capacity on high-bandwidth memory and worsening shortages (and prices) for phones and PCs. Advanced packaging, which now offsets slowing Moore's Law gains, saw industry gross profit grow from $6.1 billion in 2018 to $21.5 billion in 2025, with TSMC and memory vendors capturing much of it. Custom silicon, made economical by chiplets, contract design services and foundry capacity, is spreading value beyond Nvidia, and Bain expects ASIC unit volumes to exceed Nvidia GPUs even if Nvidia keeps more revenue. <span class="src">Hardware Strikes Back in the AI Era</span>

Bain draws strategic implications. Silicon architecture is a C-suite build-buy-partner decision, verticalization is likely (Anthropic buys chips from both Amazon and Google, and customers such as automakers bypass Tier 1 integrators to work directly with chip vendors), procurement becomes strategy through long-term agreements and multi-geography sourcing, and governments increasingly treat leading-edge silicon as strategic infrastructure. TSMC plans nine phases of new fabs in 2026 and has committed $260 billion to Arizona, yet says it cannot meet demand, and Gulf conflicts show that capital-intensive projects face military and transport-choke-point risk. <span class="src">Hardware Strikes Back in the AI Era</span>

The Financial Times reports the same rotation in venture capital. The "SaaSpocalypse" and the returns on early SpaceX investments (Founders Fund's roughly $600 million stake reportedly worth over $50 billion at the IPO, per PitchBook estimates) are pushing investors toward fusion, space and brain-computer interface companies they once avoided. AI simulation lowers the up-front cost of experimenting in physical domains, and AI infrastructure creates customers for ideas such as novel data center power or orbital compute. Investors acknowledge there is no spreadsheet benchmark for underwriting such bets, that commercialisation remains as hard as the technical breakthrough, and that even Alphabet's X lab, whose moonshots produced Waymo (valued at $126 billion) and the shuttered Loon, now spends more time on the business case. <span class="src">Moonshot capitalism AI rewrites the venture capital playbook</span>

## Open Questions

- Bain's "structural, not cyclical" claim is a forecast, and the sources provide no analysis of how hardware valuations would respond if AI demand or financing slowed.
- The FT piece is journalistic, relying on investor statements and PitchBook estimates, and does not quantify returns from the moonshot category beyond SpaceX.
- It is unclear how much of the $33.7 trillion hardware market value reflects Nvidia alone, which the sources do not separate.
