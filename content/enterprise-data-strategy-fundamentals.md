## Summary

Turning enterprise data into value depends on three foundational disciplines working together — fixing data quality at its source rather than endlessly cleaning up downstream errors, deliberately choosing between direct and indirect data monetization rather than defaulting to whichever seems easiest, and treating data strategy as a genuinely cross-functional effort rather than a departmental silo — with marketing serving as a case study in how easily any one function can undermine all three by pursuing "more data" in isolation, and Caterpillar's CEO-led Helios platform showing what genuine cross-functional ownership looks like in practice. A companion "right data" framework applies the same source-level quality discipline specifically to AI model training data, with six due-diligence questions managers should ask before trusting an AI project to succeed.

## Concept

MIT Sloan Management Review's research on data quality argues most organizations get permanently stuck in one of two dysfunctional modes rather than reaching the genuinely effective third one. In the first, unmanaged mode, data flows through a company as a "daisy chain": each department uses data to do its own job, generating new data in the process that passes to the next group in line, with people generally seeing themselves narrowly through their functional lens (salesperson, vendor manager, market researcher) rather than as participants in a shared data ecosystem. When someone spots an error, correcting it individually is natural but difficult and time-consuming, and plenty of quality issues go undetected entirely, propagating further damage downstream as they compound. Eventually, someone recognizes the cumulative business impact of this constant error stream, and the organization moves to the second mode, organized cleanup — a centralized data team implements tooling to find and fix errors faster and more cheaply. This genuinely beats pure chaos, but the gains tend to be modest: finding errors is comparatively easy, but fixing them correctly requires business context that a centralized cleanup team often lacks, trapping the organization in an endless cycle of reactive repair rather than actual improvement. The real breakthrough, per the research, comes in the third mode, proactive prevention, which requires a genuine mindset shift: in organizations that have traditionally maintained separate groups of data creators and data customers, people must come to see themselves as both simultaneously, sharing responsibility and actively collaborating — data customers clarifying and communicating their actual requirements to data creators, both sides jointly measuring data against those requirements, creators running improvement projects and implementing controls that stop errors at their source rather than downstream, and both sides continuing to evolve the arrangement together as business processes and goals change over time. Making this work at scale requires a core team responsible for coordinating the overall program, training both creators and customers, maintaining shared dashboards, and keeping senior management informed — plus a category of connector roles the researchers observe recurring across organizations under titles like embedded data manager, data product manager, data ambassador, or quality champion, who sit between the core team and the working creators/customers to support the day-to-day work of Mode 3. HelloFresh's own journey through these three modes is instructive precisely because it wasn't linear or quick: the company spent longer than it should have in unmanaged mode as a fast-growing startup, then moved to organized cleanup under a central engineering team that, despite genuine effort, lacked deep enough understanding of how different teams actually used the data it was cleaning — frustration mounted especially among the international marketing analytics team (which needed clean data for customer sentiment analysis and personalized campaigns) and the International Operations Business Intelligence team (responsible for a weekly global operating report to the C-suite, and increasingly hampered by inconsistent data cascading in from HelloFresh's many geographies). It was the OPSBI team, out of sheer frustration, that broke through to proactive prevention first, developing a data quality framework built around formal "data contracts" (data quality service-level agreements) that solidified its own role as a genuine data customer for the first time, while local business intelligence teams simultaneously took on genuine data-creator responsibility rather than remaining purely customers themselves — with clear communication channels established between the two, quality improved quickly, though initially confined within OPSBI alone. A 2020 companywide survey then made the scale of the underlying problem visible at the executive level: 61% of respondents reported unreliable data, 53% reported outright incorrect data, and nearly 84% acknowledged relying on manual workarounds to cope with these issues, findings broadly consistent with industry-wide cost estimates. That survey triggered CEO-level intervention, and HelloFresh built a companywide data quality working group to formalize creator/customer roles and standard quality processes, launched a data literacy program to build the necessary culture, transitioned to a data mesh architecture that formalized data creators' quality responsibilities, and hired its first vice president of data to drive guidance, transparent cross-team reporting, and a culture of collaboration — with embedded data product owners and data engineers subsequently added within individual business domains to sustain the improvement. The company's own account of the outcome is candid about both progress and remaining work: its self-service data platform now ingests data in minutes rather than months, and quality/governance/observability are now built into that platform directly rather than bolted on afterward — but the work of decentralizing data product teams into business domains and exploring AI-driven "self-healing data" (systems that not only observe and report on quality issues but automatically correct them) remains ongoing rather than finished. The overarching lesson MIT SMR draws is that simply implementing better tools or assigning a dedicated team to fix data problems is not sufficient on its own — the actual breakthrough required an organizational transformation centered on the creator/customer relationship itself, with accountability for data quality ultimately residing "in the business" rather than confined to a specialist data function. <span class="src">How to Get Proactive About Data Quality</span>

Where data quality addresses the foundational integrity of an organization's data, HBR's research on data monetization addresses the separate, downstream question of how to turn proprietary data directly into new revenue — a strategy gaining momentum as AI makes data easier to analyze, economic growth slows, and prominent examples of success accumulate (Amazon's advertising business built on customer-interest data brought in $56 billion last year; Walmart's newer online ad business, built on a similar model, now generates roughly $4 billion annually; much of LinkedIn's $16 billion in revenue traces directly to user data sold to recruiters; Mastercard and Visa have built entire advisory divisions selling transaction-derived insights; Reddit's stock jumped 12% on news of its 2024 data-licensing deal with OpenAI). Despite this momentum, HBR's research — drawing on in-depth case studies of more than 30 organizations and interviews with 12 senior executives leading monetization efforts across retail, media, tech, manufacturing, and marketing — finds most companies still struggle to choose the right approach and don't know where to begin, in part because many begin the process backward: building out technical infrastructure for a year or two before realizing they never clarified what products to build or who would actually buy them. The research's central finding is that the most successful monetization strategies start close to home, with existing partners, suppliers, and customers, for several converging reasons: existing partners already understand the specific industry and business context that makes a company's data valuable to them; the pre-existing relationship makes identifying good use cases and closing deals meaningfully easier; established sales and account-management relationships make it straightforward to sell a new data offering as a line-item add-on and scale it quickly; and staying within the existing ecosystem sidesteps privacy and data-custodianship risk, since proprietary data is often bound by sharing agreements that preclude sale to data brokers or other non-affiliated third parties in the first place. The alternative — selling raw, packaged data to data brokers serving hedge funds or other non-strategic buyers — can look deceptively simple (identify a buyer, agree a price, collect payment) but tends to be genuinely riskier and to create less value than deals built on existing strategic relationships, since identifying willing buyers and negotiating fair prices with parties that have no partnership history is difficult, and such arrangements expose a company's customers and suppliers to real risk (data leaks that could damage their own core business) without those parties' direct involvement or consent to the arrangement. Regardless of the specific path chosen, privacy, regulatory, reputational, and data-security risk must be managed from day one — leading firms take care to aggregate and anonymize benchmark data before sharing it, and companies pursuing any data-based offering need legal and risk management involved from the outset to assess potential problems and build mitigation plans before launch, not after a problem surfaces. None of this monetization work is possible without a genuinely solid underlying data foundation: efforts to monetize data that's poorly organized, low-quality, or incomplete reliably backfire, which is precisely why the data-quality discipline described above functions as a genuine prerequisite for successful monetization, not merely a nice-to-have. Once the foundational and relationship-selection questions are settled, companies face two further structural choices. The first is between direct monetization — charging customers or channel partners directly for data or data-based offerings, typically structured as a subscription for a defined access period, illustrated by British retailer Tesco's Dunnhumby analytics division, which grew out of the company's point-of-sale data and eventually became its own standalone business serving retailers and consumer-packaged-goods companies — and indirect monetization, which embeds data into existing offerings at no additional charge, typically favored by high-margin businesses for whom differentiation matters more than a direct data-sales line item (leading consulting firms bundling proprietary insights with their core consulting services is one pattern; Sony Interactive Entertainment giving PlayStation game creators rich, free access to gameplay, development, and marketing data is another, on the logic that better-informed creators build better games, which in turn drives increased platform selection and sales — SIE's own analytics director noted that major industry-defining innovations like Fortnite have tended to emerge first within its own ecosystem as a direct result). Indirect monetization, while lacking direct P&L visibility, can meaningfully increase the overall return on a company's data investment by expanding product offerings, improving operational efficiency, and strengthening customer retention — one technology provider in HBR's research found retention rates were highest specifically among customers using its data offerings, while a training-and-development company found providing customers with skills-demand benchmarks and personalized training suggestions directly improved satisfaction and retention, and a manufacturer that shared data scorecards on service levels and inventory availability with its suppliers reduced supply-chain bottlenecks and improved its own margins as a downstream effect. The second structural choice concerns how the data is actually packaged for sale, with three common approaches carrying progressively higher pricing potential and higher required investment: selling data raw, which is simplest for the seller but shifts the burden of extracting value onto the buyer (best suited to buyers who lack internal data/analytics capability of their own, or whose use case doesn't fit neatly with an existing channel partner, typically selling instead to data brokers, tech companies, or other sophisticated third parties); selling insight services, where the seller analyzes the data and prepares custom insights for the customer, both eliminating the customer's need to do that analytical work themselves and offering meaningfully more privacy and security protection, since sensitive raw details never need to leave the seller's hands (Mastercard Advisors' aggregated, anonymized transaction-network insights for banks and retailers exemplify this tier); and selling fully commercially-ready solutions — dashboards, workflows, machine-learning models, and storage packaged together with the underlying insights, either as standalone products or integrated directly into an existing digital product — which command the highest prices precisely because they require the least additional work from the buyer (ADP's compensation-benchmarking product, Instacart's ad-targeting platform which generated an estimated $958 million in 2024 revenue, and FordDirect's dealer-network analytics platform — which drove a 40% sales increase and 22% cost-per-lead reduction for dealers in targeted segments by combining data from dealer websites, CRM systems, and back-office systems into a unified customer-journey view — all illustrate this highest tier). <span class="src">How to Monetize Your Data</span>

Forrester's research on enterprise data strategy addresses a third dimension the other two disciplines depend on: genuine cross-functional coordination, examined specifically through the lens of B2C chief marketing officers, whose position illustrates both the temptation and the danger of pursuing data strategy within a single functional silo. CMOs currently sit in what Forrester calls a "data conundrum": four converging forces of "data deprecation" — tightening privacy laws, browser and operating-system restrictions on data collection, growing consumer adoption of privacy-protective tools and behaviors, and the ever-taller, increasingly ubiquitous "walled gardens" maintained by platforms like Amazon, Facebook, and Google — are significantly constraining marketers' access to the third-party consumer data they've historically relied on, pushing 76% of surveyed B2C marketing executives to collect more first-party data specifically in response. But Forrester is explicit that simply acquiring more data doesn't automatically translate into better targeting, insight, or measurement, and identifies this belief as a common and consequential trap for several reasons: companies are frequently already drowning in customer data yet still struggle to be genuinely data-driven, hampered by fragmented views of customer behavior, reactive rather than proactive privacy practices, and myopic customer-journey mapping — a dynamic compounded by an industry norm, per one CVS Health executive quoted, in which suggesting a brand collect less data risks being perceived as unsophisticated rather than disciplined; useful data frequently sits dormant within departmental silos, since departments tend to consider only the data they themselves own when assessing what's available to them, a mindset that produces redundant collection, limited sharing, and misinformed decisions across the wider organization (a quarter of surveyed B2C marketing decision-makers report organizational silos actively degrading the quality of their customer insights); data hoarding itself constitutes a standing enterprise risk that cheap cloud storage has made easy to underestimate, since the traditional cost incentive to minimize an organization's data footprint has effectively disappeared — illustrated starkly by T-Mobile's 2021 breach, in which the company was found to have needlessly retained sensitive information on more than 40 million prospective and former customers (most of the affected individuals weren't even current customers), resulting in a $500 million class-action settlement; and data quality itself degrades measurably over time, since stale data undermines personalization and can produce a subpar customer experience even when the underlying volume of data collected looks impressive on paper. Forrester's central prescription is that CMOs must formalize a genuine seat at their organization's enterprise-level data strategy discussions rather than pursuing a marketing-specific data agenda in isolation, since a marketing-only data strategy runs a high risk of further entrenching exactly the silos the organization needs to dismantle — this requires active collaboration with the CDO, CIO, and finance function, and typically an "orchestra" of IT, data science, risk and compliance, marketing, sales/service, and CX working together on complex cross-functional use cases (Forrester cites PepsiCo's enterprise data strategy, built over seven months in direct collaboration among data scientists, data strategists, data stewards, and data engineers, as one concrete example of this kind of coordinated effort). CMOs are, in Forrester's framing, one of the more influential stakeholders in this cross-functional effort specifically because they hold the keys to genuine customer understanding, and the research identifies four concrete responsibilities that follow from that position: surfacing and sharing business-critical consumer insights that extend well beyond marketing's own use cases (one global financial services firm uses marketing interaction data to anticipate credit card applications and proactively adjust onboarding processes, for instance); turning the broader data strategy into concrete market actions that deliver measurable business value, since marketing typically owns significant volumes of customer data (CRM, loyalty programs, campaign data) and carries substantial responsibility for actually operationalizing and scaling whatever data strategy the CDO or CTO has designed — positioning marketing as a major influence on how the strategy's ultimate ROI and success get measured; bridging the strategy to actual in-market execution, connecting high-level enterprise goals down to the people actually running marketing programs day to day, and feeding real-world market response back up into the strategy's ongoing iteration; and aligning marketing's data needs and use cases with the organization's privacy policies, a responsibility Forrester flags as genuinely under-resourced today — with only 15% of surveyed privacy decision-makers reporting that their organization's privacy team actually collaborates with marketing, despite marketing's inherently customer-facing role and direct access to customer data making this collaboration gap a real and consequential risk rather than a purely theoretical one. Forrester's practical guidance for CMOs building a better path forward centers on tying data needs to concrete customer-journey moments rather than requesting data in the abstract (being specific about the business tactic a given data request supports makes it far easier for data teams to prioritize and act on, versus vague requests that get lost in an undifferentiated backlog); genuinely assessing the risk of collecting and using customer data, including ethical and operational dimensions beyond pure security and regulatory compliance, and proactively involving legal and security teams as collaborators from the start rather than only after a conflict has already emerged; and inventorying the full range of data actually available across the enterprise, including sources well outside marketing's traditional purview (call-center interaction data, inventory, pricing, credit, fraud, and logistics data can all materially affect customer experience even though none of them originate within the marketing function itself), since a genuinely enterprise-wide data strategy depends on marketing seeing past its own departmental data holdings to understand — and help shape — the fuller picture. <span class="src">The CMO’s Guide To An Enterprise Data Strategy</span>

<span class="src">Data Transformation Is the CEO’s Business</span> (MIT SMR, May 2026) supplies a single, deeply detailed case study that makes concrete what the concept's other three sources describe at the level of research findings and general prescriptions: what actual CEO-level ownership of data transformation looks like in practice. When Caterpillar's CEO, Jim Umpleby, set out in 2017 to grow services and parts revenue through digital offerings, he discovered the company's customer data was too siloed and incomplete to support that strategy — a diagnosis this concept's other sources would recognize as a mix of unmanaged-mode data quality and departmental silos. Rather than delegating the fix to IT, Umpleby's team committed three years of runway to building an enterprise data platform (Helios), set a public $28 billion services-revenue target for 2026 to focus and measure the effort, and — the article's central structural move — made data ownership a senior-business-executive responsibility rather than a technology one: roughly a dozen vice presidents were recruited to own 14 enterprise data domains, evaluated monthly on their domains' data quality, while a newly created "data product owner" role (echoing this concept's HelloFresh case's connector roles under different titles) tracked reuse and business-value payoff for specific data products like the customer fleet list. The resulting platform consolidated eight legacy systems and roughly 200 dealer-integration interfaces, cutting data-management complexity by a factor of 30 by Caterpillar's own estimate, and services revenue grew from $14 billion in 2016 to $24 billion in 2024. Two governance structures — a Dealer Digital Council giving international dealers quarterly input into the platform roadmap, and an internal Demand Review Board ranking competing business-unit project proposals against the roadmap — gave both external and internal stakeholders a genuine voice, directly preventing the re-emergence of the kind of departmental data silos this concept's Forrester source warns are otherwise almost inevitable. Once the data foundation matured, Caterpillar layered AI directly on top of it rather than launching AI as a separate initiative: machine-learning models corrected data-entry anomalies at ingestion, vector stores made previously-unsearchable unstructured documents (equipment manuals, service records) available to AI systems, and an "AI Digital Product Council" evaluated and prioritized AI use cases using the same business-value discipline applied to the underlying data platform — direct evidence for this concept's broader claim that a genuine data foundation, not a parallel AI initiative, is what lets AI investments actually compound rather than stall on poor-quality inputs. <span class="src">Data Transformation Is the CEO’s Business</span>

<span class="src">What Managers Should Ask About AI Models and Data Sets</span> (MIT SMR, Roger W. Hoerl and Thomas C. Redman, December 2023) applies this concept's data-quality-at-the-source discipline specifically to AI model training data, addressing a gap in how AI failures actually get diagnosed. The authors' starting observation is that most data-science training focuses on the mechanics of machine learning rather than its limitations, leaving data scientists — who, per an old analytics saying, share model developers' and artists' tendency to "fall in love with their models" while data gets comparatively little attention — poorly equipped to prevent or properly diagnose AI model failures; because senior business managers ultimately decide whether and how broadly to deploy a model, closing this gap becomes a management responsibility rather than a purely technical one, even though most managers lack the technical background to spot trouble unaided. The article's central contribution is a five-element "Right Data" framework: the problem and population of interest must be clearly and specifically defined before any data work begins (echoing this concept's own emphasis on clarity of purpose before either quality or monetization work can proceed); "the right data" is a concept, not an actual dataset — the criteria a manager hopes the data will satisfy, judged against six dimensions (relevancy/completeness, comprehensiveness/adequate representation, freedom from bias, timeliness, clear definition of terms and units, and appropriate exclusions for legal, regulatory, ethical, or intellectual-property reasons); the training data actually used, which may or may not meet those criteria; the resulting trained model; and future data the model will encounter after deployment, which frequently differs from training data in ways that erode model performance over time. Managers are advised to walk model developers through a sequence of six questions spanning all three project phases — at problem definition, how and where the resulting model will actually be used, and how developers plan to acquire training data meeting the right-data criteria; during model development, what specific gaps exist between the training data actually obtained and those criteria, and how future data will be checked against the same standard; and around deployment, what controls will detect data or model drift over time, and what the top three failure modes are along with concrete mitigation plans for each. The article singles out one specific and common malpractice for direct criticism: validating a model using a "holdout" set carved from the same original training data (the standard practice on data-science competition platforms like Kaggle) is not an acceptable substitute for genuinely evaluating how the model performs against real future data, since holdout data is definitionally similar to training data in every way that matters — Amazon's facial recognition system, trained on data drawn from one geographic area but deployed far more broadly, is offered as the canonical cautionary example of exactly the "poor calibration" failure this shortcut produces. Read alongside this concept's MIT SMR data-quality source, the Right Data framework extends the same proactive-prevention logic — catching problems at the source rather than cleaning up after deployment — into the specific, high-stakes context of AI model development, where the cost of skipping that discipline is measured in embarrassing, sometimes legally consequential, post-deployment failures rather than merely degraded reporting. <span class="src">What Managers Should Ask About AI Models and Data Sets</span>

## Open Questions

- Neither the data-quality nor the data-monetization source addresses how an organization should sequence the two disciplines when both are underdeveloped simultaneously — whether reaching "proactive prevention" mode is a hard prerequisite before attempting data monetization, or whether some monetization work (e.g., selling raw data to sophisticated buyers who will clean it themselves) can proceed in parallel with an unfinished internal quality transformation.
- The CMO's Guide source is from Forrester (2023) and predates the more recent AI-driven data strategy pieces elsewhere in the vault — it's unclear how the four "data deprecation" forces it describes (privacy laws, browser restrictions, consumer privacy tool adoption, walled gardens) interact with the more recent AI-driven data-monetization opportunities described in "How to Monetize Your Data," since AI-training-data licensing deals (like Reddit's with OpenAI) represent a use case for consumer data that the CMO's Guide doesn't address at all.
- The Right Data source doesn't address how its six-question framework should be adapted for gen AI/LLM projects specifically, where "training data" often means a vast, largely uncurated internet-scale corpus rather than the bounded, purpose-collected datasets its credit-scoring and facial-recognition examples assume.
