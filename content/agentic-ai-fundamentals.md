## Summary

Agentic AI systems are autonomous, goal-directed programs that perceive, reason and act rather than only respond to prompts. The sources cover the main types of business agents, the four-step loop an agent follows to complete work, and the goals, team design and accountability practices that decide whether a deployment succeeds.

## Concept

Agentic AI is distinguished from the generative AI most people have already interacted with (a chatbot that responds to a single prompt with generated content) by three characteristics: agentic systems are built to make decisions and take action rather than only produce content; they operate toward an explicitly stated goal — maximizing sales, resolving a customer issue, optimizing a supply chain — rather than passively waiting for and responding to each new prompt; and they can independently execute complex, multi-step sequences of activity (searching databases, calling APIs, triggering downstream workflows) rather than stopping once a single response has been generated. Economics researchers define an AI agent more formally as an autonomous software system that perceives, reasons, and acts in digital environments to achieve goals on behalf of a human principal, with capabilities for tool use (APIs, web access), economic transactions (sending and receiving payment), and strategic interaction with other agents or humans. Adoption is already substantial and accelerating: a spring 2025 MIT Sloan/BCG survey found 35 percent of respondent organizations had already adopted AI agents, with another 44 percent planning near-term deployment, and major software vendors (Microsoft, Salesforce, Google, IBM) are embedding agentic capability directly into their platforms — yet even organizations at the leading edge of deployment report they don't yet fully understand how to maximize productivity and performance from agents, and the collective understanding of the societal implications of agentic AI at scale remains, in one MIT researcher's assessment, nascent if not nonexistent. <span class="src">What Is Agentic AI, and How Will It Change Work</span> <span class="src">Agentic AI, explained</span>

Businesses are deploying agents across a spectrum of ambition, organized into five recognizable types. Individual-augmentation "copilot" agents (Microsoft 365 Copilot, ChatGPT used as an assistant) boost one person's own productivity within their existing workflow, with impact dependent on that individual's own motivation to use the tool well. Workflow-automation platforms (Microsoft Copilot Studio, Salesforce's Agentforce) act as an AI-powered orchestrator and executor layered onto existing multi-step processes, where success depends heavily on implementation effort, change management, and ongoing agent management rather than the underlying model alone. Gen-AI-native domain solutions are purpose-built for a specific business function with AI placed at the core of the solution's design from the start, rather than layered onto an existing role or workflow — AI-driven customer service systems or AI-native software development pipelines are the examples given. AI-native enterprises and operating models go further still, restructuring the interaction layer, business processes, organizational structure, and even the business model itself around agentic AI in an end-to-end redesign comparable in scope to an earlier generation's digital transformations. AI virtual workers — agents functioning as employees or team members in their own right — represent the most potentially disruptive category, since they could let a company capture agentic AI's value by operating within its current organizational model rather than requiring the fuller structural transformation the AI-native-enterprise category demands. These categories aren't mutually exclusive; most organizations pursue a mix, rolling out personal copilots while automating select workflows and piloting a small number of virtual workers simultaneously. <span class="src">What is an AI agent</span>

Mechanically, an agent system typically executes work through a four-step loop: a user hands the system a task or goal; the system plans how to achieve it, decomposing the goal into subtasks that a coordinating "manager" agent assigns to specialized subagents, which draw on prior experience, learned domain expertise, and both organizational and external data to execute their piece and coordinate with each other; the system iteratively refines its output, sometimes requesting additional input from the user to improve accuracy and relevance before finalizing; and finally the system executes whatever real actions are needed to actually complete the task — booking a reservation, updating a record, triggering a downstream process — rather than stopping at a written recommendation the way a chatbot would. This is possible specifically because agent systems are built on foundation models rather than hand-coded, rule-based logic: foundation models let an agent system handle unpredictable situations its designers never explicitly anticipated (where a rule-based system would simply break), let it be directed in natural language rather than requiring formal programming (widening who inside an organization can meaningfully direct agentic workflows), and let it connect to existing software tools and platforms with dramatically less custom integration work than earlier automation approaches required. <span class="src">What is an AI agent</span>

Despite this architectural sophistication, the evidence from real deployments is that implementation — not the underlying AI model — is usually the heaviest lift. A study of an AI agent built to detect adverse events among cancer patients from clinical notes found that 80 percent of the total project effort went into unglamorous work: data engineering, stakeholder alignment, governance, and workflow integration, not prompt engineering or model fine-tuning. This finding motivates three recurring implementation imperatives. First, agents need SMART (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound) goals and sub-goals defined explicitly, along with the surrounding business context (why a goal matters, how it drives revenue) that a human worker would already understand tacitly but an agent starts out lacking entirely — without this, agentic systems can go off track in ways a rule-based automation system, constrained to a narrower scope, typically would not. Second, team composition matters for multi-agent and human-agent teams in much the same way it matters for all-human teams: research has found that people with "open" personalities perform better when paired with a conscientious, agreeable agent, while conscientious people actually perform worse when paired with an agreeable one — meaning the personality profile assigned to an agent needs to be deliberately matched to who it's working alongside, not treated as an afterthought. Third, the decision space around an agent needs to be deliberately "scaffolded" — supervision, guardrails, and well-defined limits set according to a task's criticality, the consequences of a mistake, confidence in the underlying training data, and the experience level of the humans working alongside the system — with that scaffolding progressively loosened only as a track record of reliable performance actually accumulates, rather than assumed away because the system is described as "autonomous." Underlying all of this is a governance dimension that can't be treated as a one-time setup cost: organizations need a standing governance board for accountability, delegated individual responsibility for monitoring and enforcing safety rules, robust permission-based systems given how much enterprise data and system access agents require, and — critically — shared, well-defined metrics to actually demonstrate agentic AI is achieving its intended outcomes rather than merely appearing to (the given caution: reclaiming 20 percent of someone's time from an agent does not automatically translate into a 20 percent labor-cost saving). <span class="src">Agentic AI, explained</span> <span class="src">What Is Agentic AI, and How Will It Change Work</span>
