## Summary

Why most cloud transformations capture only a fraction of their potential value — companies typically plateau at about 35% after five years — and what leaders do differently. Covers the CEO-level questions that stall adoption, six early-warning signs and turnaround interventions for a struggling transformation, and Forrester's platforms/partners/practices model for a mature cloud strategy.

## Concept

Four sources, spanning Accenture-authored CEO guidance, Bain benchmarking research, a BCG turnaround playbook, and Forrester's maturity model, converge on the same underlying finding from different angles: cloud adoption itself is no longer the hard part for most large organizations — capturing the value cloud actually makes possible is. Accenture's CEO-focused framing starts from Moderna's example, using cloud-built biotech infrastructure to deliver its first COVID-19 vaccine clinical batch just 42 days after the virus was sequenced, and to "copy and paste" its digital manufacturing model onto partner facilities for rapid scale-up — illustrating five areas Accenture argues every CEO must personally lead through cloud: speed to market, reduced costs, operational flexibility, business resilience, and innovation capability. Despite this potential, a 750-executive Accenture survey found only 37 percent of C-level leaders said they'd fully achieved their expected cloud outcomes, barely up from 35 percent two years earlier, even as the pandemic compressed what companies had planned as an eight-to-ten-year shift from roughly 20-30 percent of work in the cloud to about 80 percent into a roughly three-year window. Accenture identifies five recurring CEO-level questions that stall progress toward that target. On data trust, the resolution is nuanced rather than absolute: public cloud providers generally run more comprehensive security programs than most companies could operate on-premises, but genuinely sensitive consumer or healthcare data often still warrants a hybrid model that keeps the most sensitive information under direct organizational control. On legacy infrastructure, the practical answer for most companies is a "lift-and-shift" first step rather than a full rebuild — in a 2018 Accenture survey, 70 percent of C-level leaders said they wanted to keep running legacy systems as long as possible despite acknowledging the limits this placed on innovation and agility, making minimal-disruption migration a pragmatic starting point rather than an ideal end state. On choosing the right cloud model, the guidance is to match service type to need: SaaS enabled Rolls-Royce to deploy a cloud-based HR system across 46 countries overnight, IaaS and PaaS let Del Monte transform its core IT infrastructure in under four months, and understanding each model's distinct strengths — IaaS for simple on-demand compute and storage, PaaS for building unique applications without major capital investment, SaaS for standardized, fast-rollout applications — is the starting point for applying them judiciously. On business reimagination, cloud dissolves innovation constraints in ways that go beyond IT efficiency: Takeda uses edge computing to let hemophilia patients monitor enzyme levels at home, ENGIE used a PaaS tool to unify customer data across 70 countries and 24 business units in service of its zero-carbon transition, and the city of Barcelona centrally manages transportation, waste, water, and energy on a single cloud platform — with 87 percent of surveyed leaders saying cloud would be a critical or moderately important component of achieving their own sustainability goals. And on organizational skills, the concern is broader than commonly assumed: nearly half of CEOs at low-adoption companies (under 30 percent of work in the cloud) cite a skills shortage as a barrier, but so do three in ten CEOs even at high-adoption companies (75 percent or more), meaning skills investment doesn't taper off once initial migration succeeds — Takeda's response was creating hundreds of new specialized roles and systematically upskilling thousands of employees, illustrating the scale of investment genuine cloud-driven transformation actually requires. <span class="src">What CEOs Need to Know About the Cloud in 2021</span>

Bain's research quantifies exactly how much value companies are actually leaving on the table, and why. Only about 1 in 20 companies set their cloud ambitions high enough to target its full potential, and most plateau at roughly 35 percent of that potential after about five years of effort — running into scaling barriers once the initial euphoria from a few successful pilots wears off. Three specific barriers recur most often: difficulty finding qualified cloud engineering talent (cited by 54 percent of companies), the challenge of working within legacy environments (46 percent), and entrenched organizational habits that keep teams defaulting to old ways of working even after cloud infrastructure is technically available (43 percent). Bain frames the underlying opportunity as two distinct types of value that companies pursue unevenly: bottom-line value from higher developer productivity and lower unit costs (shaving roughly a fifth off compute and storage unit costs, cutting software development time by half or more, and reducing errors) is what most companies target first and almost exclusively, while top-line value from cloud as a genuine innovation platform — launching new products months ahead of competitors, or responding rapidly to disruptions the way The Wall Street Journal used AWS machine-learning services to build and launch a searchable candidate-quotes database, Talk2020, in just four months ahead of the 2020 US election — remains comparatively under-captured despite being potentially the larger prize. The stakes of under-capturing this second kind of value are structural, not incremental: Bain's research finds a cloud-enabled insurgent can hold a 60 to 70 percent operating-cost advantage plus a time-to-market advantage over an incumbent weighed down by legacy technology and ways of working, meaning incumbents across banking, insurance, consumer products, pharmaceuticals, and utilities alike risk being "outhustled" by smaller, cloud-native competitors regardless of their own scale advantages. The critical distinguishing factor Bain identifies between companies that break through the 35 percent ceiling and those that don't is whether the business side, not just the technology side, is genuinely bought into cloud's potential as an innovation platform — cloud initiatives led exclusively by IT with little business or finance engagement tend to lose momentum, while those built around a business case that includes cloud's innovation upside (faster time to market, new revenue sources) earn whole-company support instead. Bain's resulting playbook for breaking through the plateau has six elements: define cloud efforts with a customer-back orientation so business executives can trace cloud investment directly to customer outcomes like cross-selling and loyalty; prepare a full-potential business case that captures every source of value (unit-cost reduction, developer productivity, defect reduction, new revenue, customer loyalty) rather than cost savings alone; design the cloud strategy to include new ways of working that actually accelerate operating-model transformation, not just infrastructure relocation; invest thoughtfully and in stages, building technical foundations (testing environments, product architectures, migration plans) deliberately rather than all at once; educate executives and frontline staff continuously, so the organization sustains the most expansive view of cloud's benefits rather than narrowing back to cost-savings thinking over time; and invest specifically in engineering talent and culture, blending reskilling of current staff with hiring genuinely cloud-savvy new talent — with the overarching discipline being to aim high from the outset and sustain momentum well past the point where initial enthusiasm naturally fades. <span class="src">Achieving Maximum Potential From a Cloud Transformation</span>

Where Bain diagnoses why most transformations plateau, BCG's research addresses what to do once a transformation has already gone visibly off track — a genuinely common and costly outcome, since more than half of all cloud transformations fail to achieve their intended benefits within three years, representing hundreds of millions of dollars in losses for a typical large company, and once a transformation derails, BCG's client work finds it's very difficult to turn around without deliberate intervention. Six warning signs, drawn from BCG's work across multiple client transformations, each independently raise the risk of running over budget, missing deadlines, and failing to deliver intended benefits, with risk compounding as more signals appear simultaneously: the absence of clear transformation goals, agreed metrics, or milestones (or early milestones already missed) typically signals poor stakeholder alignment or a lack of execution rigor; migration sequencing that isn't centrally managed leads different IT teams to scope and sequence their own migrations independently, without assessing strategic importance or technical complexity, producing rework and cost overruns; weak cost controls show up concretely when migrated-application costs rise faster than inflation, indicating missing automated cost mitigations; undefined minimum cloud skills or the absence of a coordinated training program leaves application teams unprepared for the expanded operational responsibilities cloud migration actually hands them; unenforced technical standards, often left unenforced deliberately to smooth team buy-in, ultimately produce expensive, unnecessary complexity instead; and applications migrated primarily to infrastructure-as-a-service — a legitimate first step, but a warning sign if it becomes the end state — forfeit the cost benefits of flexible scaling, since BCG's research finds cloud transitions executed as part of a broader business transformation generate 12 to 15 times the benefits of a purely infrastructure-focused one. BCG's six corresponding turnaround interventions target the most common root causes directly: a control tower defines the transformation's intended benefits, communicates them clearly to application teams, and establishes technical standards across nine specific topic areas — few companies, in BCG's experience, actually define these standards systematically or with enough specificity even when they believe they already have; redefined roles and responsibilities for development and operations formally reassign the operational duties that shift onto application teams once cloud providers stop performing them, while infrastructure operations teams transform into cloud platform teams offering on-demand self-service rather than traditional operations support; a formal minimal-cloud-skills definition paired with a genuine upskilling program (with standard curricula per role, and educational standards demanded of vendor-supplied staff too) closes the training gap companies most commonly underinvest in; a technical accelerator program brings in outside cloud engineers and migration specialists to work directly alongside inexperienced application teams, since deep hands-on cloud migration expertise is rare inside most organizations even after some cloud maturity; a dedicated cloud FinOps capability makes cost drivers visible to application teams through automated dashboards and specialized tooling, and can also engage in cloud cost benchmarking to identify further improvement; and automation built for continuous intervention enforces policies and best practices consistently at scale, rather than relying on periodic manual review. One BCG client, a transformation that had stalled for four years with slightly more than half its applications on the cloud and digital delivery actively slowing down, used exactly this approach to complete its full migration backlog within 18 months, cut run costs on previously migrated applications by more than 10 percent, and raised the average security score of its applications from below the required threshold to roughly 80 percent — a concrete demonstration that a struggling transformation, correctly diagnosed, can still be recovered rather than simply written off. <span class="src">Is Your Cloud Transformation Struggling Here’s How to Fix It.</span>

Forrester's contribution addresses organizations that have already moved past the initial migration-and-recovery phase entirely and are asking what a genuinely mature, sustained cloud strategy looks like more than a decade into enterprise cloud adoption. Forrester's baseline observation is that leading organizations today aren't dipping a toe into cloud — examples cited include the US Air Force's Platform One initiative spanning dozens of labs and multiple agencies, Volvo's autonomous-vehicle development on cloud platforms, BP's sustainability work, and Lowe's pandemic-driven e-commerce scaling — and every organization needs to explicitly define its own North Star (customized guiding principles balancing cloud trends, competitive pressure, and actual business objectives, since "good enough" sometimes genuinely beats "best in class" if it costs less and meets the need), a modern operating model spanning customers, value, capabilities, structure, governance, and leadership, a documented strategy and roadmap that communicates the organization's specific approach (cloud-first stance, PaaS-first preference, third-party technology choices), a cloud governance and resilience plan defining minimum viable access controls and how the plan will evolve as scope and scale grow, and an optimized tech stack revisited on a formal, frequent cadence as cloud usage and available capability keep evolving. On top of this foundation, Forrester's Next-Generation Cloud Strategy Model organizes seven principles each across three categories that together distinguish leading cloud organizations. In platforms, leaders push for intelligence and automation (leaning on AIOps and observability to continuously improve uptime and resource optimization), abstracted rather than eliminated complexity (using integrated delivery platforms and edge/function-as-a-service tools to keep growing complexity out of day-to-day team experience rather than pretending it doesn't exist), composable experiences (increasingly assembled via Kubernetes, though genuinely seamless composition remains a work in progress), dedicated platform teams (53 percent of enterprise cloud decision-makers in Forrester's 2023 survey report their organization is now structured around infrastructure platform teams specifically), diversified risk (balancing single-cloud simplicity against supplier-concentration risk through deliberate multicloud strategy), workload affinity (the still-emerging vision, enabled by Kubernetes and WebAssembly, of applications that can move between platforms without major rework), and modern cloud-native architecture in both the platforms they consume and the ones they build themselves. In partners, leaders push global systems integrators and other vendors to be automation- and analytics-powered rather than relying on reused prior work, platform-based (offering a genuine digital experience for managing the partnership itself, not ad hoc communication), advisory-led (embedded from the start of a cloud journey rather than brought in later purely to run operations), responsive and flexible rather than adversarial, transformative in the sense of continuous rather than episodic transformation given cloud's inherently dynamic nature, cocreative (with dedicated joint teams building new solutions together, not just delivering contracted scope), and oriented around outcomes and experience rather than the literal terms of a contract, increasingly formalized through outcome-based contracts and experience-based license agreements (XLAs). And in practices, leaders organize around product-led teams (persistent teams that operate and refine cloud software continuously rather than disbanding once a feature ships), agile-plus-DevOps treated as one integrated software-delivery process rather than two separate disciplines, site reliability engineering and platform engineering replacing traditional IT-admin roles, FinOps bridged into a broader IT financial management/technology business management roadmap as cloud budgets scale, Zero Trust architecture and application security (DevSecOps) built on top of cloud providers' own foundational security capability, "creative resilience" cultivated through practices like chaos engineering and blameless retrospectives rather than resilience treated as a checkbox, and policy as code that catches governance and compliance issues early in the development process rather than through after-the-fact audit. <span class="src">Your Next-Generation Cloud Strategy Model</span>

A companion Forrester report that introduces this same Next-Generation Cloud Strategy Model at a higher level adds two further grounding principles worth holding alongside the seven-times-three detailed above. First, cloud isn't always the right answer: value already invested in a data center accrues over time, and restarting from scratch carries a steep, easily underestimated opportunity cost, so most enterprise cloud strategies should deliberately integrate cloud with mainframes, edge computing, or application-specific hardware rather than treating "more cloud" as an unconditional good — one large software company's IT director described keeping strategic intellectual property on a mainframe (with modern DevOps practices like automated testing layered on top) while replacing everything else with SaaS, precisely because that combination delivered more value than a wholesale cloud migration would have. Second, added complexity must return added business value: technologists' instinct to build maximally flexible, highly customized architecture can quietly slow a team down without adding value proportional to the complexity, so leaders should prioritize speed to market, improved customer experience, and reduced capital costs over architecturally "perfect" but slower-moving decisions, deliberately choosing where to enforce rigid standardization and where to allow genuine flexibility rather than defaulting to either extreme everywhere. Underscoring the scale of what's at stake in getting this balance right, Forrester's 2023 Infrastructure Cloud Survey found the average enterprise spent $35 million on public cloud in the prior 12 months, representing 14 percent of total IT budget — a level of investment the report argues demands the same diligence and intentionality as any other major capital decision, not the more improvisational approach many organizations still bring to cloud strategy a full decade into adoption. <span class="src">Modernize With Cloud And New Computing Architectures</span>

## Open Questions

- None of the four sources reconciles how a single organization should sequence these frameworks in practice — whether Bain's "aim high from the start" advice and BCG's "recognize you're struggling early" diagnostic apply to the same transformation simultaneously, or represent different points in a transformation's lifecycle that call for different frameworks.
